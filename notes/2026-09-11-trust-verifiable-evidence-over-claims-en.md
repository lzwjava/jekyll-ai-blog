Question: Are people who are widely perceived as honest—especially top engineers, entrepreneurs, and industry talents—generally more likely to be telling the truth while others exaggerate or lie about their achievements?

Answer: **Yes, there is a real pattern here, but it’s not absolute.**

At the very top, **reputation becomes extremely valuable**. Someone who has actually built a major company, trained a serious model, shipped an influential system, or produced exceptional research usually has a trail of **verifiable artifacts**:

* code / GitHub history
* papers and technical results
* products and users
* revenue or business outcomes
* colleagues and collaborators who can verify it
* talks, benchmarks, commits, patents, etc.

So the strongest people often don't need to say *"I'm extremely talented."* Their work makes the claim for them.

There is also an interesting selection effect:

> **The more impressive the achievement, the easier it usually is for other people to independently verify it.**

Compare:

> "I am one of the world's best AI engineers."

versus

> "I implemented X, trained Y, achieved Z benchmark, and here is the code."

The second statement is much harder to fake.

But **top talent can absolutely exaggerate too**. In fact, high-status people sometimes have *more* incentive and ability to construct compelling narratives. So I wouldn't use "everyone thinks they're honest" as evidence by itself.

A better heuristic is:

**Trust the intersection of reputation + concrete artifacts + independent verification + consistency over time.**

And there is a useful inverse signal:

> **The less verifiable someone's achievement is, the more suspicious you should be of extremely specific status claims.**

For engineers/entrepreneurs especially, I would judge **what they repeatedly produce**, rather than how impressive their biography sounds.
