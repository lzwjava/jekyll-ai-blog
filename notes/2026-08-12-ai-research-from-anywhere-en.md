Question: Can frontier AI research be done outside Silicon Valley by leveraging the internet and open communities?

Answer:

Yes. A major change in the AI era is that **knowledge distribution has become almost free**, and the bottleneck is shifting from "access to information" to "ability to execute continuously."

Someone like Su Jianlin is a good example of the internet-era researcher pattern:

* Read papers quickly.
* Reproduce experiments.
* Modify implementations.
* Share results.
* Discuss with other researchers.
* Build a public track record.

The important asset is not only the location. It is the **feedback loop**.

A traditional research loop:

```
university
    ↓
advisor
    ↓
lab
    ↓
paper
    ↓
conference
```

An internet-native AI researcher loop:

```
paper
 ↓
code
 ↓
experiment
 ↓
benchmark
 ↓
blog / GitHub / discussion
 ↓
feedback
 ↓
new experiment
```

The second loop can happen anywhere.

A person in Guangzhou, Chengdu, or even a small county can access:

* Hugging Face models and datasets
* open-source training code
* papers from arXiv
* GPU clouds
* technical communities
* GitHub discussions

The advantage of Silicon Valley or top universities is still real:

1. **Dense human network**

   Being physically close to researchers creates more random collisions:

   ```
   lunch → conversation → idea → startup → paper
   ```

2. **Better institutional resources**

   Large labs have:

   * thousands of GPUs
   * research engineers
   * proprietary datasets
   * internal knowledge

3. **Credential and career signaling**

   A PhD from a top lab is still valuable for certain paths.

But for many AI engineering/research directions, especially:

* LLM training techniques
* inference optimization
* agent systems
* evaluation
* fine-tuning
* open-source models

the distance is much smaller than before.

The key difference is:

**Watching videos and reading papers ≠ doing research.**

The people who progress fast usually have a daily loop:

```bash
read paper
    ↓
implement small version
    ↓
run experiment
    ↓
measure
    ↓
write notes
    ↓
share
    ↓
repeat
```

For example, training GPT-2 on a single GPU, reproducing nanoGPT experiments, testing LoRA/QLoRA, writing detailed notes — these activities create real understanding.

The internet has made "small independent labs" possible.

A single person today with:

* one decent GPU,
* cloud access,
* strong programming ability,
* discipline,

can reproduce experiments that required a university lab 10–15 years ago.

The remaining challenge is not geographic. It is **research taste**:

* Which problem matters?
* Which experiment gives information?
* What should be measured?
* What hypothesis is worth testing?

That is why people who do many experiments and share them publicly often become visible. They create a compounding knowledge graph around themselves.

Silicon Valley gives you **density**.

The internet gives you **reach**.

A strong individual can combine both: live anywhere, publish openly, collaborate globally, and occasionally travel to conferences/labs when high-value interactions matter.
