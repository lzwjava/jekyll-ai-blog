Question: For you specifically, is it better to learn high-end GPU repair or work for a startup/company in an AI role?

Answer: **I would make the AI role the main path, and GPU repair a serious side track—not the other way around.**

Given your background, I’d roughly allocate:

* **70–80%:** AI/LLM engineering + getting paid for it
* **20–30%:** hands-on high-end GPU repair / electronics

### Why AI should be the main bet

You already have unusually strong compounding assets here:

```text
12 yrs software engineering
        ↓
Java/backend/infrastructure
        ↓
LLM training + inference
        ↓
CUDA / GPUs / distributed systems
        ↓
agents + open source
        ↓
AI consulting / startup / AI infra job
```

Your existing experience can compound into **higher-value AI infrastructure work**.

GPU repair is different:

```text
electronics
  ↓
schematics / power rails
  ↓
VRAM / VRM diagnosis
  ↓
oscilloscope / microscope
  ↓
BGA / reballing
  ↓
board-level repair
  ↓
used GPU trading / repair business
```

It's a legitimate skill, but it doesn't naturally strengthen your AI-engineering résumé nearly as much as another year of serious work on inference, training, CUDA, distributed systems, or agents.

### But I actually like GPU repair for you

Not because it is necessarily the highest-income path.

Because it gives you something **AI work doesn't**:

**physical-world engineering.**

You can buy a broken 3090 for relatively little, put it on your bench, measure rails, probe VRAM, read the schematic, and eventually make a dead machine work again.

That creates a very different learning loop:

```text
buy broken hardware
       ↓
form hypothesis
       ↓
measure
       ↓
find fault
       ↓
repair
       ↓
test
       ↓
sell / keep / learn
```

And it fits your existing interest in running a small GPU lab.

So I wouldn't abandon it.

I'd treat it as an **optionality engine**.

### The important distinction

Don't choose:

> AI **or** GPU repair.

Choose:

> **AI as primary economic engine + GPU repair as asymmetric experimental business.**

For example:

```text
Day job / consulting
       │
       ├── AI infrastructure
       ├── LLM inference
       ├── training
       └── agents
             │
             │ cash + knowledge
             ↓
       GPU repair lab
             │
             ├── broken 3090/4090
             ├── VRAM
             ├── VRM
             ├── BGA
             └── used GPU market
```

Eventually they can even converge.

A person who understands **both**:

```text
GPU silicon
+ VRAM
+ PCIe
+ CUDA
+ Linux
+ drivers
+ distributed training
+ inference
```

has a much more unusual skill set than either a normal software engineer or a normal electronics repair technician.

### What I would do in your position

For the next **12 months**:

**Primary: get into a better AI role / AI consulting.**

Target things like:

* AI infrastructure
* inference engineering
* LLM serving
* CUDA/GPU optimization
* distributed training
* agent infrastructure
* ML platform engineering

Don't optimize for another generic Java outsourcing position.

**Secondary: build a real GPU repair bench.**

Start cheap:

```text
GT630
  ↓
Quadro / old NVIDIA cards
  ↓
3060 / 3070
  ↓
3090
  ↓
4090
```

Don't jump immediately into expensive 4090/5090 repair before you can reliably diagnose cheaper boards.

The key milestone isn't:

> "I watched 50 hours of GPU repair videos."

It's:

> **"Give me a dead GPU and I can systematically tell you why it is dead."**

That's a real skill.
