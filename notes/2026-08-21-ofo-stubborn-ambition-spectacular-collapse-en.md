**Answer:**

## 🚲 The Ofo Story: Ambition, Stubbornness, and Spectacular Collapse

---

### The Beginning — 20 Skewers of Lamb (2014)

Dai Wei was a student at Peking University's Guanghua School of Management when he and four other members of the cycling club bought some bikes and placed them at the campus gates in the spring of 2014. Students could scan a QR code on the frame, download an app to pay a small deposit, and the lock snapped open — riding for around 15 cents per hour. The idea reportedly sparked over a casual dinner of lamb skewers.

---

### The Rise — Yellow Bikes Everywhere

The Chinese bike-sharing company grew from obscurity to a valuation of around $3 billion at its highest point. Its dockless bicycles grew from Beijing campuses to become an icon of young, urban cool. Ofo's advertisements featured major Chinese pop stars showing trendy youngsters pedaling around the hippest areas of town.

---

### The Key Characters & Rejections

**① Zhu Xiaohu (GSR Ventures) — Early Backer Turned Enemy**

Dai Wei attracted the attention of Zhu Xiaohu, a savvy investor known for backing successful startups like Didi and Ele.me. Initially skeptical, Dai was hesitant to accept Zhu's 10 million RMB investment offer — but then returned to accept it.

As the price war with Mobike intensified, the relationship soured. Zhu Xiaohu, feeling the strain of the ongoing price wars, suggested a merger — an idea Mobike entertained but Dai Wei flatly rejected. This led to Zhu selling all his ofo shares, cashing out a cool $3 billion.

In 2016, Zhu had boldly claimed China's bike rental war would end within three months with ofo on top. As the market matured, he began pushing for an ofo-Mobike merger, and finally sold his ofo shares to Alibaba in January as the merger fell through.

**② Cheng Wei (Didi Chuxing) — Shareholder Turned Acquirer Turned Rejected**

Didi's founder Cheng Wei stepped in as a savior, investing in ofo's Series C round and becoming the largest shareholder. But when ofo's cash crisis deepened, Didi tried to move from investor to owner. Dai Wei held talks with Didi CEO Cheng Wei about a potential acquisition offer but told Cheng that ofo would never give up. Dai then rallied employees by comparing himself to Winston Churchill at Dunkirk — refusing to surrender.

**③ The SoftBank / Masayoshi Son Episode — A Poisoned Lifeline**

SoftBank's Masayoshi Son threw in a game-changing $1.8 billion investment. With that, everyone on the ofo team thought they had it in the bag. In a risky move, Dai Wei invested all available capital to meet SoftBank's operational benchmarks. User orders surged to 30 million. But SoftBank's conditions proved to be a trap — ofo burned through everything chasing the metrics, leaving itself financially hollow.

**The Merger Door Finally Closed by Alibaba**

At the end of 2017, when Tencent and Didi participated in merger negotiations between ofo and Mobike, Alibaba first purchased shares of ofo from Zhu Xiaohu and obtained veto rights, then issued a loan to ofo. Since then, the door of merger between Mobike and ofo was permanently closed.

---

### The Collapse

Over 12 million people requested refund of deposits online. Dai Wei wrote to employees saying ofo faced "immense cash flow pressure" — needing to process refunds, pay back suppliers, and maintain daily operations, with "every penny spent cautiously."

A court in Beijing placed Dai Wei on a credit blacklist, restricting him from staying at fancy hotels, traveling first class, or sending his children to expensive schools.

Today, Dai Wei is infamous as a defaulter owing billions, pursued by over 16 million people — his name synonymous with the unreturned 99 RMB deposit from his failed company.

---

### The Verdict

"It now appears bike-sharing is the stupidest business, but the smartest brains of China all tried to get in," said the founder of now-bankrupted bike-sharing company 3Vbike.

Dai Wei's fatal flaw was **refusing every exit** — the Zhu Xiaohu merger, the Cheng Wei acquisition, and overextending to meet SoftBank's benchmarks. Each "no" that could have saved the company instead accelerated its ruin.
