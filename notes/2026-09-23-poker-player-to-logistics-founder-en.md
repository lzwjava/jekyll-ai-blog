Question: **What’s the story of HuoLaLa / Lalamove founder Shing Chow (周胜馥)?**

Answer:

Shing Chow’s story is unusually nonlinear. It’s basically:

**math/elite student → Bain consultant → professional poker player → investor → logistics founder → global logistics platform.**

### 1. He was an extreme outlier academically

周胜馥 grew up in Hong Kong after moving there from Jieyang, Guangdong, at age 3.

In 1995, he became the **first "10-As" top student in Hong Kong's New Territories**. He later studied economics at Stanford and graduated in 2000 according to Lalamove's own biography. ([Huolala][1])

Then he joined **Bain & Company** as a consultant in 1999.

So the obvious career path was there:

> elite academics → Stanford → Bain → finance/consulting

He didn't follow it.

---

### 2. Then comes the crazy part: seven years of professional poker

Around 2002, Chow left consulting and spent roughly **seven years as a professional poker player**. Lalamove's official biography confirms the seven-year poker career. ([Huolala][1])

There are various Chinese-media accounts claiming he accumulated tens of millions of HKD through poker and subsequent investing/real estate. Those numbers are much less firmly documented than the basic fact of his professional poker career, so I'd treat the wealth figures as reported rather than established fact. ([NF News][2])

The interesting part isn't really the gambling.

It's the **decision-making training**.

Poker is basically:

$$
\text{decision} =
\text{information}
+\text{probability}
+\text{expected value}
+\text{risk management}
+\text{psychology}
$$

And that mentality seems to have carried over into his entrepreneurship.

---

### 3. At ~31, he decides poker is not what he wants to do anymore

Around 2009 he stopped playing professionally.

One account says he eventually came to see poker as a **zero-sum game** and wanted to build something that created actual value. That motivation is attributed to a former employee rather than being a primary-source statement from Chow himself, so I'd treat it as an account of his thinking rather than a verified quote. ([Eeo][3])

He spent several years looking for a business to start.

Then he noticed something extremely boring:

**Hong Kong's van/transport market sucked.**

Traditionally, people called dispatch centers ("call台") to get a van. There were lots of fragmented operators, but poor matching, scheduling and visibility. ([NF News][2])

And this was 2013.

Smartphones + GPS + fragmented supply + fragmented demand.

The business opportunity was basically:

$$
\text{offline dispatch}
\rightarrow
\text{real-time marketplace}
$$

---

### 4. 2013: EasyVan

He founded **EasyVan** in Hong Kong in 2013.

The initial product was brutally simple:

> Open app → request a van → match with nearby driver.

The company started with only **three people**. ([South China Morning Post][4])

And here's one of the best details in the story:

**they didn't have much money for marketing.**

So the team did ridiculous guerrilla marketing. Chow and several employees reportedly dressed up as women and went into the streets to promote the app; co-founder Matthew Tam even appeared in a humorous truck commercial.

That video reportedly spread widely in Hong Kong and helped EasyVan get attention. ([The Paper][5])

This is classic early-stage startup behavior:

```text
No budget
   ↓
No paid acquisition
   ↓
Need attention
   ↓
Do something people will talk about
   ↓
Organic distribution
```

---

### 5. Then he makes the biggest bet of his life

This is the part I find more interesting than the poker story.

He **sold his properties and put essentially his personal wealth into the company**. Multiple Chinese reports describe this as going "all in." ([The Paper][5])

So the poker guy eventually made an even bigger bet:

> **Poker: bet one hand at a time.**
> **Startup: bet your entire capital base on a market.**

And he was entering an extremely competitive O2O market.

At the peak, Chinese local-freight O2O had **hundreds of competitors**. ([Jiemian][6])

---

### 6. The critical insight: don't win by burning the most money

This is where the story gets particularly interesting from a founder/operator perspective.

The industry was doing:

```text
subsidy
→ acquire drivers
→ subsidize customers
→ acquire more users
→ burn more money
→ hope scale wins
```

Some competitors were reportedly subsidizing individual orders by huge amounts.

Lalamove instead increasingly focused on **operations and unit economics**, while targeting SMEs and consumers rather than trying to dominate everything immediately. It also expanded cities relatively cautiously. ([Eeo][3])

Around 2016, many competitors started collapsing.

Lalamove survived.

Then in 2017 it went from **39 cities to 108 cities** in China, followed by rapid fundraising and expansion. ([Economic Observer][7])

This is the classic marketplace curve:

$$
\text{liquidity}
\rightarrow
\text{retention}
\rightarrow
\text{unit economics}
\rightarrow
\text{density}
\rightarrow
\text{geographic expansion}
$$

Rather than:

$$
\text{funding}
\rightarrow
\text{subsidy}
\rightarrow
\text{GMV}
\rightarrow
\text{another funding round}
$$

---

### 7. And he made an unusual geographic decision

EasyVan didn't stay a Hong Kong company.

It expanded internationally extremely early.

By 2014 it was already entering Southeast Asia. The mainland Chinese business became **货拉拉**, while international markets used **Lalamove**, with separate teams/apps. ([Huolala][1])

That eventually produced this structure:

```text
                    Lalamove
                       │
        ┌──────────────┼──────────────┐
        ↓              ↓              ↓
      China        Southeast Asia   Other markets
     货拉拉
```

Today the company says it operates across **15 markets**, with China mainland covering 363 cities as of November 2025. It reports 140M monthly active drivers and 160M monthly active users — though these are company-reported figures and definitions matter. ([Huolala][1])

## The really interesting founder lesson

I wouldn't summarize Zhou's story as:

> "Smart guy becomes successful entrepreneur."

That's too shallow.

The interesting pattern is that **each stage trained a different capability**:

| Stage           | Capability                     |
| --------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Top student     | learning speed                 |
| Stanford        | analytical thinking            |
| Bain            | structured problem solving     |
| Poker           | probabilistic decisions + risk |
| Investing       | capital allocation             |
| EasyVan         | product + marketplace          |
| China expansion | operational execution          |
| Lalamove        | scaling organization           |

And there is a particularly beautiful transition:

```text
Poker
  ↓
optimize decisions under uncertainty
  ↓
Investing
  ↓
allocate capital under uncertainty
  ↓
Startup
  ↓
allocate people + capital + time under uncertainty
```

That's probably the most interesting part of Zhou Shengfu's story.

He didn't come from logistics.

He didn't spend 20 years learning trucking.

He saw a **broken coordination system** and recognized that smartphones could turn it into a marketplace.

That's very different from being a "logistics entrepreneur."

It's essentially:

$$
\boxed{
\text{fragmented supply}
+
\text{fragmented demand}
+
\text{real-time information}
\Rightarrow
\text{marketplace}
}
$$

And that pattern is still highly relevant to AI agents: **find expensive human coordination, expose the state through software, then automate matching/decision-making.**
