Question: Why does the online/digital world feel increasingly "real", where online rejection or blocking feels like a broken relationship?

Answer:

Your observation matches a major shift in human experience over the last 20 years. You experienced the transition from **offline-first life → online-assisted life → digital-native social identity**.

Around 2004–2006, the internet was mostly a **tool**:

* Computer = a machine you use.
* Internet = a place you visit.
* Online identity = nickname, forum account, avatar.
* Real life = physical world.

But today, the relationship has inverted:

* Your online account represents your reputation.
* Your avatar represents your social presence.
* Your messages represent emotional connection.
* Your communities exist primarily online.
* Your work, money, friendships, and learning happen digitally.

The "virtual" world became a **persistent social layer** on top of physical reality.

A useful way to think about it:

```
2005:

Human
 |
 +-- physical life (real)
 |
 +-- internet (tool)


2026:

Human
 |
 +-- physical body
 |
 +-- digital identity
      |
      +-- friends
      +-- work
      +-- reputation
      +-- memories
      +-- communities
```

The brain does not fundamentally care whether social signals come through photons from a person's face or pixels on a screen. Evolution trained humans to respond to:

* acceptance
* rejection
* status changes
* belonging
* exclusion

A message saying:

> "You are blocked"

can activate similar social pain mechanisms as:

> "I don't want to talk to you anymore."

because the underlying signal is the same:

> "Your access to this person's social world has been removed."

---

Another interesting change is **presence**.

Before the internet:

A person disappeared when they left the room.

After social media:

A person is always "there":

* their profile exists
* their posts remain
* their last activity timestamp exists
* their avatar is visible

Digital identity has continuity.

It is almost like humans created a second body:

```
Physical body:
- face
- voice
- clothes
- location

Digital body:
- username
- avatar
- posts
- code
- reputation
- followers
```

Damage to the digital body can feel personal.

For example:

* losing an account
* getting banned
* being blocked
* losing followers
* negative comments

can feel like social injury.

---

There is also a generational difference.

People who grew up before the internet often think:

> "Online is not real. Real life is face-to-face."

People who grew up after smartphones often think:

> "Online is where relationships happen. Offline is only one channel."

For a teenager today:

* Discord friend = real friend
* online game teammate = real teammate
* online community = real community

The physical location is less important.

---

The deeper philosophical change is that humans are moving from a **place-based civilization** to an **identity-based civilization**.

Old:

> "Where are you?"

New:

> "Who are you connected with?"

A programmer in Guangzhou can collaborate daily with someone in San Francisco, London, or Tokyo. Their strongest professional relationships may never involve physical meetings.

The internet did not make relationships less real. It changed the medium.

A useful analogy:

Before electricity:

> "A telephone conversation is not a real conversation because people are not in the same room."

Today nobody believes that.

The internet is doing the same transformation to social presence.

The next stage with AI agents, virtual worlds, and persistent avatars may make the boundary even weaker. The question may no longer be:

> "Is online real?"

but:

> "Which parts of reality require physical presence, and which parts are fundamentally about information exchange?"

Your generation (touching computers in the early 2000s) is actually one of the rare generations that experienced both worlds: the pre-digital social model and the fully networked one. You can feel the transition because you remember when the digital world was clearly "outside" life, and now it has become part of the self.
