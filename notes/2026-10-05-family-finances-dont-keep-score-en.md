Question: How should we understand the idea that within a home or family, people shouldn't be too strict or precise about benefits, money, and financial matters, unlike in business?

Answer: Mostly yes, though with an important caveat.

**Why the idea makes sense**

- **Different logic of relationship.** Business runs on exchange: I give X, you give Y, and we keep it balanced. Families run on care and mutual commitment. Parents don't invoice children, and spouses don't bill each other for cooking. Sociologists often describe this as "communal" versus "exchange" relationships.
- **Long time horizon.** Family contributions even out over decades. One person may earn more now, another may care for children or aging parents, and roles shift. Tracking every transaction misses the bigger picture.
- **Counting can signal distrust.** Itemizing every favor tends to make the other person feel like a counterparty rather than family. It can turn generosity into obligation.
- **Contributions aren't all monetary.** Housework, emotional support, and caregiving have real value but resist exact pricing.

**The caveat: "not strict" doesn't mean "unclear" or "unfair"**

- **Be relaxed about small things, clear about big ones.** Splitting a restaurant bill to the cent is unnecessary. But large matters, such as who owns the house, loans between relatives, support for parents, inheritance, and shared savings, benefit from open, explicit agreements.
- **Vagueness can hide imbalance.** When one person quietly carries most of the burden, "we're family, don't count" can become a way to avoid fairness. Resentment grows silently, and many family conflicts over money start exactly this way.
- **Transparency is different from scorekeeping.** Healthy families can talk openly about income, expenses, and expectations without charging each other for every favor.
- **Cultural context matters.** In many cultures, including Chinese families, money flows between generations as an expression of duty and love. This works well when expectations are mutual and understood, and badly when they're assumed but never discussed.

**A practical rule of thumb:** Be generous and flexible in daily life, but make major financial arrangements clear and agreed upon. Fairness over the long run matters more than exactness in each moment, and if someone feels consistently taken advantage of, it's worth discussing openly rather than dismissing with "we're family."
