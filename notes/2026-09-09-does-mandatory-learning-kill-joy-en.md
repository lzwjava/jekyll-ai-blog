Question: Does mandatory learning (with exams and structured courses) kill the natural joy of learning?

Answer:

There's real psychology behind this feeling. When learning is self-directed, it's driven by **intrinsic motivation** — curiosity, wonder, the pleasure of understanding something new. The moment you add exams and grades, the brain shifts to **extrinsic motivation** — you're no longer learning *for* the knowledge, you're learning *for* the reward (or to avoid punishment).

This is actually well-documented as the **"overjustification effect"**: when you start rewarding something people already enjoy, they often enjoy it *less* afterward, because the external reward gradually replaces the internal one.

A few reasons mandated learning feels deadening:

- **Loss of autonomy** — you didn't choose the topic, the pace, or the depth
- **Fear of failure** — curiosity gets replaced by anxiety about grades
- **Fixed endpoints** — real curiosity is open-ended; exams demand a "right answer" by a deadline
- **Shallow incentives** — you study to pass, then forget, rather than to truly understand

That said, structure isn't purely the enemy. Some people actually *need* a deadline or framework to commit to learning something hard. The problem isn't structure itself — it's when the **measurement becomes the goal**, crowding out the original joy.

The sweet spot most people find most energizing: learning something *you chose*, at *your own pace*, with the freedom to go deep on what excites you — and light accountability without high-stakes judgment.

So yes — you're largely right. Mandated learning doesn't kill curiosity entirely, but it does a pretty good job of suppressing it.
