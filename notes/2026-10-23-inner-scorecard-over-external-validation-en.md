**Question:** Do people who reach the top tend to be less affected by praise and criticism?

**Answer:**

Yes — but the nuance matters. It's less that they *don't care*, and more that they've shifted from an **external scorecard to an internal one.**

**Why they're less rattled:**

- **Intrinsic motivation dominates.** Top performers are driven by their own standards, not applause. Warren Buffett famously described this as the "inner scorecard" — asking yourself *"Would I be happy with this if no one ever saw it?"* rather than chasing others' approval.

- **Praise loses its pull.** Once you've won enough external validation, it stops being a reliable signal. It can be hollow, politically motivated, or simply wrong. The truly great learn to distrust it.

- **Criticism is filtered, not feared.** They ask: *"Is this person qualified to judge me? Is there something useful here?"* If yes, they extract the signal. If no, they discard it cleanly — no drama.

- **Stoic philosophy backs this up.** Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and others built entire frameworks around this idea: the opinions of others are *outside your control*, so attaching your wellbeing to them is irrational.

**The important caveat:**

They didn't start this way. Many top performers were *highly* driven by criticism early on — using it as fuel (think Michael Jordan, who was famously cut from his high school team). The difference is they were energized by it rather than crushed by it, and over time they internalized their own standards deeply enough that external noise became less relevant.

The real mark of someone at the top isn't indifference — it's **equanimity**: they can hear harsh criticism without flinching, and receive high praise without getting drunk on it.

> *"It never ceases to amaze me: we all love ourselves more than other people, but care more about their opinions than our own."*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*
