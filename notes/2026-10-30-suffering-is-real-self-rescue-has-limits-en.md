Question: Is the saying "众生皆苦，唯有自渡" (All beings suffer; one can only save oneself) true?

Answer:

Partially true — and worth unpacking carefully.

**The first half is solid ground.**
"众生皆苦" echoes the Buddha's First Noble Truth: *dukkha* — suffering, impermanence, dissatisfaction — is woven into existence. Not as pessimism, but as an honest diagnosis. No one escapes loss, aging, or the ache of wanting things to be other than they are.

**The second half is where it gets complicated.**
"唯有自渡" — "only you can carry yourself across" — is true in one sense: no one can do your inner work for you. A teacher can point at the moon; you still have to look. A therapist can hold space; you still have to feel. Liberation, if it comes, is walked, not handed over.

But taken too far, it tips into something colder: *don't bother with others; everyone is on their own.* That's where it parts ways with Buddhism proper. The Bodhisattva ideal — delaying one's own liberation to help all beings cross — is the direct counter-argument. Compassion isn't naive; it's the very path.

**The fuller version of the saying is more honest:**
> 众生皆苦，唯有自渡。渡人者，先渡己。
> *All beings suffer; each must save themselves. But to carry others across, you must first cross yourself.*

That sequencing matters. Self-work isn't selfishness — it's the prerequisite for genuine help, not the end of the story.

So: yes, suffering is universal, and yes, no one can do your awakening for you. But "I can only save myself" as a conclusion? That's where the phrase can become a comfortable excuse to stop caring.
