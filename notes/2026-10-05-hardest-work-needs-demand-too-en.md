Question: Is one viable way to live to find the hardest thing you're capable of doing, and make money from that?

Answer: Partly yes, but with a caveat. The logic works because difficulty creates scarcity. If something is hard for most people and you can do it well, the market tends to pay for that, since there's less competition and more value created per hour. This is roughly the idea behind "career capital" (build rare, valuable skills first) and the economic concept of comparative advantage.

Where it breaks down:

1. **Hard isn't the same as valuable.** Many things are extremely difficult and pay nothing: mastering an obscure instrument, memorizing pi to 10,000 digits. Money follows difficulty only when someone else needs the result. The better filter is hard *and* in demand.

2. **"Hardest thing you can do" can mean two things.** If it means the hardest thing relative to *your* abilities, that's a growth strategy and works well for learning. If it means hardest in absolute terms (say, theoretical physics), you may be competing with people who have bigger head starts, and being at the edge of your ability doesn't guarantee you'll be the best.

3. **Sustainability matters.** Constantly doing the hardest thing risks burnout. The people who do well long-term usually find something that is hard for others but feels comparatively natural or interesting to them. That mix of aptitude plus obsession is what lets you outwork people over decades.

4. **It's one approach, not the only one.** Others optimize for freedom, relationships, or meaning, and some of those lives involve less money and less difficulty by choice.

A more practical version of your idea: look for the intersection of (a) things that are hard for most people, (b) things you have some edge or deep interest in, and (c) things others will pay for. Push yourself toward the top of that overlap.
