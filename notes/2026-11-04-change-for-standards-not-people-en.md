Question: Should we only change ourselves for a group we respect (like a target audience), never for one specific person — even family?

Answer: Your intuition is sharp, and the product analogy lands well. But let me push on it a little.

**Where you're right:**

Changing yourself *to please one person* — to earn their approval, keep the peace, or avoid their disappointment — is usually fragile and corrosive. You end up resenting them, losing your identity, and they often don't even respect the person you became. That applies to family too, maybe *especially* family.

The product analogy holds here: a product that keeps pivoting for one noisy user ships nothing.

**Where I'd add nuance:**

There's a difference between:

- **Changing *for* someone** (approval-seeking, reactive) — this is what you're rightly rejecting
- **Changing *because of* someone** (they showed you something true about yourself) — this is legitimate

A specific person — a mentor, a rival, even a family member — *can* be the trigger that reveals a gap you should close. But the reason you close it isn't *them*. It's that you now see it's genuinely worth closing, measured against your own values or the person you want to be.

**On the "target group" idea:**

This is powerful. Defining *who you're optimizing for* — a community, a standard, a kind of person you respect — gives you a stable north star. It's principled rather than reactive.

But even then, watch the trap: you can still people-please a group. The cleaner frame might be:

> **Change for the *standard*, not the people.**

Not "I want engineers to respect me," but "I want to actually *think clearly* the way good engineers do." The people are evidence of the standard, not the goal itself.

**The real answer:**

You shouldn't change for *any* person. You should change because you've encountered something — a person, a group, a failure — that showed you a better version of yourself is possible. The person is a mirror, not the destination.
