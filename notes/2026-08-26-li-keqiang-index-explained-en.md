**Question:** How does the method of measuring China's real economy through electricity production, bank loans, and other hard data work — and who actually proposed it?

**Answer:**

This framework is known as the **"Li Keqiang Index"** (克强指数), named after China's former Premier **Li Keqiang**, not Miles Guo. According to a WikiLeaks revelation, Li Keqiang speculated in 2007 about the problems with China's official growth figures and suggested that observers instead focus on three data points: **industrial electricity usage**, **railway freight volume**, and **bank loans**.

In 2010, *The Economist* formalized this into what it called the "Li Keqiang index," based on a leaked U.S. State Department memo claiming the premier — then a provincial official — used those indicators as a better way to track China's economy than unreliable official data.

**Why those three indicators?**

China's GDP figures have long been considered suspect — even Li Keqiang himself reportedly said in 2007 that they were "man-made" and "for reference only." The three hard indicators resist easy manipulation:

- **Electricity consumption** — factories and businesses can't fake how much power they draw
- **Bank loan disbursements** — credit flow reflects real investment and business activity
- **Railway freight volume** — physical goods movement is hard to falsify

In the standard Li Keqiang Index, bank loans are weighted at 35%, rail traffic at 25%, and energy usage at 40%.

**Miles Guo's role:** Miles Guo (郭文贵), also known as Miles Kwok, is a former Chinese property tycoon and political activist who fled to the U.S. in 2014 and frequently speaks about the Chinese economy and CCP corruption — but he is not the originator of this measurement framework. If you saw him discussing electricity and bank debt as economic signals, he was likely citing or echoing Li Keqiang's index as part of his anti-CCP commentary.

**Modern extensions of this approach:**

The San Francisco Fed's China Cyclical Activity Tracker (China CAT) expands this further, combining eight non-GDP indicators: consumer sentiment, electricity production, exports, fixed asset investment, new floor space constructed, industrial production, rail freight shipments, and retail sales.
