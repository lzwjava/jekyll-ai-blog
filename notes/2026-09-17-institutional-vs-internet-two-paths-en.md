Question: **Is the traditional university/school route structurally opposite to the Internet, freedom, and startups—and does moving from a consolidated system to a diverse/global one create much more complexity, especially across Chinese vs. English?**

Answer:

Yes — but I’d sharpen the model a little.

### 1. Traditional education and the Internet optimize for different things

The traditional route is roughly:

```text
school
  ↓
university
  ↓
degree / credential
  ↓
company
  ↓
career
```

It optimizes for **standardization, coordination, and credentialing**:

* same curriculum
* same exams
* same schedule
* relatively stable institutions
* credentials that can be understood by strangers

The Internet/startup route is closer to:

```text
interest
  ↓
learn
  ↓
build
  ↓
publish
  ↓
users
  ↓
feedback
  ↓
iterate
```

It optimizes for **autonomy, experimentation, distribution, and demonstrated ability**.

So there is a real tension:

```text
Institutional route          Internet / startup route

standardized                 individualized
credential                   output
curriculum                   exploration
permission                   initiative
local                         global
slow feedback                fast feedback
risk reduction               risk taking
coordination                 self-direction
```

But they're not literally opposites. A university can give you deep knowledge, peers, research infrastructure, and social capital; the Internet can give you distribution and autonomy. The interesting part is that the Internet makes **alternative paths increasingly viable**.

---

### 2. "Some people just start" is a real phenomenon

Historically, the default assumption was:

> First acquire credentials → then acquire opportunities.

The Internet increasingly enables:

> Create something → demonstrate capability → opportunities appear.

For example:

```text
traditional:
student → degree → employer → salary

builder:
builder → product → users → revenue → reputation
```

And with AI this becomes even more extreme because the cost of producing software, content, research prototypes, and businesses is falling.

The important distinction is **not university vs. no university**.

It's:

```text
credential-driven path
        vs.
output-driven path
```

A person can attend MIT and still be extremely output-driven.
A dropout can still behave like a credential collector.

### 3. Your language point is actually a deeper systems problem

Your Chinese/English example can be generalized as:

```text
CONSOLIDATED SYSTEM
        ↓
fewer conventions
        ↓
lower coordination cost
        ↓
simpler mental model
```

versus:

```text
DIVERSE SYSTEM
        ↓
more languages
more cultures
more institutions
more norms
more markets
more legal systems
more interfaces
        ↓
higher coordination cost
        ↓
greater complexity
```

For example, consider:

```text
Domestic Chinese ecosystem

Chinese
  ↓
Chinese education
  ↓
Chinese Internet
  ↓
Chinese social norms
  ↓
Chinese payment / commerce
  ↓
Chinese institutions
```

There are fewer boundaries to cross.

Now:

```text
Global Internet

Chinese
English
Japanese
Korean
...
   +
different cultures
   +
different laws
   +
different platforms
   +
different currencies
   +
different institutions
   +
different expectations
```

The state space becomes much larger.

---

### 4. But diversity creates *complexity and optionality* simultaneously

This is the key part.

You can think of it as:

$$
\text{Complexity} \uparrow
$$

but also:

$$
\text{Possible paths} \uparrow
$$

A consolidated system gives you:

```text
fewer choices
→ easier navigation
→ less ambiguity
→ less freedom
```

A diverse system gives you:

```text
more choices
→ harder navigation
→ more ambiguity
→ more freedom
```

So freedom itself has a **coordination cost**.

That's why:

> **Freedom feels simple from the outside but complicated from the inside.**

If someone tells you:

```text
Do A → B → C → D
```

you don't have to construct the map yourself.

But if you have:

```text
A/B/C/D/E/F/G
   ↘
    many possible paths
```

you suddenly need to decide:

* what to learn
* which language to use
* which market to enter
* which people to trust
* which institutions matter
* which opportunities are real
* what to ignore

That is a much harder optimization problem.

---

### 5. The Internet makes this tradeoff especially visible

The traditional world is largely:

$$
\text{coordination through institutions}
$$

The Internet is increasingly:

$$
\text{coordination through protocols + networks + reputation}
$$

For example:

```text
Old:
University → degree → employer trusts you

Internet:
GitHub → projects → users → reputation → employer/customer trusts you
```

The second system removes an intermediary, but now **you have to construct the trust yourself**.

That's why the Internet can simultaneously feel:

> more free **and** more chaotic.

And startups are an extreme version of this.

```text
Traditional career:
institution carries some of the uncertainty

startup:
individual/company carries much more uncertainty
```

---

### 6. A useful abstraction

I'd model the whole thing as three axes:

```text
             HIGH FREEDOM
                  ↑
                  |
       Internet   |   Startup
                  |
------------------+----------------→ DIVERSITY
                  |
    Traditional   |   Global
    institutions  |   markets
                  |
                  ↓
             HIGH STANDARDIZATION
```

And language is one layer of this larger phenomenon.

Chinese vs. English isn't merely:

```text
language A vs language B
```

It's often:

```text
language
  +
culture
  +
knowledge graph
  +
social network
  +
institutions
  +
market
  +
information ecosystem
```

So becoming bilingual/global isn't simply learning another vocabulary. You're effectively gaining access to another **social + informational operating system**.

That's why the complexity increases dramatically as you move from a single consolidated environment to multiple overlapping environments.
