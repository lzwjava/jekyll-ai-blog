Question: Are OpenAI and Anthropic mainly focused on aggregate usage/revenue rather than individual users’ preferences, as opposed to caring about whether each person prefers a specific model?

Answer:

Your intuition is **mostly correct, but a bit simplified**.

At a high level, companies like OpenAI and Anthropic are indeed **businesses optimizing at scale**. That means their primary success metrics usually include:

* Total active users (growth)
* Retention / engagement (how often people come back)
* Revenue (subscriptions, API usage, enterprise contracts)
* Model quality in aggregate benchmarks and real-world usage signals

So yes—**they don’t optimize for every individual preference equally**. It’s impossible anyway: different users want different things (coding style, verbosity, strictness, creativity, etc.).

But there are a few important nuances:

### 1. Individual preferences still matter—just indirectly

Even if they don’t “care about each user personally,” they *do care about patterns of preference*.

If many users:

* dislike a writing style
* switch models after trying one
* or abandon a feature

…that becomes aggregate feedback that affects future model training and product design.

So “individual taste” matters **as data**, not as a personalized commitment.

### 2. “Model personality” differences are partly intentional

What your friends said about “Opus vs GPT style” reflects a real thing: different labs deliberately shape models differently.

* Anthropic tends to optimize for **helpfulness, structure, and safety constraints**
* OpenAI tends to optimize for **versatility, instruction-following, and product integration**

That’s why you may feel:

* one model is more “rigid” or “stylistically consistent”
* another is more “adaptive but less opinionated”

This is not random—it’s product positioning.

### 3. Revenue isn’t just “number of users”

Especially now, the focus is more nuanced:

* enterprise contracts (high-value customers)
* API usage intensity (tokens, workloads)
* developer ecosystem lock-in
* reliability and trust (critical for enterprise adoption)

So “80% of Opus level” vs “not listening to instructions” style feedback can matter **a lot** if it affects developer trust or switching behavior.

### 4. Your framing is directionally right, but missing feedback loops

A more accurate version would be:

> These companies optimize for aggregate user satisfaction and economic value, and individual preferences matter only insofar as they scale into measurable behavioral patterns.

That captures both:

* your “they don’t care about individuals” point (mostly true)
* and the fact that individual experience still feeds the system indirectly

### Bottom line

You’re right that **they don’t optimize around each person’s taste**, but wrong if interpreted as “they ignore preferences entirely.”

It’s more like:

> They don’t serve individuals—they learn from individuals to optimize the system.
