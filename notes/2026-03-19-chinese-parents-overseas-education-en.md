Question: Is it common for Chinese parents to send their children abroad for a bachelor's or master's degree while spending 1 million CNY or more per year?

Answer:

## Chinese Students Studying Abroad: Scale, Costs, and the 1 Million CNY Question

### How Many Chinese Students Study Abroad?

China is the **world's largest source of international students**. According to UNESCO data, a record number of Chinese students were studying for a degree abroad in 2023 — a staggering 1,021,303 students. From 1978 to 2024, a total of 8.88 million Chinese went abroad to study, and among the 7.43 million who completed their studies, 6.44 million chose to return to China.

Top destinations include the US, UK, Australia, Canada, Japan, and increasingly European and Southeast Asian countries. In the 2024/25 academic year, China was the second-largest source of international students in the US with 265,919 students, reflecting a 4% decline.

---

### What Does It Actually Cost? (Tuition + Living)

The total annual cost depends heavily on the destination country:

**United States (most expensive):**
- Tuition at elite private universities can run $65,000–$80,000 USD/year (~¥470,000–¥580,000 CNY)
- Add living costs of $20,000–$30,000/year, and total annual expenses can reach **¥600,000–¥800,000+ CNY**
- By 2023, attending Columbia University as an undergraduate demanded an annual tuition of $65,524 USD, compared to roughly 250,000–300,000 RMB/year for an average private US university in 2013.

**United Kingdom:**
- There are documented cases of students spending 1 million yuan on a UK university education overall. For a 3-year bachelor's program with high tuition and London living costs, totals can approach or exceed ¥300,000–¥400,000/year.

**Average Planned Budget:**
- According to an educational survey conducted between January and February 2024, Chinese students planning to study abroad anticipated a budget of around 528,000 yuan on average for their entire overseas study period. This is a *total* budget across all years, not per year — meaning the average is well below 1 million CNY annually.

---

### Is 1 Million CNY Per Year Common?

**Short answer: No, it is not common — but it does happen among wealthy families.**

- The average Chinese family studying abroad spends roughly **¥150,000–¥400,000 per year** on tuition and living combined, depending on destination.
- Many families can afford tuition and living costs in foreign countries that range from 150,000 yuan to 300,000 yuan per year.
- Spending **1 million CNY per year** would require attending a top-tier US or UK university in an expensive city, plus premium accommodation, travel, and lifestyle costs. This is possible but represents the upper end of spending.

A real-world example: one Chinese student's parents had spent 1.5 million CNY (~$211,500) to fund her undergraduate studies and living expenses over several years at a US university — which averages out to roughly ¥300,000–¥375,000/year, not ¥1 million/year.

---

### Who Are These Families?

Some parents are professionals like lawyers, doctors, or business executives who want the best education for their children; others are business owners who want their kids to learn new ideas abroad and then return home to help grow the family business.

In 2016, China had more than one million millionaires, and according to one survey, an estimated 83% of them planned to send their children abroad at an average age of 16.

Students from average-income families have become the primary demographic for overseas studies since 2018, with an increasing trend toward the popularization and normalization of overseas education.

---

### Are There Concerns About This Investment?

Yes. Annual salaries for graduates who studied abroad fell from 268,200 RMB in 2020 to 204,500 RMB in 2023, and within China, the lowest-paying jobs for student returnees paid as little as 4,000 RMB (~$556 USD) a month.

A 2023 survey by New Oriental Education and Kantar found that among students and parents intending to pursue postgraduate degrees abroad, 27% said their funding plans had been impacted by the pandemic — far higher than the 19% who said the same in 2021 and 2022.
