Question: For one's reputation, does it depend on how others perceive you rather than self-promotion? And is peer/technical feedback important? But can some innovations, like the Transformer architecture, go unrecognized for a time?

Answer:

You've raised a genuinely nuanced point that touches on **reputation, peer recognition, self-promotion, and the sociology of innovation**. Let's break it down carefully.

---

## 1. Reputation Is Fundamentally About Others' Perception — Not Self-Promotion

This is essentially correct. Reputation, by definition, is a *social construct* — it exists in the minds of others, not in your own self-assessment. Self-promotion can *signal* your work to others, but it cannot *create* reputation on its own. Overconfident self-promotion without substance is often counterproductive.

Peer recognition can give voice to individuals who may not naturally self-promote or who work in quieter or more technical roles — valuing what people bring to the table in a deeper, more authentic way.

In technical fields especially, **what your peers say about your work carries far more weight** than anything you say about yourself. A senior engineer praised by colleagues for elegant architecture or rigorous code review earns a reputation that no LinkedIn post can manufacture.

---

## 2. Technical Peer Feedback Is Especially Credible

In technical communities — engineering, science, academia — reputation is heavily shaped by:

- Code reviews and architectural decisions others praise
- Citations of your research by respected peers
- Being invited into expert circles (editorial boards, design committees, etc.)

Each act of peer review has an incremental effect in terms of recognition. When such recognition is publicly displayed, it gradually contributes to building one's overall reputation as an individual researcher — and may lead to further invitations, such as joining editorial boards.

This means peer feedback is not just important — in technical fields it is often **the primary currency of reputation**.

---

## 3. The Crucial Caveat: Great Innovations Can Be Delayed in Recognition

Your instinct about the Transformer is well-placed. This is a well-documented phenomenon in science called the **"Sleeping Beauty" problem** — papers or innovations that are ahead of their time and go unrecognized until the broader field "wakes up."

Delayed recognition, in which innovative discoveries are re-evaluated after a long period, has significant implications for scientific progress. Big findings are often initially restricted or neglected, as the scientific community is sceptical about them.

Causes for delayed recognition have been postulated to include: information overload from the large amount of available information, modest communication skills of authors, insufficient promotion of ideas, conflict with existing theory, the author's position in the social hierarchy of science, and the conservative nature of establishments.

### The Transformer Example

The 2017 "Attention Is All You Need" paper introduced the original encoder–decoder Transformer model. At the time, the focus of the research was on improving seq2seq for machine translation, by removing its recurrence to process all tokens in parallel. It was initially seen as *just another machine translation paper*, not the revolution it turned out to be.

While on the surface appearing to be yet just another machine translation paper, "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the Transformer architecture which was destined to become the backbone of modern large language models, vision-language models, and countless other applications.

Its full impact on AI — GPT, BERT, LLMs — only became clear *years later*. The peers who reviewed it in 2017 did not fully grasp what they were looking at.

---

## 4. What This Means Practically

| Factor | Role in Reputation |
|---|---|
| Self-promotion | Signals existence, but does not *create* credibility |
| Peer/technical feedback | Primary source of legitimate reputation in expert fields |
| Time | Innovations may need years before peers recognize true value |
| Community position | Being known in a field helps get your work *noticed* sooner |

The tension you identify is real: **peer feedback is the gold standard for reputation, but peers themselves can be wrong or slow** — especially for genuinely novel ideas that challenge existing paradigms.

Resistance to new ideas seems to be an enduring human characteristic, and scientists — despite extolling the virtues of objectivity — have often proved themselves very human in this respect. Many of the great breakthroughs of modern science were initially rejected or ignored, sometimes for decades, mainly because of bias.

---

## Summary

- ✅ Reputation is what *others* think of you, not what you claim about yourself
- ✅ Technical peer feedback is the most credible form of reputation in expert communities
- ✅ But peer recognition is fallible — paradigm-shifting work can be misunderstood or ignored initially
- ✅ The Transformer is a perfect example: initially seen as a narrow NLP paper, later recognized as foundational to all of modern AI
- ⚠️ This means true reputation may require **both peer recognition AND time** — especially for truly original work
