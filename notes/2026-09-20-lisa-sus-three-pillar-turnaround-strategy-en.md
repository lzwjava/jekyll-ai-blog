Question: **What were Lisa Su’s “three pillars” / core strategy for turning AMD around?**

Answer:

Yes. The famous three-part strategy Lisa Su gave AMD when she became CEO in 2014 was remarkably simple:

> **1. Build great products**
> **2. Deepen customer relationships**
> **3. Simplify everything we do**

She explicitly chose only three because, as she later explained, if there are five or ten priorities, people lose focus. ([Forbes][1])

### 1. Build great products

This was the **core**.

AMD's problem wasn't primarily "marketing harder" or "hire more people." It needed technically differentiated products.

Su concentrated AMD's engineering resources on a few major roadmaps, most famously **Zen CPU architecture**, while focusing the company on high-performance computing. The idea was:

```text
technology roadmap
      ↓
great architecture
      ↓
great product
      ↓
customer adoption
      ↓
revenue / cash
      ↓
more R&D
      ↓
better products
```

AMD's current official mission still literally says:

> **"Build great products that accelerate next-generation computing experiences."** ([Advanced Micro Devices, Inc.][2])

And Su's more recent explanation is even more interesting: technology companies need to **look 3–5 years ahead and make big technology bets**, then invest heavily in R&D to make those bets real. ([Harvard Business Review][3])

### 2. Deepen customer relationships

This isn't traditional "sales."

Her model is roughly:

```text
customer:
    "Here is the problem we will have in 3 years."

AMD engineers:
    "Then we need this architecture."

AMD:
    builds it

customer:
    deploys it

feedback:
    → next generation
```

Su has said that building great products and deep customer relationships **go hand in hand**. Customers—cloud providers, OEMs, enterprises—can tell AMD what problems they're trying to solve several years into the future. That gives engineers information for choosing the right technical bets. ([Andreessen Horowitz][4])

So:

**Customer → future problems → product roadmap → product → customer**

rather than:

**Engineer → build something cool → hope somebody buys it.**

### 3. Simplify everything

This one is easy to underestimate.

It means:

```text
fewer markets
fewer priorities
fewer projects
clearer organization
clearer accountability
less bureaucracy
more resources on the important roadmap
```

When Su took over, AMD had limited resources. She couldn't afford to pursue everything.

So the strategic question became:

> **Where can AMD actually be world-class?**

AMD focused on high-performance computing and targeted areas such as gaming, data center and immersive platforms, while aligning the organization around those priorities. ([Forbes][5])

## The deeper pattern

I think the interesting part isn't the three words themselves. It's the **causal loop**:

```text
        ┌──────────────────┐
        │  Pick a few      │
        │  huge markets    │
        └────────┬─────────┘
                 ↓
        ┌──────────────────┐
        │ Focus engineering│
        │ on great products│
        └────────┬─────────┘
                 ↓
        ┌──────────────────┐
        │ Deep customer    │
        │ relationships    │
        └────────┬─────────┘
                 ↓
        ┌──────────────────┐
        │ Learn future     │
        │ customer needs   │
        └────────┬─────────┘
                 ↓
        ┌──────────────────┐
        │ Better roadmap   │
        │ / big bets       │
        └────────┬─────────┘
                 ↓
             repeat
```

And **"simplify" is the constraint that protects the loop**.

That's why the strategy worked particularly well for a technically deep company like AMD: **don't spread scarce engineering talent across 50 mediocre things. Concentrate it on a few things that can become exceptional.**

For your own AI company, there's a pretty direct analogy:

```text
Lisa Su / AMD             AI startup

Great products       →    great agent/model/product
Deep customers       →    work directly with real users
Simplify             →    kill low-value projects
Long-term bets       →    invest in a few core technologies
Execution            →    ship every week
```

AMD's current public language has evolved, but the same themes remain: great products, high-performance/adaptive computing, innovation, customer value, and **execution excellence**. ([AMD][6])
