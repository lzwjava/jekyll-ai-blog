**Question:** Do people actually run a one-person company (OPC) from home with many computers or AI agents working in parallel to do software consulting for clients? Can it succeed, and what are the results so far, given that a human can't multitask that much?

**Answer:**

Yes, people are doing it, and your multitasking concern is the main limit.

**What's happening**

- The OPC model is real and growing, especially in China. Nearly 7.32 million OPCs were newly registered there by the end of last year, up 42.3% year over year, helped by a revised Company Law and agentic AI tools.
- More than 20 Chinese cities have introduced OPC-specific support policies.
- Registrations don't show how many of these companies earn real money.

**Do they really run 10 machines?**

- Mostly no. The agents are software, so they usually run in the cloud or on one machine. Most one-person companies reportedly run 3–6 specialized agents (research, content, code, sales, support) sharing one memory.
- The limit isn't computers. It's how many things one human can supervise.

**Your multitasking point is correct**

- Agents can parallelize the doing, but you still handle the reviewing, deciding, client communication, selling and taking responsibility for the result.
- One commentary on agent-assisted coding says you must define which repos and environments allow auto mode, which changes still need a human, and how actions get logged. That is the human bottleneck.
- Typical reported gains are more modest than "10 people." One report says typical cases show 3–5× efficiency gains, with one person covering what a 5-person team did.
- Ten parallel agents don't give you 10× output. They give you 10× the output you have to check, and bad code shipped to a client is your liability.

**Reported results**

- The success stories are mostly product builders, not consultants. For example, Base44's founder built it alone in six months and sold it to Wix for $80M. Also, 19% of founders in YC's latest batch were solo, with a median core team of 3.
- A caution on the evidence: most of what I found comes from trend newsletters and promotional blogs, some auto-generated, and not from audited studies. One source claims 77% of solopreneurs are profitable in year one, but I'd treat that skeptically because there's no clear survivorship-bias correction.

**Can software consulting succeed this way?**

- It can, if you stay small and realistic. Consulting is a good fit because clients pay for outcomes, and agents speed up scoping, prototyping, boilerplate, tests and docs.
- Success usually depends on:
  - **A narrow niche**, such as one industry or one type of integration.
  - **Limiting active projects** to what you can personally review, often 2–4 at once rather than 10.
  - **Reviewing everything** that goes to the client, with tests as your safety net.
  - **Sales and trust**, which AI doesn't replace and which are often the real bottleneck.
  - **Pricing on value**, not hours, since your hourly cost keeps dropping.
- Risks include clients doing it themselves with AI, price pressure as competitors adopt the same tools, quality and security errors, and burnout from constant context-switching.

In short, one person plus agents can plausibly do the work of a small team, but not an army. Expect a good solo consulting income rather than a 10-person agency, and scale by quality and niche rather than machine count.
