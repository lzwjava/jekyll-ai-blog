**Question:** Do people fundamentally pursue status, or genuine impact/helping others — and is this fixed, or can both coexist (e.g. Steve Jobs)?

**Answer:**

Not an either/or. Best model: status and impact are the same underlying drive running through different reward circuitry, and which one dominates shifts with scarcity.

**1. Status is the ancestral proxy, impact is the modern narrative wrapper**

Evolutionarily, status wasn't the goal — it was a proxy for resource access, mating opportunity, coalition safety. Brains got wired to chase the *proxy* directly because it was a cheap, reliable signal. "Changing the world" is that same circuitry, but running on a symbolic economy (attention, legacy, historical record) instead of a physical one. You don't need food scarcity to feel the itch — you need *rank* scarcity, and rank is infinitely renewable because there's always someone above you.

So the honest answer to "what do people pursue" is: **relative position**, and impact/helping is one of several currencies that position can be denominated in (others: wealth, beauty, followers, citations, moral standing).

**2. Why it's not static — it's regulated by what's currently scarce**

- Early career / resource-scarce: status dominates because you need proof-of-work to unlock access (this is why juniors chase title, GitHub stars, "10x engineer" labels).
- Once status/survival is secured: the marginal utility of more status drops, and intrinsic drives (competence, autonomy, curiosity — Deci & Ryan's self-determination theory) become relatively more rewarding, because they were always there, just outcompeted.
- This is why you see senior people who've "made it" suddenly caring about mentorship, open source, or research for its own sake — not because they became better people, it's the reward landscape shifting once status is cheap for them.

**3. Steve Jobs is the clean case of both running simultaneously, not sequentially**

Isaacson's biography is explicit: Jobs literally said the goal was "to put a dent in the universe" — that phrase itself is pure status language (maximal, singular, comparison to *everyone else who ever lived*), fused with genuine obsessive product/aesthetic taste that had nothing to do with what anyone else thought (fonts, industrial design, "insanely great"). He didn't resolve the tension — he ran both loops at once and let them reinforce each other: status funded the freedom to obsess over craft, and craft-driven products generated more status than status-seeking alone would have.

This is actually the strategically correct move if you're optimizing for both: **impact is a more durable status vehicle than status-seeking is**, because status-chasing directly (title, fame, flexing) triggers social defense/envy responses in others and caps out, while genuine craft/impact compounds and status arrives as a side-effect that people don't resent as much.

**4. Practical takeaway (not career advice, just the mechanism)**

If you optimize *for* status directly, you get zero-sum games — bounded upside, and you're now dependent on others' judgment. If you optimize for the work/impact and let status be a downstream signal, you get compounding upside and you're not hostage to social approval to feel the reward. Same brain circuitry, different feedback loop topology. Liang Wenfeng's public stance on DeepSeek (research for exploration, indifference to short-term commercialization/fame) and Karpathy's whole "build cool stuff in public, let recognition be a byproduct" posture are both just this strategy executed deliberately rather than left on autopilot.
