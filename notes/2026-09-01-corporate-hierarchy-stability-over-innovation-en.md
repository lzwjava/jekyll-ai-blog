Question: Do large corporations build hierarchical structures primarily to reward seniority with better compensation and easier work, rather than focusing on tech innovation like companies such as Databricks or OpenAI?

Answer:

You're largely right, and this is a well-observed tension in organizational theory. Here's how it breaks down:

**The "Stable Machine" Corps (Microsoft, IBM, JPMorgan, etc.)**

- Hierarchy serves multiple purposes: *reward*, *control*, *risk management*, and *political insulation*
- Senior people often accumulate privileges (comp, PTO, autonomy) as a *retention and loyalty mechanism*, not necessarily because their output scales proportionally
- The structure itself becomes self-perpetuating — managers justify headcount, headcount justifies more managers
- Innovation is often *acquired* (buyouts) rather than built internally, because the machine isn't optimized for speed
- Risk tolerance is low, so layers of approval slow everything down by design

**The "Tech-Driven" Orgs (Databricks, OpenAI, SpaceX, early Google)**

- Flat-ish hierarchy, output-driven culture — your *leverage comes from what you ship*, not your title
- Senior engineers/researchers still work extremely hard — often harder than juniors
- Comp is heavily tied to *equity and performance*, not tenure
- Culture of "brilliant jerks tolerated if they produce" is common
- They can afford flat structures *because the tech IS the moat* — bureaucracy would kill them

**The Honest Nuance**

- Big corps aren't purely corrupt — hierarchy also provides *stability, compliance, and scale* that startups genuinely can't manage
- But yes, over time, many large orgs drift toward **rewarding political navigation over technical output**
- This is sometimes called the **"Peter Principle"** — people rise to their level of incompetence — or **"org debt"**, analogous to tech debt
- The structure optimizes for *survival of the institution*, not maximizing innovation

The core tradeoff: big corps trade **speed and innovation** for **stability and predictability**. The hierarchy is partly a feature, partly an emergent bureaucratic disease.
