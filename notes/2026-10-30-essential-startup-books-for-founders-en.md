## 1. The Mom Test

Rob Fitzpatrick · Customer discovery

Read this first.

Learn how to distinguish real demand from people merely saying your idea is good.

For your AI consultancy, don't ask, "Would you use an AI agent that automates your workflow?" Ask how the customer handles that workflow today, how much it costs, what has already failed, and who controls the budget.

Apply it: Interview 10 potential customers before committing weeks to a new product.

## 3. The Lean Startup

Eric Ries · Experiments and product-market fit

Your engineering background makes it easy to build sophisticated products. The danger is building too much before proving that customers care.

Apply it: Test a paid AI service with a few customers before building a full SaaS platform. Measure actual payment, retention, and repeat usage—not GitHub stars or demo enthusiasm.

## 4. Traction

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares · Distribution

A product with no distribution is a common startup failure. This book covers different channels for acquiring customers and finding the ones that work.

Apply it: Test your technical blog, developer communities, open-source projects, founder content, and direct outreach as channels for generating AI consulting leads. Track which actually creates paying customers.

## 5. High Output Management

Andrew Grove · Management and leverage

This is a foundational book on building an organization that produces more output than its founder could produce alone.

It becomes especially relevant when you have contractors, employees, or AI agents doing work in parallel.

Apply it: Design a system where you handle customer discovery and architecture while AI agents handle bounded implementation, testing, documentation, and research—with measurable quality gates.

## 6. The Hard Thing About Hard Things

Ben Horowitz · Founder psychology and difficult decisions

Read this for the reality of running a company when the answer isn't obvious: financial pressure, hiring mistakes, organizational conflict, and difficult trade-offs.

## 5. The most important business principle for your situation

I'd separate your company into two engines:

- Cash-flow engine: AI consulting, agent implementation, model training, inference optimization, and software development. Sell measurable business outcomes and use the revenue to fund your experiments.
- Equity engine: Build reusable tools, infrastructure, open-source projects, and eventually products such as the next generation of Fun Live. Look for recurring demand and distribution rather than assuming that a technically excellent product will sell itself.

This is a strategic hypothesis, not a guarantee. Consulting can consume all your time, while product development can consume cash without producing revenue. You need explicit limits on both.

Your existing engineering skills are already an asset. The highest-return learning now may be the ability to identify valuable problems, sell solutions, and turn repeated customer needs into products.
