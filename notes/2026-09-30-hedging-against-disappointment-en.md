Question: Is it a good strategy to keep expectations low, expecting things to be worse than hoped, so that life brings less suffering?

Answer: Partly, but with a caveat. The core idea is sound: suffering often comes from the gap between what we expected and what happened. If you expect a perfect outcome and get an ordinary one, you feel worse than someone who expected an ordinary one. Aiming for realism rather than optimism can protect you from a lot of unnecessary disappointment.

But "expect things to be poorer" as a blanket rule has costs:

- **It can become self-fulfilling.** If you expect a job interview, relationship, or project to go badly, you may put in less effort or act more guardedly, which makes the bad outcome more likely.
- **You lose the pleasure of anticipation.** Looking forward to things is itself a source of happiness, and constant pessimism gives that up.
- **Expecting the worst can hurt as much as living through it.** Chronic pessimism can mean suffering in advance, even for bad outcomes that never arrive.

A more useful distinction is between **outcomes** and **effort or standards**:

1. **Be realistic about outcomes**, especially ones outside your control (other people's behavior, luck, timing). Think "this could go well or badly, and I'll cope either way," not "this will go badly."
2. **Keep high standards for your own effort.** You can care a lot and try hard while holding the result loosely.
3. **Prepare for setbacks without dwelling on them.** The Stoics practiced imagining what might go wrong so they'd be ready, not so they'd feel gloomy. Psychologists call a similar approach "defensive pessimism," which works for some anxious people when it prompts planning rather than dread.
4. **In relationships, say what you need.** Silent expectations that others fail to meet are a major source of suffering, and lowering them isn't always the fix. Sometimes communicating them is.

So the goal isn't to expect less from life, but to expect more accurately and stay flexible when reality differs from your plan.
