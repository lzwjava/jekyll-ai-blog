Question: Why do we cut potatoes into slices for air frying, and what happens if we use other shapes like triangles or squares?

Answer:

**Why "one cut = one slice" logic**

When you cut a potato once straight through, you get **two pieces** — but each cut face is a **flat, thin cross-section**, which is what we call a slice. The word "slice" emphasizes the *shape and thinness*, not just that it was separated.

---

**Why slices beat chunks (pieces) in an air fryer — yes, you're right about surface area**

You nailed the core idea. An air fryer works by blasting hot air around the food. The more surface area exposed to that moving air, the better it cooks. Here's the breakdown:

| Shape | Surface Area | Cooking Result |
|---|---|---|
| Thick chunk/piece | Low (air only hits outside) | Outside burns, inside raw |
| Thin slice | High (both flat faces + edges exposed) | Even, crispy cook throughout |

A thin slice is essentially **all surface** — the heat reaches the center easily because the center is barely any distance from the edge.

---

**What about triangles, squares, wedges?**

Shape doesn't matter as much as **thickness does**. Here's how different cuts perform:

- **Triangle / wedge** — thicker toward one end, so it cooks unevenly. The thin tip may burn before the thick base is done
- **Square / cube (diced)** — works fine, but needs tossing/shaking more often since fewer flat faces rest against airflow
- **Rectangle / baton (fry shape)** — great, similar principle to a slice — long and thin means good air exposure
- **Round slice** — ideal, uniform thickness means perfectly even cooking

The rule is: **uniform thickness + thinness = better air frying**, regardless of the exact shape.

---

**Summary**

Yes, your intuition is correct — slices are better because they maximize contact with the circulating hot air. The shape (round vs triangle vs square) matters less than keeping the thickness consistent and thin.
