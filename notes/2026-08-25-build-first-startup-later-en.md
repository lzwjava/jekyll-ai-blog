Question: **What is Paul Graham’s “How Universities Should Prepare Founders” about?**

Answer: It’s a **new Paul Graham essay from August 2026**, and the core argument is surprisingly radical:

> **Universities should not primarily teach “entrepreneurship.” They should produce people who are extremely good at building things and who habitually build things on their own.** ([Paul Graham][1])

### The core model

Graham thinks successful founders have roughly:

```text
deep technical/domain knowledge
        +
ability to build
        +
habit of building independently
        ↓
      founder
        ↓
   startup ideas emerge
```

Not:

```text
MBA
  ↓
"entrepreneurship" course
  ↓
business plan
  ↓
pitch investors
  ↓
startup
```

He explicitly attacks business-plan competitions because they teach students to optimize for **investors rather than users**. His preferred loop is:

```text
interesting problem
      ↓
build something
      ↓
use it
      ↓
learn deeply
      ↓
modify it
      ↓
maybe other people want it
      ↓
startup
```

He says the best startup ideas often come from **random side projects**, precisely because someone isn't consciously trying to invent a startup. ([Paul Graham][1])

### The part I think is especially relevant to you

Graham says universities should encourage students to **work on their own projects** for four reasons:

1. **Projects teach deeply.** Building is a stronger learning mechanism than studying for exams.
2. **Projects reveal cofounders.** You discover who is actually good to work with by building together.
3. **Projects normalize autonomy.** A startup doesn't have a teacher assigning the next task; you're already accustomed to deciding what to do.
4. **Projects generate startup ideas.** The weird project you're building for fun may turn out to be extremely valuable. ([Paul Graham][1])

And this is a particularly good sentence to understand:

> **“Build things without thinking about whether they'll turn into startups.”** ([Paul Graham][1])

That's basically the opposite of **“I want to become an entrepreneur, what startup should I start?”**

### His deeper thesis

He believes the university's highest-value role for a future founder is basically:

```text
learn powerful ideas
        +
get very good at building
        +
give yourself lots of unstructured time
        +
build weird things with friends
```

The university should actually **remove some constraints**, rather than adding more entrepreneurship programs.

He uses Harvard's reading period as an example: students suddenly had a period with **no immediate coursework deadline**, and both Microsoft and Meta originated from projects during such periods. ([Paul Graham][1])

He even makes the provocative argument that universities should tolerate some students "wasting" their free time, because the upside from the highly energetic students can be enormous. ([Paul Graham][1])

### And there's an important AI-era point

Graham adds a note specifically addressing AI:

> Even if AI writes most code, students should still study CS.

His reasoning is that understanding CS remains valuable for **problem solving and engineering judgment**, and if AI writes your code, you're increasingly functioning like an engineering manager who still needs to understand the work deeply. ([Paul Graham][1])

That is quite aligned with the direction you're already taking: **LLMs + systems + actually building agents/products**, rather than trying to accumulate credentials about entrepreneurship.

The essay's deepest idea is probably:

```text
Don't optimize for "becoming a founder."

Optimize for:

    becoming unusually capable
            +
    building things constantly
            +
    following interesting problems

              ↓

        founder opportunities
        emerge naturally
```

That is very close to Graham's older idea of **“live in the future”**: get close to the technological frontier, and things that seem surprising to everyone else start looking obvious to you. ([Paul Graham][2])
