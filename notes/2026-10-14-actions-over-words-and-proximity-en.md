## Question: Should we judge people by their actions rather than their words, intentions, or physical presence—and appreciate those who actually help us?

**Answer: Yes. You're recognizing an important distinction between what people seem to offer us and what they actually contribute to our lives.**

I'd break your observation into four principles.

### 1. Physical proximity doesn't equal genuine support

Some people are physically close to us. We meet them, talk to them, have dinner with them, and see them regularly.

But when we need something concrete—an opportunity, a contract, technical knowledge, a customer introduction, or practical help—they might offer nothing.

Meanwhile, someone we've never met in person might:

- Trust our work and give us a project.
- Share valuable knowledge freely.
- Introduce us to a potential customer.
- Take a risk by working with us.
- Give us an opportunity when we have little to offer in return.

**The person who is physically closer isn't necessarily the person who contributes more to our lives.**

Distance measures physical proximity, not the quality of a relationship.

### 2. Judge relationships by actions, not imagined intentions

You said something important: it isn't just about whether people trust us or what they think about us. We should observe their actions.

Imagine three people:

| Person | What they say or seem to think | What they actually do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | Says they believe in you | Never offers any concrete support |
| B | Doesn't express much enthusiasm | Gives you a valuable customer introduction |
| C | Has never met you in person | Trusts you with a paid project |

Who has contributed the most to your progress?

Based on these facts, C has provided a meaningful opportunity, and B has also helped. A might genuinely care about you, but that care hasn't translated into practical assistance.

We cannot always know what another person thinks. We can, however, observe their behavior and make decisions based on it.

One qualification matters: **not helping you doesn't necessarily mean someone doesn't care about you.** People have their own constraints, responsibilities, and priorities. Someone can wish you well without being able or willing to help.

Therefore, evaluate people's actual contributions without pretending you can read their minds.

### 3. Nobody owes us an opportunity. We should appreciate those who give us one

This is perhaps the most important part of your observation.

Suppose you're building an AI consultancy.

You have technical skills, experience building products, and the ability to deliver projects. But you need customers.

Someone you've known for ten years doesn't give you a contract.

Then a person who discovered your technical blog yesterday contacts you, trusts your abilities, and pays you to solve a real problem.

You shouldn't resent the old acquaintance for not giving you business. They have no obligation to do so.

But you should recognize what the new customer has done.

They have provided more than money. They've given you:

- An opportunity to demonstrate your capabilities.
- A chance to create economic value.
- Evidence that someone is willing to trust your work.
- Potentially, a reference, a repeat customer, and a long-term relationship.

**Gratitude is the appropriate response to voluntary help. Entitlement is the expectation that other people must help us.**

This doesn't mean accepting every opportunity regardless of its terms. You can appreciate someone's trust while still negotiating fairly and protecting your interests.

### 4. Remember who helped you, but don't turn relationships into a ledger

There's a subtle distinction here.

You should appreciate people who help you, but you don't need to maintain a rigid accounting system in which every favor must be repaid.

A healthier approach is to remember:

- Who gave you a chance when you were unproven.
- Who shared knowledge without demanding anything in return.
- Who treated you fairly when you had little bargaining power.
- Who trusted you to deliver when the outcome was uncertain.

When you become more successful, you can help others in return—not necessarily the same people, and not necessarily in the same way.

Someone gives you your first customer introduction. Years later, you might help another engineer find their first customer.

This creates a positive cycle of trust and opportunity.

Of course, gratitude doesn't mean blind loyalty. If someone who helped you previously later behaves dishonestly, you can acknowledge their past contribution while still setting boundaries.

## 5. The deeper lesson: Focus on creating value and building trust

Consider two different approaches to life.

**Approach A: Expectation**

- These people should support me because we've known each other for years.
- They should recognize my abilities.
- They should give me opportunities.
- Why are they helping other people instead of me?

This approach gives other people considerable control over your emotional state.

**Approach B: Observation and action**

- I'll observe how people actually behave.
- I'll appreciate those who voluntarily help me.
- I'll build relationships with people who share my values and work well with me.
- I'll develop skills and products that create value for others.
- I'll pursue opportunities rather than wait for particular people to recognize me.

This approach gives you more agency.

For someone building an AI company, the distinction is especially practical.

You don't need every former colleague, friend, or acquaintance to believe in your business. You need to find people whose problems you can solve, deliver results, and earn their trust.

A customer who pays you for a successful project is demonstrating trust through action. A developer who contributes to your open-source project is demonstrating interest through action. A business partner who reliably delivers on commitments is demonstrating trustworthiness through action.

Their actions aren't perfect measures of their character, but they provide evidence you can work with.

And remember that trust is reciprocal: **you should also be the person who delivers what you promise, pays people fairly, and helps others when you can.**

That is how you build a reputation that attracts future opportunities.

## My conclusion

I would summarize your philosophy in three sentences:

1. **Don't assume that people owe you something just because you know them, have helped them, or expect them to recognize your value.**
2. **Pay attention to what people actually do, while recognizing that their actions don't always reveal their complete intentions.**
3. **Be genuinely grateful to those who voluntarily trust you, help you, or give you an opportunity—and become someone who does the same for others.**

There's one more thing worth remembering: don't become so focused on identifying who helps you that you overlook the people who simply treat you with kindness, respect, and honesty. Not every valuable relationship produces a contract, and not every meaningful contribution can be measured in money or professional advancement.

**Build your life around creating value, recognizing genuine contributions, and treating people fairly. Don't demand that everyone help you, and don't forget those who do.**
