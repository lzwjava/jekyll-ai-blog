## Question: If China enters a long property/debt adjustment, how should I live well?

**Answer:**

My answer is: **don't try to predict China perfectly. Build a personal balance sheet that works under both stagnation and recovery.**

The biggest mistake would be to optimize your life around rising Chinese property prices.

If the economy becomes Japan-like, the scarce asset changes from **real estate** to **productive human capital + globally marketable skills + liquidity + mobility**.

The IMF's current view is not "China collapses"; it is that weak domestic demand, property adjustment, high debt and deflation could persist, while growth increasingly depends on exports. ([IMF][1])

So I'd use this playbook.

---

## 1. Don't make your house your retirement plan

This is probably the most important one.

In the 2000s/2010s, a reasonable Chinese strategy could be:

```text
earn money
   ↓
buy apartment
   ↓
wait
   ↓
property appreciates
   ↓
wealth
```

I would **not** assume that works for the next 10–20 years.

Instead:

```text
income
 ↓
cash / liquid assets
 ↓
skills
 ↓
business ownership
 ↓
global assets
 ↓
mobility
```

Your home should primarily provide **housing utility**, not be treated as your main investment thesis.

And I wouldn't rush to sell purely because "China will become Japan." The better question is:

> **Does this property provide enough housing value relative to its mortgage, maintenance cost and opportunity cost?**

# 4. Keep your burn rate low

This is underrated.

Suppose two people each earn ¥50k/month.

Person A:

```text
income 50k
mortgage 20k
car 8k
lifestyle 15k
savings 7k
```

Person B:

```text
income 50k
housing 10k
living 12k
savings/investment 28k
```

Person B has **much more freedom**.

If the economy deteriorates:

```text
income -30%
```

Person B can survive.

Person A immediately has a crisis.

So your real wealth is:

> **months of freedom you can purchase with your liquid assets.**

I'd target **12–24 months of family runway** if you're going entrepreneurial.

Not because disaster is coming.

Because **optionality is extremely valuable during structural transitions.**

---

# 5. Don't chase yield

During a long deflationary period, people often make a terrible mistake:

> "My income isn't growing, so I need to take more investment risk."

No.

You need to distinguish:

```text
wealth preservation
vs
wealth creation
```

For wealth preservation:

```text
cash
high-quality bonds
diversified liquid assets
reasonable housing
```

For wealth creation:

```text
business
software
AI skills
equity
IP
```

Don't use leverage to manufacture returns.

Especially don't do:

```text
borrow ¥2M
→ speculate on property
→ borrow more
→ speculate on stocks
```

when the macro regime is deleveraging.

# 6. Your biggest asset should be your ability to relocate

Think of your family as having a **mobility option**.

```text
China
  │
  ├── Guangzhou
  ├── Shenzhen
  ├── Hong Kong
  │
  ├── Singapore
  │
  └── North America
```

You don't necessarily have to move.

The important thing is:

> **You can move if the opportunity becomes much better somewhere else.**

That means maintaining:

* strong English
* internationally recognizable technical skills
* valid travel documents
* international professional network
* globally usable portfolio/GitHub
* some foreign-currency exposure
* ability to work remotely
* immigration options where practical

This is much more valuable than trying to forecast whether Guangzhou property falls another 10% or 20%.

# 9. Don't wait for the macro economy to improve

This is important.

A Japan-style economy can still contain enormous individual winners.

Japan after 1990 produced companies and fortunes in:

* gaming
* electronics
* robotics
* manufacturing
* software
* global exports

A stagnant macro environment doesn't mean:

```text
everyone becomes poor
```

It means:

```text
asset inflation ↓
easy opportunities ↓
competition ↑
productivity becomes more important
```

So you want to be on the **productivity side** of the economy.

```text
        ↓         ↓         ↓
      China       HK     Overseas
        ↓         ↓         ↓
      job      clients    company
```

The goal isn't:

> **"Predict whether China becomes Japan."**

The goal is:

> **"Make myself economically useful in Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Hong Kong, Singapore, or the US."**

If you accomplish that, **China stagnating is survivable; China recovering is also beneficial.**

And there is a nice asymmetry here: if China's economy recovers strongly, you benefit from being there; if it stagnates, your international skills and mobility give you an escape route.

That's a much better position than making one gigantic bet on Chinese real estate.

One current data point reinforces the strategy: China's services sector is still expanding, while advanced-technology SMEs are receiving increasing policy support—so the economy is not simply "collapsing"; it's undergoing a **reallocation of growth engines**. ([Reuters][3])
