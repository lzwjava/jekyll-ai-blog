**Question:** Is a good way to run a WeChat group to regularly show real results (weekly or monthly) instead of talking too much, while documenting progress in a public log like my GitHub blog? As I achieve more results—such as LLM training, inference experiments, or improving my IELTS score—will people naturally check those updates?

**Answer:**

Yes, this can be an effective approach, but it works best when combined with consistency and usefulness.

A public progress log serves several purposes:

* It creates a history of what you've actually done, instead of only making plans.
* People who join later can quickly understand what you've been working on.
* It gives interested members something to explore without requiring you to repeat the same explanations.
* Over months or years, it becomes evidence of long-term learning and execution.

Your GitHub blog is a reasonable place for this because it provides an organized archive rather than messages disappearing in chat.

However, simply posting results is not enough. The results should answer questions that readers naturally have:

* What was the goal?
* What did you try?
* What happened?
* What did you learn?
* What will you try next?

Even a short weekly update like this is valuable:

> **Week 27**
>
> * Trained a 286M parameter language model for 90k steps.
> * Benchmarked BERT inference on an RTX 4070.
> * Learned how FlashAttention reduces memory usage.
> * Studied 8 hours for IELTS and completed two reading tests.
> * Next week: deploy a cloud inference API for community members.

This is much easier for people to follow than dozens of daily messages.

Over time, visible progress often builds credibility because people can see the accumulation of work. Someone interested in local LLM deployment, for example, might browse your previous posts before asking questions in the group.

It's also helpful to distinguish between **activity** and **results**:

| Activity                     | Result                                           |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| "Today I studied attention." | "Implemented multi-head attention from scratch." |
| "Trying cloud GPUs."         | "Served 50 inference requests successfully."     |
| "Learning English."          | "Improved IELTS reading practice from X to Y."   |
| "Reading papers."            | "Reproduced the paper's benchmark."              |

People generally find concrete outputs more informative than descriptions of effort alone.

One thing to keep in mind is that not everyone in a group will regularly check external updates. Many members may read only occasional highlights, while a smaller subset will become regular followers of your progress. That's normal. A concise weekly or monthly summary in the group, with a link to the full write-up for those who want details, often works well.

The long-term advantage is that after dozens or hundreds of updates, you build a searchable record of experiments, projects, and lessons learned. That archive can be useful not only for your community, but also for collaborators, employers, or anyone evaluating your work. Consistent, well-documented progress tends to be more persuasive than frequent claims without accompanying evidence.
