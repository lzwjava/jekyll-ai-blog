The chart tells a clear story in three phases:

**Phase 1 — Record-breaking surge (2021–2023)**
China pumped out ever-larger volumes of new loans each year, peaking at ~¥22.75 trillion in 2023. The government was aggressively stimulating the economy — first to recover from COVID, then to sustain growth. Banks were essentially directed to lend.

**Phase 2 — Sharp reversal (2024)**
Loans dropped by nearly ¥5 trillion in a single year. The main culprits: the property market collapsed (fewer mortgages, fewer developer loans), local governments stopped borrowing as freely, and consumers stayed cautious. The credit machine slowed even though interest rates were cut.

**Phase 3 — Structural weakness (2025–2026)**
At ¥16.27 trillion, 2025 was the lowest since 2018 — and 2026 is tracking even weaker. This is the concerning part: it suggests the problem isn't just a cyclical dip. Businesses and households simply don't want to borrow, even when credit is cheap. Economists call this a "liquidity trap" dynamic — pushing on a string.

**The big-picture implication**

In China's growth model, credit is the engine. When loan issuance shrinks, so does investment, construction, and consumption. The trend since 2023 is a warning sign that China is struggling with deflationary pressure similar to what Japan experienced in the 1990s — too much debt, too little demand, and interest rate cuts that aren't doing enough to revive borrowing appetite.
