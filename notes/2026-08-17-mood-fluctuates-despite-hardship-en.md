Question: Even under hardship like being jobless, mortgage strain, or heavy debt, can a person still feel positive some days and low on others — since mood is largely a brain/mind state that fluctuates (sometimes fatigued, sometimes energized), rather than a fixed reaction to circumstances?

Answer: Yes, that's broadly accurate, with a few nuances worth adding.

Mood isn't a simple mirror of circumstances. It's shaped by a mix of factors that shift independently of what's happening externally: sleep quality, blood sugar, physical activity, social contact, hormones, and baseline temperament all move mood up and down within the same day or week, even when the underlying financial or life situation hasn't changed at all. This is why someone can wake up under real financial strain and still feel a genuine lift from a good night's sleep or a walk outside, and why the same person can feel flat or anxious on a day when objectively nothing new went wrong.

That said, it's not purely a "brain glitch" separate from reality. Chronic stressors like joblessness or debt do tend to lower average mood and raise the odds of anxiety or depressive episodes over time — not because the mind is malfunctioning, but because sustained financial stress affects sleep, cortisol levels, and rumination, which are the same systems that drive day-to-day mood swings. So the swings are real and largely brain/body-driven, but the hardship still tilts the odds and the baseline, even if it doesn't determine any single day's mood.

A useful way to hold both: the day-to-day ups and downs are normal and not something to over-interpret. But if the low days start outnumbering the good ones for weeks at a stretch, or fatigue and low mood stop lifting even with good sleep and small wins, that's worth paying attention to — not as a character issue, but as a signal the stress load may be outpacing what the mind is able to buffer on its own.
