Question: Can prolonged fear, hostility, and intense political conflict at the top of a corrupt government make people become cold, harsh, ruthless, or even evil?

Answer: **Yes, that can happen—but it isn't inevitable.** The mechanism is less “politics magically makes people evil” and more that a sustained hostile environment can systematically change how people perceive risk, trust, morality, and other people.

A useful way to think about it:

```text
constant threat
      ↓
hypervigilance
      ↓
"I must protect myself / my faction"
      ↓
low trust + suspicion
      ↓
loyalty becomes more important than truth
      ↓
opponents become perceived as threats
      ↓
cruel actions become easier to justify
      ↓
repetition → normalization
      ↓
cold / ruthless behavior
```

The particularly dangerous part is **mutual fear**.

If person A thinks:

> "If I don't destroy B first, B will destroy me."

and B thinks exactly the same thing, both sides can escalate even if neither originally wanted extreme conflict.

Then institutions can reinforce it:

* **Power becomes zero-sum:** losing office may mean losing wealth, freedom, or even safety.
* **Trust collapses:** people assume others are plotting against them.
* **Information becomes distorted:** subordinates tell leaders what they think leaders want to hear.
* **Loyalty beats competence:** "Are you one of us?" becomes more important than "Are you right?"
* **Violence/coercion becomes normalized:** once extreme actions repeatedly produce short-term political benefits, the psychological barrier falls.
* **Dehumanization develops:** opponents stop being seen as ordinary humans with competing interests and become "enemies."

And there is an especially nasty feedback loop:

```text
fear → repression → resentment → conspiracy
       ↑                         ↓
       └──── more fear ←─────────┘
```

This is why people operating for years inside highly adversarial political systems can become **extremely suspicious, emotionally hardened, and willing to do things that they might have considered unthinkable earlier in their lives**.

But there's an important distinction: **harsh circumstances don't automatically create evil people.** Some people become more cynical and ruthless; others become more principled precisely because they've seen what happens when everyone abandons principles.

In fact, one of the strongest protective mechanisms is having **rules and institutions that allow people to lose power without believing they will be destroyed**.

If losing an election means:

```text
"I become the opposition."
```

political competition can remain relatively peaceful.

If losing means:

```text
"I lose everything → my enemies will retaliate → perhaps I go to prison/die."
```

then political competition can become existential warfare.

That difference is enormous. It explains why **the structure of incentives and perceived personal survival** can matter as much as individual morality.
