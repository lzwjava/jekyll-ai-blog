Question: **In a capitalist economy, do companies with strong products and brands get pricing leverage—so customers accept higher prices?**

Answer: **Yes. That is one of the core economic advantages of a strong business.**

The key idea is **pricing power**.

A company is not simply selling a product at `cost + margin`. If customers perceive meaningful differences between alternatives, the company can capture some of the value it creates as profit.

A simplified model:

```text
Customer's willingness to pay
            │
            ▼
       ┌─────────┐
       │ Product │
       │ + Brand │
       │ + Trust │
       │ + UX    │
       └─────────┘
            │
            ▼
      Willingness to pay
            │
            ▼
      ┌──────────────┐
      │ Actual price │
      └──────────────┘
            │
            ▼
       Gross profit
```

Suppose two companies sell roughly equivalent products:

```text
Company A:
cost = $70
price = $100
profit = $30

Company B:
cost = $70
strong brand / product / distribution
price = $150
profit = $80
```

Why would someone pay `$150`?

Because **the customer isn't buying the manufacturing cost**. They're buying the perceived value.

For example, a strong brand can provide:

* **Trust** — "I know this will work."
* **Lower perceived risk** — "If something breaks, they'll support me."
* **Status / identity** — the product says something about the buyer.
* **Better experience** — UX, ecosystem, convenience.
* **Switching costs** — leaving the product is painful.
* **Distribution** — it's available everywhere.
* **Consistency** — customers know what they're going to get.

This is why two products with similar physical production costs can have radically different economics.

### The important distinction

**Good product ≠ automatically high pricing power.**

Pricing power comes from something closer to:

```text
Pricing Power
≈
Customer Value
× Differentiation
× Trust
× Switching Costs
× Scarcity
```

while being constrained by:

```text
Competition
Substitutes
Customer price sensitivity
Regulation
Capacity
```

So imagine three businesses:

```text
Commodity business
    ↓
"Everyone sells basically the same thing."
    ↓
Price ≈ market price
    ↓
Thin margins


Differentiated business
    ↓
"My product is meaningfully better."
    ↓
Some pricing power
    ↓
Higher margins


Strong brand + product + ecosystem
    ↓
"Customers specifically want MY product."
    ↓
Large pricing power
    ↓
Potentially very high margins
```

This is also why **brand is economically valuable**. Brand isn't merely a logo or marketing aesthetic. At its strongest, it changes the customer's **reservation price**—the maximum price they're willing to pay.

A useful way to think about a company is therefore:

```text
Revenue
= customers × price

Profit
= customers × (price - variable cost) - fixed costs

Enterprise value
≈ expected future cash flows
```

A company that can increase `price` without proportionally losing customers has an enormous economic advantage.

For example:

```text
10M customers × $10/month = $1.2B/year

10M customers × $15/month = $1.8B/year
```

Same customers. Same infrastructure. Same basic product.

The extra `$600M/year` is why **pricing power is one of the most valuable properties a business can possess**.

And this leads to an even more interesting question: **why does capital flow toward companies with pricing power rather than merely companies with high revenue?** That's essentially the bridge from *"running a business"* to *"building a valuable company."*

I can break that down using Apple, NVIDIA, Costco, and a commodity manufacturer as four concrete cases.
