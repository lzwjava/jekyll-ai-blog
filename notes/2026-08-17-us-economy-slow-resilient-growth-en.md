**Question: How will the U.S. economy perform over the next 10 years, roughly 2026–2036?**

**Answer:**

My base case is **“slow but surprisingly resilient growth”**, not either a U.S. collapse or another 1990s-style boom.

If I had to summarize 2026–2036 in one sentence:

> **The U.S. will probably remain the world's strongest large economy, but growth will increasingly depend on productivity, AI, capital investment, immigration, and energy rather than simply having more workers.**

The interesting part for you is that **AI could materially change this baseline**.

### 1. The boring baseline: ~2% real growth

The Fed's longer-run projections cluster around roughly **2% real GDP growth**. Its June 2026 projections also explicitly mention strong productivity and continued AI-related capital spending as supports for growth. ([Federal Reserve][1])

So don't expect:

```text
US GDP
2026 ── 2%
2027 ── 2%
...
2036 ── 2%
```

Real economies don't behave that smoothly. I'd think in ranges:

```text
Bear case:       0–1.5% average
Base case:       1.7–2.3%
AI bull case:    2.5–3.5%+
```

The **AI bull case is the interesting one**.

If AI meaningfully increases output per worker, the U.S. could sustain significantly higher growth than demographics alone would imply.

---

## 2. The biggest structural problem: demographics

The U.S. is aging.

Census projections show the working-age share declining over the coming decades. ([Census.gov][2])

Normally:

```text
GDP growth
≈ labor-force growth
+ productivity growth
```

If labor-force growth approaches zero:

```text
GDP growth ≈ productivity growth
```

This is why AI matters enormously.

Imagine:

```text
workers:       +0.2%
productivity:  +1.8%
--------------------
GDP:            ~2.0%
```

But suppose AI eventually pushes productivity growth toward 3%:

```text
workers:       +0.2%
productivity:  +3.0%
--------------------
GDP:            ~3.2%
```

That difference compounds enormously over ten years.

At 2% annual growth:

```text
GDP × 1.22
```

At 3%:

```text
GDP × 1.34
```

At 4%:

```text
GDP × 1.48
```

That's why I would watch **U.S. productivity**, not just GDP.

---

# 3. AI is probably the largest upside variable

This is where the next decade could become unusual.

The current AI boom isn't merely a software-sector phenomenon.

The chain is:

```text
AI models
   ↓
GPU / accelerator investment
   ↓
datacenters
   ↓
electricity generation
   ↓
networking
   ↓
robots / manufacturing
   ↓
software agents
   ↓
higher output per worker
   ↓
GDP
```

The Fed is already seeing AI-related capital spending as one of the factors supporting economic activity. ([Federal Reserve][3])

And there is a potentially enormous second-order effect:

```text
AI coding agent
      ↓
one engineer produces 2×
      ↓
software becomes cheaper
      ↓
more software gets built
      ↓
more businesses become automated
      ↓
capital investment increases
      ↓
productivity rises
```

If agents eventually become capable of doing substantial portions of knowledge work autonomously, current economic models could be badly underestimating the upside.

# 4. But the U.S. has a serious fiscal problem

This is the part I would **not** ignore.

CBO's February 2026 baseline projects:

* 2026 federal deficit: **$1.9T**
* 2036 deficit: **$3.1T**
* federal debt: **120% of GDP in 2036**
* deficits remain substantially above their historical average. ([Congressional Budget Office][4])

So you get this strange combination:

```text
                  U.S. 2036

AI productivity       ↑↑↑
capital investment    ↑↑
energy investment     ↑↑
population            ↑
government debt       ↑↑↑
interest expense      ↑↑↑
aging                 ↑↑
```

The debt doesn't necessarily mean "collapse."

The more likely outcome is:

```text
higher debt
   ↓
higher interest burden
   ↓
political pressure
   ↓
tax changes / spending changes / financial repression
   ↓
slower fiscal flexibility
```

The U.S. has enormous advantages because its debt is denominated in dollars and Treasuries remain central to global finance.

But **debt is probably one of the biggest constraints on U.S. policy during 2026–2036**.

# 5. The U.S. probably remains unusually attractive to capital

This is an underrated advantage.

The U.S. has:

```text
deep capital markets
+
world-leading universities
+
frontier AI companies
+
huge software industry
+
large consumer market
+
abundant energy potential
+
dollar reserve currency
+
immigration
+
entrepreneurial ecosystem
```

This creates a positive feedback loop:

```text
talent
 ↓
companies
 ↓
capital
 ↓
technology
 ↓
high salaries
 ↓
more talent
```

Silicon Valley is powerful partly because this loop has existed for decades.

China has enormous manufacturing capability.

Europe has excellent industrial capacity and institutions.

But the U.S. remains unusually strong at **turning frontier technology into companies and capital**.

# 6. Manufacturing will probably increase

I don't think the U.S. is going to become the world's cheapest manufacturing country.

That's not the goal.

Instead:

```text
China:
cheap + huge manufacturing ecosystem

US:
automation + robotics + AI + capital-intensive manufacturing
```

Think:

```text
old factory:

1000 workers
100 machines

future factory:

200 workers
500 machines
AI control
robots
computer vision
autonomous logistics
```

The U.S. doesn't need to win on labor cost if it can win on **capital productivity**.

That could be especially important for:

* semiconductors
* aerospace
* defense
* energy
* robotics
* datacenters
* biotech
* advanced manufacturing

# 7. Energy could become a massive advantage

AI creates an unusual economic constraint:

```text
more intelligence
      ↓
more compute
      ↓
more electricity
```

So the AI economy becomes partly an **energy economy**.

If the U.S. can rapidly expand:

```text
natural gas
nuclear
solar
wind
grid infrastructure
storage
```

then cheap electricity becomes an economic moat.

The combination:

```text
US capital
+
US AI
+
US chips
+
US energy
```

could be extremely powerful.

# 8. There will probably be recessions

Don't interpret the 10-year outlook as:

```text
2026  ↗
2027  ↗
2028  ↗
...
2036  ↗
```

Much more likely:

```text
       recession
          ↓
  ↗───────┐
 /        │
/         ↓
          recession
             ↓
      AI investment boom
             ↓
          recession
```

You should expect several periods of:

* recession
* unemployment spikes
* asset crashes
* banking/credit stress
* inflation shocks
* geopolitical shocks

The important distinction is:

> **A recession is not the same thing as structural economic decline.**

The U.S. can have a nasty recession in 2028 and still have a stronger economy in 2036.

# 9. My rough 2036 scenario tree

I'd personally assign something like:

| Scenario                    | Probability | 2026–36 real GDP growth |
| --------------------------- | ----------: | ----------------------: |
| **AI productivity boom**    |         20% |                   3–4%+ |
| **Normal strong U.S.**      |         50% |                1.8–2.5% |
| **Stagnation**              |         20% |                0.5–1.5% |
| **Major structural crisis** |         10% |                   <0.5% |

These aren't forecasts from an institution—just my synthesis of the structural forces.

The most important uncertainty is:

**How much of human cognitive labor can AI actually replace or amplify?**

That's the variable economists currently have the hardest time modeling.

# 10. And this matters for your own U.S. decision

If you're thinking about the U.S. not just academically but as a place to **build an AI company / work in AI**, I'd actually be considerably more bullish than my GDP forecast.

Because:

```text
GDP growth ≠ opportunity growth
```

A country can grow 2% while a particular industry grows 20%.

For example:

```text
US economy       +2%
AI infrastructure +15%
AI software       +25%
AI agents         +30%
robotics          +20%
datacenters       +15%
```

You don't need the entire economy to boom.

You want to sit **inside the high-growth frontier**.

And over 2026–2036, I think these are among the most structurally interesting U.S. areas:

```text
AI agents
AI infrastructure
model training
inference
GPU/cloud infrastructure
robotics
energy + datacenters
AI security
biotech + AI
defense + AI
industrial automation
```

So my overall view is:

> **I would bet against a U.S. economic collapse over the next decade. I would also bet against a return to effortless 4–5% normal growth. My base case is a relatively slow ~2% economy with potentially explosive pockets of AI-driven productivity.**

The **bull case is much more interesting than the baseline**: if AI becomes a general-purpose technology comparable to electricity, computing, or the internet, the 2030–2036 U.S. economy could look substantially different from today's economy.

And the latest CBO baseline is already explicitly projecting **growth moderating while debt rises sharply**, which makes productivity growth the key variable to watch. ([Congressional Budget Office][4])
