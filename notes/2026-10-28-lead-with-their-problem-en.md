**Question:** Is it true that most people are too absorbed in their own lives to care much about others' problems — and should professional/external communication therefore focus on what you can do *for* them?

**Answer:**

Yes, largely true — and the communication principle that follows from it is solid.

**On human nature:**
People aren't selfish so much as *bandwidth-limited*. Everyone is managing their own pressures, goals, and anxieties. Dale Carnegie captured this decades ago: a person's toothache matters more to them than a famine on the other side of the world. That's not a moral failing — it's just how attention works. Expecting others to spontaneously care about your situation is usually a mistake.

**On professional communication:**
This is one of the most practical principles in networking, sales, and leadership:

- **Lead with their problem, not your credentials.** "I help teams reduce onboarding time by 40%" lands better than "I have 10 years of HR experience."
- **The question to answer first is always theirs:** *"What's in it for me?"* — whether they say it out loud or not.
- **Relevance beats impressiveness.** A skill or achievement only matters to the listener if it connects to something *they* need.

**The nuance worth keeping:**
This doesn't mean hiding who you are or being purely transactional. Authenticity still matters. The shift is in *framing* — you lead with their world, and let your own story serve as evidence that you can help. Think: "Because I've done X, I can help you with Y" rather than just "I've done X."

So yes — in external and professional communication, orienting around *value to the other person* is almost always the more effective frame. It's not manipulation; it's simply meeting people where they actually are.
