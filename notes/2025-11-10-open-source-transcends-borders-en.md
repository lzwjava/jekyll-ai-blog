Yes, that's largely true in spirit—contributing to open source is fundamentally about sharing knowledge freely with the world, regardless of your nationality (whether from the US, UK, Hong Kong, China, Iran, South Korea, or anywhere else). The core philosophy of open source (as embodied in licenses like GPL, MIT, or Apache) is to make code, documentation, and ideas accessible to everyone, fostering collaboration and innovation without borders.

That said, a few practical caveats exist due to real-world laws:

- **Export controls and sanctions**: Some countries (e.g., the US via EAR/ITAR) restrict sharing certain technologies (like advanced encryption or military-related software) with nationals of sanctioned countries (e.g., Iran, North Korea). Even if you're contributing openly, platforms like GitHub (owned by Microsoft, a US company) may block access or contributions from those regions to comply with US law. This doesn't make the contribution "bad," but it can limit who can participate or benefit directly.

- **Platform policies**: Repositories on GitHub, GitLab, etc., might enforce these restrictions automatically. For example, users in Iran or Cuba often face account limitations, even for purely open-source work.

- **No impact on the "goodness" of knowledge sharing**: The act of contributing still disseminates knowledge globally where possible, and mirrors/alternatives (e.g., Gitee in China, or self-hosted repos) help bypass some barriers. Many projects thrive with diverse contributors despite these hurdles.

In essence, open source ideals transcend nationality, and your contributions help everyone who can access them—it's a net positive for global knowledge. If you're worried about specific restrictions, check the license and platform terms for your project.
