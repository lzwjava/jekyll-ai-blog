**Question:** As I get older, I find that Paul Graham's ideas about creating a startup feel somewhat misleading — shouldn't it really be about creating value and leading by example? Isn't the urge to start a startup just to work hard for a few years and achieve financial freedom actually quite utilitarian (功利) and shallow? Shouldn't people focus more on learning and personal growth?

**Answer:**

You've touched on a genuinely important tension that many thoughtful people feel when they re-read Paul Graham's work with more life experience. Let me break this down honestly.

---

## Where Your Critique Has Real Merit

### 1. The "Compress Your Life Into a Sprint" Problem

Graham's approach essentially suggests compressing all your hard work into just a few years on a startup. While this may be a great way to build businesses that push industries forward, it has real dangers — the appeal is framed around freedom, focus, and making something lasting, but it can become a purely mercenary calculus.

This is precisely the **功利 (utilitarian/mercenary)** quality you're sensing. The framing of "work insanely hard for 3–5 years, then exit and be free" treats the startup as a *financial instrument*, not a vehicle for genuine contribution or growth.

### 2. The Accessibility Illusion

Graham assumes the ability to start a company is equally accessible to everyone, and this presumed equality of opportunity is used to legitimize gross wealth inequality. What actually happens is that wealthy people like Graham fund startups because they *think* these things are valuable — and through venture funding, rich people legitimize startups.

So the "anyone can do it" narrative obscures a system that's already heavily filtered by class, network, and access.

### 3. The Growth & Learning Gap

Graham's essays appeal directly to the deeply held insecurities of intelligent introverts — we want to believe there's a world that will worship us, not cram us into ever smaller boxes and treat us like cogs. As an inspirational piece, it's undeniably powerful. Yet for many points, he drifts into territory that is clearly not his specialty.

The essays are motivational *manifestos*, not balanced guides to human development. They say very little about the slow, non-linear nature of real personal growth, wisdom, or becoming a good person.

---

## Where Graham's Ideas Still Hold Up (Partially)

To be fair, not everything is mercenary:

### He Does Talk About Doing Work You Love

Graham highlights what he sees as an obvious mistake: when you're uncertain whether to follow your interests or make money, it's usually not because the options are perfectly balanced — it's because of ignorance. His solution is to try what you love, make the real thing rather than the lucrative thing.

This is actually the *opposite* of 功利 thinking — it's about intrinsic motivation. The problem is it gets buried under the wealth-creation framing.

### He Values Craftsmanship

Graham compares hackers and painters as both being "makers" — people trying to make *good things*, not just profitable things. If in the course of making good things they discover new techniques, so much the better.

---

## The Deeper Philosophical Problem You're Identifying

Your instinct is sound. There is a fundamental difference between:

| Graham's Dominant Frame | What You're Pointing To |
|---|---|
| Build fast, compress effort, exit rich | Build slowly, grow as a person, lead by example |
| Startup as wealth vehicle | Startup as expression of values |
| Financial freedom as the goal | Wisdom, contribution, character as the goal |
| "Make something people want" (instrumentally) | Create genuine, lasting value |

The Chinese concept of **功利** (gōng lì) captures it well: an orientation toward *utility and gain* as the primary measure of whether something is worth doing. Graham's framework, when stripped down, is deeply 功利 — even if he occasionally gestures toward deeper motivations.

As the philosopher **Confucius** would frame it, the *junzi* (gentleman/person of virtue) acts from moral cultivation, not from calculating return on effort. The startup-as-sprint model is essentially the opposite of this.

---

## What a More Mature Alternative Looks Like

As you've sensed, a more honest framework for meaningful work would include:

- **Creating real value** — solving problems because they matter, not to exit
- **Leading by example** — building culture, mentoring, showing what integrity in business looks like
- **Continuous learning** — treating your career as a lifelong education, not a 5-year race
- **Community and relationships** — not just "co-founders as assets" but genuine human bonds
- **Playing long games** — patient capital, sustainable growth, not unicorn-or-bust

The tech ecosystem has fully internalized the values of capitalism at its most extreme: growth-obsessed, short-sighted, and entirely structured around the needs of investors. You cannot be a software startup and just be a sustainable business — the ecosystem will reject you. Every single tech company has to become a unicorn or it is considered a failure. Graham, to his critics, helped codify and spread this mindset.

---

## Conclusion

You're right to feel this way as you get older. Paul Graham's ideas are **useful as tactics** but **impoverished as a life philosophy**. The urge to "sprint for financial freedom" is indeed 功利 — it treats life as an optimization problem rather than a journey of growth, contribution, and becoming. The best founders — and the best people — aren't sprinting toward an exit. They're building something they'd be proud of whether it made them rich or not.
