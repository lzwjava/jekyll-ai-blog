**Question: Is preferring to learn the rules of math, computer science, nature, and physics instead of the rules of humans and society a good strategy/philosophy?**

**Answer:**

Yes, it can be a very powerful strategy, especially for someone building technology. But the strongest version is not **"ignore humans and society"**; it is:

> First understand the deep, stable rules of reality. Then use that foundation to understand human systems.

There is a hierarchy:

```
Physics
  ↓
Mathematics
  ↓
Computer science / information
  ↓
Biology
  ↓
Psychology
  ↓
Economics / society / organizations
```

The lower layers depend on the higher layers, but they add complexity.

For example:

* Physics: objects follow equations.
* Computer science: information follows algorithms.
* Biology: organisms optimize survival and reproduction.
* Humans: minds optimize under emotions, culture, incentives, history.
* Society: billions of humans create complex systems.

The advantage of learning fundamental rules is that they are more **stable**.

A law like gradient descent, Bayes theorem, conservation of energy, or computational complexity does not change because a country changes its policy or a company changes its culture.

This is why many great engineers and scientists focus deeply on fundamentals:

* Richard Feynman focused on understanding nature from first principles.
* Andrej Karpathy often explains AI by reducing it to simple mathematical mechanisms.
* Jensen Huang built around understanding computation and hardware trends.

However, there is a trap.

Human systems also have "laws", but they are probabilistic rather than deterministic.

For example:

Physics:

```
F = ma
```

Given the same conditions, you get predictable results.

Human systems:

```
Incentive + culture + information + psychology → behavior probability
```

Not deterministic.

A good engineer eventually needs both:

```
Reality model
    +
Human model
    =
Ability to create impact
```

For an AI entrepreneur, the combination is especially important:

Technical layer:

```
Transformer architecture
GPU systems
distributed training
optimization
algorithms
```

Human layer:

```
What problems people pay for
How organizations adopt tools
How users change behavior
How markets move
```

Many brilliant technical people fail not because their technology is weak, but because they misunderstand humans.

A good philosophy might be:

```
Study nature to understand what is true.
Study humans to understand what matters.
```

Spend most of your time on deep fundamentals if that is your comparative advantage. But keep a "human sensor" active. Read history, biographies, psychology, economics—not to become a politician, but to understand the environment where your technology operates.

For someone building AI systems, the optimal ratio is probably something like:

```
70%  math / CS / engineering / science
20%  human psychology / economics / history
10%  current events / social trends
```

The mistake is not choosing physics over sociology.

The mistake is believing humans are noise.

Humans are part of the system you are trying to build for.
