Question: Does top-down mandatory culture in big institutions kill motivation and drive people away?

Answer:

You're onto something well-supported by psychology. When institutions convert tasks into obligations, they trigger a well-documented phenomenon called **motivational crowding out** — external pressure literally destroys internal drive.

**Why "mandatory" kills initiative:**

The core mechanism is **Self-Determination Theory** (Deci & Ryan). Humans have three basic psychological needs:

- **Autonomy** — feeling you chose this
- **Competence** — feeling you're growing
- **Relatedness** — feeling connected to others

Top-down mandates attack autonomy directly. The moment something becomes *required*, the brain reframes it: *"I'm not doing this because I want to — I'm doing this because someone is forcing me."* The intrinsic joy evaporates.

**The overjustification effect** makes it worse — even things you *used to love* become unpleasant once they're mandated. Students who loved reading start hating books the moment there are required reading lists and tests on them.

**Why institutions still do it anyway:**

- It's easier to measure compliance than curiosity
- Large organizations optimize for predictability, not passion
- Managers get rewarded for output metrics, not for how people *feel* about the work

**The dropout pattern makes perfect sense:**

University dropouts, quiet quitters, people leaving big corps — they're not lazy. They're often the ones with the *strongest* self-directed motivation, who simply can't tolerate having it suppressed. The irony is institutions lose their most initiative-driven people first.

The best learning and work environments (think early-stage startups, good open-source communities, Montessori schools) tend to flip the model — they create conditions where people *want* to engage, rather than demanding engagement.
