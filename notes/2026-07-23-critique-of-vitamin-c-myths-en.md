Question: What is Yin Wang’s article “对维生素C的怀疑” saying, and is the reasoning correct?

Answer:

The article argues that **modern people overestimate the importance of vitamin C (VC) and unnecessary supplementation has become a marketing-driven habit**.

The core points:

1. **Vitamin C is necessary, but deficiency is rare**

   Yin Wang starts from the historical fact:

   * Sailors in long ocean voyages developed scurvy because they lacked fresh fruits/vegetables for months.
   * Citrus fruits prevented scurvy because they contained vitamin C.
   * Therefore, vitamin C is essential.

   His argument:

   > The conditions that caused scurvy were extreme. Modern people eating normal food almost never reach that state.

   This part is largely correct.

---

2. **Daily VC requirement may be lower than people think**

   He estimates:

   * Human body stores about 1.5–2g vitamin C.
   * Scurvy appears after roughly 2–3 months of zero intake.
   * Therefore daily loss might be around 10–20mg.

   This is a rough back-of-envelope calculation.

   The official recommendations are higher:

   * US RDA: ~75–90 mg/day for adults.
   * China/Japan recommendations are around this range.

   Why higher?

   Because recommendations are not only "minimum to avoid scurvy". They include:

   * population variation
   * smoking (higher oxidative stress)
   * pregnancy/lactation
   * maintaining blood saturation levels
   * uncertainty margins

   So the statement:

   > "Humans only need 10–20mg"

   is too simplified.

---

3. **High-dose vitamin C (500–2000mg+) probably does not give magical benefits**

   This is where Yin Wang is closer to mainstream science.

   Linus Pauling promoted megadoses:

   * 6–18 grams/day

   Claims included:

   * preventing cancer
   * preventing colds
   * extending life

   Modern clinical evidence does **not support most of these claims**.

   For normal people:

   * Vitamin C supplements do not prevent common colds.
   * They may slightly reduce duration/severity in some cases.
   * They do not have proven anti-cancer effects.

   So:

   > "Vitamin C is not a miracle drug"

   is correct.

---

4. **Synthetic vitamin C vs natural vitamin C**

   He argues:

   > Factory-made VC may be suspicious because it is chemically synthesized.

   This is weak.

   Chemically:

   * Vitamin C molecule = ascorbic acid.
   * Your body does not know whether the molecule came from:

     * orange
     * rose hip
     * glucose fermentation factory

   The molecule is identical.

   Similar example:

   * Synthetic caffeine ≈ coffee caffeine.
   * Synthetic vitamin B12 ≈ biological vitamin B12.

   "Natural" does not automatically mean safer.

---

5. **The "rose hip natural vitamin C" marketing criticism is fair**

   Example:

   A bottle says:

   "Vitamin C with Rose Hips"

   Many consumers misunderstand it as:

   "VC extracted from roses."

   Usually:

   * 500mg ascorbic acid
   * tiny amount of rose hip powder

   The criticism of misleading marketing is reasonable.

---

6. **Vitamin C as food additive**

   He questions why companies add VC to drinks.

   Actually:

   Vitamin C is widely used as:

   * antioxidant
   * oxygen scavenger
   * prevents oxidation of flavors/colors

   Example:

   Apple juice + oxygen → browning.

   Vitamin C reacts with oxygen first.

   It is not mainly added because companies want customers to get healthier.

   This is normal food chemistry.

---

7. **Animals producing vitamin C**

   Correct:

   Most mammals synthesize vitamin C.

   Humans, guinea pigs, some bats, and primates lost functional GULO enzyme.

   The evolutionary explanation:

   Ancient primates had abundant vitamin-C-rich diets (fruit), so losing the pathway was not strongly selected against.

   Yin Wang's skepticism here is not supported by biology.

   We have:

   * DNA evidence
   * comparative genomics
   * biochemical experiments

   showing GULO loss.

---

8. **Where the article becomes unreliable**

   The scientific criticism is mixed with unsupported claims:

   Examples:

   > "Scientists have deceived us many times, so experiments are questionable."

   This is not a scientific argument.

   Science is not:

   "trust scientists"

   but:

   * reproduce experiments
   * compare evidence
   * check mechanisms

   The GULO example is actually one of the better-supported evolutionary biology cases.

My summary:

| Claim                                                | Evaluation           |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- |
| Modern people rarely get scurvy                      | ✅ Correct            |
| Most people don't need vitamin C pills               | ✅ Mostly correct     |
| Mega-dose VC cures cancer/colds                      | ✅ Correct skepticism |
| Natural VC is better than synthetic                  | ❌ Not supported      |
| Food companies add VC mainly suspiciously            | ❌ Misleading         |
| Humans cannot synthesize VC because of GULO mutation | ✅ Strong evidence    |
| Daily requirement is only ~10mg                      | ⚠️ Oversimplified    |

The strongest insight in the article:

> Vitamin C went from a necessary nutrient → marketed as a health miracle.

That criticism is valid.

The weak part:

> Moving from "marketing exaggerates VC" to "therefore VC science is suspicious."

The actual scientific position is more boring:

* Eat normal vegetables/fruits.
* Do not expect vitamin C pills to create superhuman health.
* Supplement if you have increased needs or poor diet.
* 500–2000mg daily is usually unnecessary for healthy people.
