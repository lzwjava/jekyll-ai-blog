Question: Is one reason why 5-year-olds don't get hooked on competitive games like Honor of Kings (王者荣耀) because they're not skilled enough, keep losing, and don't feel satisfied — meaning the game's engagement/indulgence system essentially fails on them?

Answer:

You're onto something genuinely interesting, and you're **mostly right** — but the full picture is richer than just "they lose too much." Let's break it down properly.

---

## Yes — The Core Engagement Loop Breaks Down for Young Children

Modern competitive games like Honor of Kings (or League of Legends, etc.) are built on a **reward/engagement loop**:

> Play → Skill → Win → Reward (dopamine hit) → Want to play again

Reward systems in games serve as the driving force that keeps players engrossed, fuels their motivation to hone their skills, and entices them to return for more gameplay. However, too many punishments (like constant losing) can lead to frustration, while an imbalance in the reward system leads to a decrease in player enjoyment.

For a 5-year-old, this loop collapses at **every stage**:

- They lack the fine motor skills to execute complex controls
- They can't process the strategic complexity fast enough
- They lose constantly, which means **the reward never comes**
- No reward → no dopamine → no desire to return

So yes, the indulgence/engagement system **fails to ignite** for them.

---

## But There's a Deeper Reason: They Can't Even Understand Competition

This is what most people miss. It's not just about losing — young children may **cognitively fail to grasp what competition even means**.

Research on very young children found that those who failed the "false belief" task showed almost no tendency to engage in competitive moves, even when opponents were actively working against them. This strongly suggests these children cannot make sense of competitive behavior at all.

In a competitive game, you must understand that your opponent **wants the opposite of what you want** — that their goal is to defeat you. This requires what psychologists call **"telic perspective taking"** — understanding someone else's objectives, not just their physical actions. Only about 35% of 3- and 4-year-olds correctly identified that an opponent would want something different from what they themselves wanted.

This means a 5-year-old playing Honor of Kings may not even register that enemies are strategically trying to beat them — they just see chaos.

---

## The "Flow State" Problem

Game engagement also depends on what psychologist Csikszentmihalyi called **"flow"** — the sweet spot where challenge matches skill.

A flow state occurs when an optimal match between skill and challenge is found, and it is a highly intrinsically motivating experience. Activities that are too difficult may fail to produce this state.

Honor of Kings is designed for **much higher skill levels** than a 5-year-old possesses. There is no flow — only overwhelming difficulty and punishment. The "indulgence system" (the engagement loop) assumes a baseline competence that 5-year-olds simply don't have yet.

---

## What Games Do Work for 5-Year-Olds?

Among 2–6 year old children, about 50% played educational or prosocial video games, with only 27% playing violent or competitive games. Young children naturally gravitate toward games with:

- **Simple controls** (tap, drag)
- **No direct opponents** (exploration, building)
- **Frequent small rewards** regardless of performance
- **Fantasy and surprise elements** rather than win/loss outcomes

Even young children with limited fine motor skills can express creativity and gain self-esteem in a well-designed game. The key word is "well-designed **for them**."

---

## So, Is the Indulgence System "Failing"?

**Yes and no.** The indulgence system of a competitive MOBA is not broken — it works perfectly for its **intended audience** (teens and adults). It simply was **never designed** for 5-year-olds. It's like saying a treadmill "fails" because a toddler can't run on it.

The real takeaway is: **competitive game engagement loops require a minimum cognitive and motor threshold that children under ~7–8 haven't reached yet**. Below that threshold, losing isn't just frustrating — it's meaningless, because the child can't even fully understand why they lost or what "winning" would feel like.
