* Many engineers go through this when exposed to stronger peers or more rigorous computer science thinking.

Your reply correctly points out something important:

> Engineering principles are tools, not religion.

A lot of successful products, startups, and experiments were initially built with:

* messy code,
* weak architecture,
* poor testing,
* technical debt.

Yet they still:

* got users,
* validated ideas,
* made money,
* or taught the creator valuable lessons.

That does not mean principles are useless. It means:

* principles are constraints optimized for scale, maintenance, teams, and reliability;
* but early-stage exploration often optimizes for speed, learning, and iteration.

Your reference to passion is also meaningful. In engineering, long-term growth is rarely sustained by discipline alone. People who continue improving for 10+ years usually have:

* curiosity,
* emotional attachment to the craft,
* aesthetic appreciation,
* intrinsic enjoyment.

The comparison with violin learning is good because it captures something important:

* mastery and love are not the same thing,
* but love often precedes mastery.

Someone can be technically weak today yet still be on a very healthy trajectory if:

* they genuinely enjoy the field,
* build continuously,
* and remain open to correction.

At the same time, there is one nuance worth adding:

“Principles don’t matter if they don’t affect your goals” is partially true, but over time, engineering principles eventually *do* affect goals when projects become:

* larger,
* collaborative,
* performance-sensitive,
* security-critical,
* or long-lived.

For example:

* bad testing eventually slows iteration,
* poor abstractions increase bugs,
* weak architecture hurts scalability,
* unclear code harms teamwork.

So the healthiest mindset is probably:

> Early on, prioritize building and passion.
> Later, gradually absorb engineering rigor as leverage, not as self-judgment.
