**Question:** What does the future hold for China's loan issuance?

**Answer:**

The outlook is one of **gradual stabilization, not a strong rebound** — and there are both reasons for cautious optimism and genuine structural concerns. Here's the breakdown across three scenarios:

---

**Near-term (2026): Still Weak, Bottoming Out**

As of early 2026, it was the eighth consecutive annual decline in new yuan credit extension, underscoring persistently lower demand from Chinese businesses, and loan growth slowed to 6.5% — the least since at least 1998.

However, there are some early stabilization signals. In Q1 2026, China's GDP grew 5.0% and PPI returned to positive year-on-year growth for the first time in 41 months, reducing the near-term urgency for broad-based monetary easing.

On policy, the PBOC signaled it will continue a moderately loose monetary policy in 2026, using tools such as RRR cuts and interest rate reductions to ensure sufficient liquidity.

---

**Medium-term (2027): Slow Recovery, Mortgage Stabilization**

A gradual recovery in buyer sentiment in wealthier cities and renewed growth in mortgage loan issuance are expected only in 2027. UBS forecasts property sales, new starts and investment to decline by just 0–5% in 2027, with the overall drag on GDP narrowing to much smaller levels compared to 2025–2026.

Outstanding yuan loan growth is projected to trend around 7.0% in 2027 and 6.8% in 2028 — modest but stable.

---

**Structural Ceiling: The Old Model Won't Come Back**

The key issue is that even if loans recover, the **old high-growth credit model is gone**. China's earlier growth paradigm — driven by resource-intensive fixed-asset investment and manufacturing — has reached a turning point.

High real interest rates and deflationary pressures in producer and consumer prices have reduced incentives for new investment, and private sector credit has been particularly weak — household borrowing growth reached an all-time low at just 1.1% year-on-year in November 2025.

Persistently weak sentiment — despite lower borrowing costs — continues to dampen credit demand. Monetary policy should stay accommodative, with scope for further easing, while fiscal efforts work to restore confidence.

---

**Bottom line:** Loan volumes are likely to stabilize in the ¥16–18 trillion range in the near term, not rebound to 2023's peak of ¥22+ trillion. The new normal is slower but hopefully more sustainable credit growth — less driven by real estate and infrastructure, more by consumption, green energy, and technology. The risk is that without a property market floor, loan demand stays depressed for years, as Japan experienced in the 1990s.
