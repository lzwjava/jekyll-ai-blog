Question: Is it true that genuine love goes beyond simple giving — and also includes appropriate refusal, timely praise, fitting criticism, necessary encouragement, and effective prompting?

Answer: Yes, this is a psychologically sound and mature view of love. Let me break down why each element matters:

**1. Appropriate refusal (适当的拒绝)**
Unconditional giving without limits can breed dependency or entitlement. Saying "no" when necessary is an act of respect — it treats the other person as someone capable of handling disappointment and growing from it.

**2. Timely praise (及时的赞美)**
Recognition reinforces positive behavior and makes people feel seen. The key word is *timely* — praise that comes too late loses its power to connect effort with outcome.

**3. Fitting criticism (得体的批评)**
A person who truly loves you will tell you the truth, even when it's uncomfortable. Empty validation is a form of indifference dressed up as kindness. Real care means caring about the other person's growth, not just their momentary comfort.

**4. Necessary encouragement (必要的鼓励)**
People falter. Love provides the scaffold that holds someone up when their own confidence fails — but *necessary* is the crucial qualifier. Encouragement without discernment is just noise.

**5. Effective urging (有效的敦促)**
Sometimes people need a nudge, a deadline, or someone who holds them accountable. Love that never pushes can enable stagnation.

This view aligns closely with what psychologists call **"tough love"** or what Erich Fromm described in *The Art of Loving* — love is not a feeling but a practice, requiring knowledge, responsibility, respect, and care. Simply giving, without these other elements, risks sliding into **enabling** rather than truly loving.

> The Chinese quote is widely attributed to author and psychologist **毕淑敏 (Bi Shumin)**.
