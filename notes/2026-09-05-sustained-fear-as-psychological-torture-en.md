Yes, you're pointing at something genuinely serious — and it has a name in psychology and ethics.

**Question:** Is it a grave moral wrong to deliberately strip someone of their sense of security, keeping them in a constant state of fear and anxiety for years or decades?

**Answer:**

Yes, this is considered one of the most damaging things you can do to a human being. Here's why:

**Psychologically**, chronic insecurity and sustained fear cause measurable, lasting harm:

- The brain's threat-detection system (amygdala) becomes **permanently dysregulated** — the person cannot relax even when safe
- It leads to **Complex PTSD (C-PTSD)**, which is distinct from regular PTSD and harder to treat
- Long-term cortisol flooding damages memory, immune function, cardiovascular health, and cognition
- The person may eventually lose the ability to trust, form relationships, or feel joy — even after the threat is gone

**Morally**, it is a profound violation because:

- Security is a **foundational human need** (Maslow's hierarchy — it sits just above survival)
- Stripping it deliberately is a form of **psychological torture**, recognized under international human rights law
- It destroys **autonomy** — a person in constant fear cannot think freely, plan, or live authentically
- It is often used as a **control mechanism** — abusers, authoritarian regimes, and cults all use it intentionally

**Your instinct is correct** — yes, people *will* break. Sustained, inescapable fear over years produces:

- Paranoia and hypervigilance that looks like "madness"
- Learned helplessness
- Dissociation
- Sometimes, collapse of personality entirely

The cruelty is amplified precisely *because* it leaves no visible wound, making it easy to deny or dismiss.

This is why solitary confinement, housing instability as punishment, and chronic emotional abuse are all considered forms of torture by mental health and human rights communities.
