Question: Why do people say startups and school/degree can't be done in parallel — and why do founders drop out?

---

Answer:

**The core tension: two things that each demand 100%**

A degree has a fixed schedule — exams, deadlines, lectures. A startup has a *reactive* schedule — a critical user calls at 2am, an investor wants a meeting tomorrow, your server is down. You can't pause school for a product launch, and you can't pause a startup for finals week. They don't share time gracefully because neither tolerates being second priority.

---

**Why parallelism breaks down specifically**

| School | Startup |
| --- | --- |
| Fixed curriculum | Constantly pivoting |
| Semester-long thinking | Week-by-week survival |
| Predictable schedule | Completely reactive |
| Graded on thoroughness | Rewarded for speed |
| Thinking mode | Doing mode |

Your cognitive load is also zero-sum. Deep focus on product architecture and deep focus on a thesis are both "full brain" — context-switching between them erodes both.

---

**Why people actually drop out (not romance — math)**

When a startup starts *working* — getting users, revenue, or investor interest — staying in school becomes an enormous **opportunity cost**. The reasoning is:

1. **Window risk** — Markets move fast. A competitor can close your window while you're sitting in class. The degree will still exist in 2 years; the opportunity may not.

2. **Investor signal** — VCs fund full-time founders. A part-time founder signals you don't believe in it enough to bet on it yourself.

3. **Compounding momentum** — Early traction (first 100 users, first revenue) compounds if you push it. Breaking rhythm to study breaks that compounding.

4. **The degree has a backup path; the startup doesn't** — You can always go back to school. You usually can't go back to 2019 when that market gap existed.

---

**The survivorship bias warning**

The famous dropouts — Gates, Zuckerberg, Jobs, Dell, Zuckerberg — all left when their company was *already working*. They didn't drop out to start; they dropped out because staying had become the risky choice. Most people who drop out for a startup without traction simply… don't have a degree and also don't have a company.

---

**The honest summary**

Dropout isn't a strategy — it's a *symptom*. When a startup genuinely demands your full attention and is showing real signs of life, school starts to feel like the distraction. The dropout is the moment you decide the expected value of the startup exceeds the option value of the degree.
