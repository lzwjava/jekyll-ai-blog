**Question:** Does using years of service (tenure) as a key factor in promotion decisions make sense, given that people are generally not very reliable or loyal?

**Answer:**

Yes, seniority-based promotion is genuinely common and has real logic behind it — but it's also increasingly challenged. Here's a balanced take:

**Why it makes sense:**

- **Incentivizes loyalty** — If people tend to job-hop, rewarding those who stay is a rational response. Tenure becomes a signal that someone has *chosen* to commit, which has real organizational value.
- **Reduces favoritism accusations** — Objective criteria (years served) are harder to dispute than subjective performance ratings, which can be biased or political.
- **Institutional knowledge** — Longer-tenured employees often carry deep context about systems, clients, and culture that's genuinely hard to replace.
- **Stability in low-trust environments** — In industries or cultures where interpersonal trust is hard to establish, a rule-based system feels fairer.

**Where it breaks down:**

- Tenure ≠ competence. Someone can stay 10 years while contributing minimally.
- It can **demotivate high performers** who see less capable colleagues promoted ahead of them purely by waiting.
- In fast-moving fields (tech, startups), it can make organizations dangerously slow to promote the right people.

**The industry reality:**

- **Traditional industries** (banking, government, Japanese corporations — 年功序列) still heavily use it.
- **Modern tech/startups** lean toward merit-based, but often have hidden tenure biases in practice anyway.
- Most mature companies use a **hybrid**: tenure as a *floor* (you need X years to be *eligible*), but performance as the *ceiling* (you still have to earn it).

**My honest take:** The user's underlying instinct is pragmatically sound — rewarding demonstrated commitment over promised potential is rational. The problem isn't using tenure, it's using it *exclusively*. The best systems use tenure as a necessary-but-not-sufficient condition.
