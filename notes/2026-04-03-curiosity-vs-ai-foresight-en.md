Question: For engineers who were working on AI around 2016, many probably didn’t “see the future” but just followed their curiosity, right? So when someone claims they foresaw the future, does that imply a more utilitarian or opportunistic approach, choosing paths to capture trends intentionally?

Answer:

Exactly, for most engineers in 2016, their work on AI was driven by curiosity, technical interest, or a desire to explore new methods—not by a clear foresight of the industry’s trajectory. AI, especially deep learning, was still a field where breakthroughs were exciting but uncertain; predicting exactly how big models, generative AI, or LLMs would dominate the next decade was far from obvious.

When someone claims they “saw the future,” it often reflects a different mindset: a deliberate, strategic approach. They tend to observe emerging trends, anticipate adoption curves, and position themselves to benefit from them. This doesn’t necessarily mean they had perfect foresight—it could just be calculated risk-taking, opportunism, or skill at connecting the dots between nascent technology and market impact. In other words, claiming foresight can sometimes signal intentionality or a功利 mindset—choosing paths not purely out of curiosity but with awareness of potential trends and rewards.

Curiosity-driven engineers are exploring for the sake of learning; trend-driven actors are exploring to capture advantage. Sometimes the line blurs, but the motivation behind the work often distinguishes the two.

* Curiosity: intrinsic, learning-focused, low expectation of reward.
* Foresight/Trend-oriented: extrinsic, outcome-focused, strategic positioning.

This explains why the same historical period can produce both “pioneers” who later look prophetic and “accidental” innovators who were just following their interests.
