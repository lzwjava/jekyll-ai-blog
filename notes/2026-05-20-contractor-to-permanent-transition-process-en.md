**Question:** In large corporations, when a contractor wants to interview for a permanent role at the client company, how does the process of getting vendor/agency endorsement work? What does that endorsement look like in format?

**Answer:**

This is a staffing/contracting industry process question. Here's how it actually works:

## The Structural Reality

When you're a contractor, you're employed by a **vendor/staffing agency**, placed at a **client** (the bank, corp, etc.). The client can't directly poach you without consequences — most vendor-client contracts have **anti-solicitation / right-to-hire clauses**.

There are two paths:

---

## Path 1: Conversion Fee (No Endorsement Needed)

The client pays the vendor a **conversion fee** (typically 10–25% of your first-year salary) to hire you directly. No endorsement letter needed — it's purely commercial. You just interview, the client pays the fee, done.

---

## Path 2: Endorsement / Release Letter (Fee Waived or Reduced)

If the vendor wants to maintain the relationship with the client, they may **voluntarily release you** and waive/reduce the conversion fee. This is where an endorsement or release letter comes in.

### What It Actually Is

It's not really an "endorsement" in the recommendation-letter sense. It's a **contractual release / consent letter** from the vendor to the client, saying:

> "We consent to \\[Client\\] hiring \\[Your Name\\] as a permanent employee and waive our right-to-hire fee / non-solicitation claim."

### Typical Format

```
[Vendor Company Letterhead]
Date: XX/XX/XXXX

To: [Client HR / Hiring Manager Name]
    [Client Company Name]

Subject: Release / Consent to Hire – [Your Full Name]

Dear [Name],

This letter confirms that [Vendor Company] consents to the direct 
employment of [Your Name] (currently engaged as a contractor under 
Agreement #XXXX dated XX/XX/XXXX) by [Client Company] in a permanent 
capacity, effective [Date or "upon mutual agreement"].

[Vendor Company] hereby waives any right-to-hire fee and 
non-solicitation claim arising from the above-referenced contract 
with respect to this individual.

We appreciate our ongoing partnership with [Client Company] and wish 
[Your Name] success in their new role.

Sincerely,
[Account Manager / Director Name]
[Vendor Company]
[Contact Details]
```

---

## How to Actually Get This

The vendor won't volunteer it. You have to manage this politically:

**1. Don't go behind their back first.** If the client tips off the vendor before you're ready, you lose leverage.

**2. Talk to your vendor account manager directly.** Frame it as: *"I've been approached about a perm role — I wanted to be transparent with you before anything moves forward."* This gives them face.

**3. The vendor's incentive to release you:**
- Maintaining the client relationship (they don't want a legal dispute with a big client)
- You might refer other contractors to them
- They may negotiate a **reduced fee** rather than full waiver

**4. If vendor refuses:** The client either pays the full conversion fee, or you wait out the **cooling-off period** (usually 6–12 months after your contract ends) specified in the contract before joining without a fee.

---

## At Banks Specifically (Your Context)

Global banks like yours typically have **Preferred Supplier Lists (PSL)**. The vendor is highly motivated to not burn the relationship over one contractor. In practice, most PSL vendors will issue the release letter as a goodwill gesture, especially if:

- You've been there long (1+ year) — they've already made margin
- The account manager likes you
- The bank asks as a soft favor vs. a legal demand

The **bank's HR/procurement team** usually handles the paperwork, not you — you just need to confirm the vendor has sent it before you sign the offer letter.
