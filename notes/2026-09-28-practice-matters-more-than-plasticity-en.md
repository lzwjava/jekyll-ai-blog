Question: Does the brain get "harder to wire" as people age, and does that mean people who start something like LeetCode/programming at 15 have a lasting advantage?

Answer: There are two separate things bundled in this question, and they point in slightly different directions.

**1. Does the brain lose plasticity with age?**
Yes, but less dramatically than the old "you can't teach an old dog new tricks" idea suggests. It used to be assumed that neuroplasticity peaks young and then falls off sharply brain plasticity was assumed to reach its peak during young age and then gradually decline with advancing age. But newer imaging and longitudinal research has walked that back: despite age-related changes, older adults maintain the capacity for skill improvement through training, and the predominant view that neuroplasticity happens early in life and is largely reduced in adulthood has been challenged by contemporary research showing experience- and practice-dependent plasticity also in the adult human brain. Some strategic/processing-speed measures do start slipping earlier than people expect — strategic memory begins to decline at age 20 — but that's a narrow measure, not a general "brain stops learning" cliff. Skill learning specifically remains available well into older adulthood: a 2015 study found older people who learned high-challenge skills like photography and quilting showed improvements in memory, attention, and semantic processing, with some benefits lasting at least a year.

**2. So do 15-year-old LeetCode grinders have a real, lasting edge?**
Yes — but the reason isn't "their brain is more wire-able," it's more mundane: they simply get more total reps and more years compounding. A few real factors:

- **Time horizon, not hardware.** A 15-year-old with 5 years of runway before a CS internship/job accumulates way more deliberate practice than someone starting at 25. Compounding practice, not some biological plasticity cliff, is doing most of the work.
- **Fewer competing life obligations.** Teenagers often have more free, low-stakes hours to grind than adults juggling jobs/rent/family — that's an opportunity advantage, not a neurological one.
- **Fluid pattern-recognition speed does trend slightly better in youth** for some tasks (working memory, raw processing speed peak in the late teens/20s for many people), which can make raw "solve this novel puzzle fast" tasks feel easier — but this is a small effect compared to accumulated practice, and it fades in relevance the more a skill depends on pattern-matching and experience (which most of programming/algorithms does).
- **No hard "critical period" for something like coding/algorithms**, unlike, say, phoneme acquisition for a native accent in language learning, which does have stronger age-sensitive windows. LeetCode-style skills are learned skills, not innate-wiring windows, so adults can absolutely catch up and even surpass early starters with consistent practice — studies show the brain retains plasticity well into later life, and neuroplasticity enables older adults to learn new skills effectively.

**Bottom line:** the "great advantage" of the 15-year-old grinder is mostly accumulated hours + a longer runway, not a fundamentally more plastic brain. An adult starting later loses some raw processing-speed edge and free time, but keeps essentially full capacity to learn, and can close most or all of the gap with consistent deliberate practice.
