Question: What do you think about the author’s idea that focusing on “200+ juggling counts” is low-level repetition and that abandoning quantitative goals improves skill and enjoyment?

Answer:

This view is actually quite solid—and it aligns with both skill acquisition theory and real-world practice across many domains.

---

### 1. He is pointing at a real distinction: **quantity vs. quality of practice**

In motor skills like juggling (or football freestyle), simply repeating the same movement many times does **not automatically lead to higher-level ability**.

Research on juggling shows that expert performance depends heavily on **precision, prediction, and control of variables** (angle, timing, trajectory), not just repetition count ([Ars Technica][1]).

That means:

* Doing 200 easy, stable repetitions
* ≠ learning a new coordination pattern
* ≠ improving adaptability or technique

So his phrase “low-level repetition” is quite accurate:
You can get very good at *one narrow pattern* while not progressing overall.

---

### 2. This matches the concept of **“deliberate practice” vs. “mechanical repetition”**

In learning theory:

* **Mechanical repetition** → reinforces what you already can do
* **Deliberate practice** → pushes you slightly beyond current ability

If your “200 juggles”:

* are comfortable
* don’t introduce variation
* don’t challenge control

then they mostly strengthen habit, not skill.

This is why beginners often plateau:

> They optimize stability, not growth.

---

### 3. His criticism of **quantitative metrics** is also valid—but incomplete

He’s right that metrics like “number of juggles” can become:

* ego-driven
* anxiety-inducing
* disconnected from real improvement

And psychologically:

* chasing numbers → pressure ↑
* pressure ↑ → fluidity ↓
* fluidity ↓ → performance ↓

So when he says “after letting go, I felt happier and improved,” that’s believable.

However, there is a nuance:

👉 **Quantification itself is not the problem—misusing it is.**

Good metrics:

* track progress *relative to skill goals*
* support feedback loops

Bad metrics:

* become the goal itself

So:

* “200 touches” as *training feedback* → useful
* “must hit 200 or I failed” → harmful

---

### 4. “Free style vs. metrics” — a deeper tension

His idea that counting conflicts with “freestyle” reflects a real philosophy:

* **Freestyle mindset** → exploration, creativity, adaptability
* **Metric mindset** → optimization, repeatability, control

Both are valuable, but they serve different stages:

| Stage        | What matters more        |
| ------------ | ------------------------ |
| Beginner     | repetition (stability)   |
| Intermediate | variation (skill growth) |
| Advanced     | expression (freestyle)   |

He is likely transitioning from **intermediate → advanced**,
where creativity and feel become more important than raw counts.

---

### 5. The most important insight in his statement

This sentence is the core:

> “If it doesn’t lead to new development, more repetition has no meaning.”

That’s basically a principle you can generalize to everything:

* coding
* language learning
* AI research
* career growth

**Progress = exposure to new structure, not just more volume**

---

### 6. My honest take (balanced)

* ✔ His critique is **correct and valuable**
* ✔ Especially for people stuck in “grinding without growth”
* ❗ But it can be misinterpreted if taken too far

A more complete version would be:

> Repetition builds foundation, but only variation creates advancement.

If someone abandons metrics too early:

* they may lose discipline
* progress becomes vague

If someone clings to metrics too long:

* they stagnate

---

### 7. Practical takeaway (what actually works)

Instead of:

* “I must reach 200”

Better approach:

* mix **3 modes of practice**:

1. **Stability mode**
   → repeat to build control
2. **Exploration mode**
   → try variations, new tricks
3. **Expression mode**
   → freestyle, no pressure

That combination avoids both extremes.

---

### Final thought

What he experienced—

> “less pressure → more enjoyment → better performance”

—is not accidental.

In many high-skill domains,
the moment you stop chasing the metric directly,
you finally improve the underlying system that produces it.

That’s a subtle but powerful shift.
