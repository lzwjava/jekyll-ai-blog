Question: Does Hong Kong's job market discriminate against middle-aged or older people?

Answer:

Yes, age discrimination is a real and widely reported problem in Hong Kong, particularly for workers aged 50 and above.

**Evidence from surveys**

- The Equal Opportunities Commission (EOC) study found that 29% of job seekers aged 45 or older felt they had been discriminated against, and that blue-collar and elementary workers faced more severe age discrimination.
- The EOC said workers aged 50 and above report being particularly susceptible to age-discriminatory treatment. More than two-thirds of employed respondents agreed that workers in their 50s are vulnerable.
- A separate EOC-commissioned survey found that 35% of respondents had experienced some form of age discrimination at work in the past five years. The most common forms were lower pay, being passed over for promotion and being let go after restructuring.
- A survey by the Association of the Retired Elderly found that 34% of respondents over 50 said they were rejected for not meeting job requirements because of their age.

**Why it happens**

- Negative stereotypes about older workers' productivity and adaptability persist. The EOC notes that many mature workers face stereotypes that create barriers to re-employment, and age-friendly initiatives remain rare.
- Cost is another factor. Employers may prefer younger candidates to reduce salary and medical insurance costs. The government's Employment Programme for the Middle-aged, which subsidises employers who hire jobseekers aged 40 or above, suggests the market is not friendly to older jobseekers.

**Legal protection**

- Hong Kong has no law that explicitly prohibits age discrimination in employment, unlike its ordinances on sex, disability, family status and race. The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights has voiced concern about the lack of explicit legislation.
- The Labour Department only offers voluntary guidelines, for example advising employers to avoid referring to age in selection criteria and to avoid interview questions that could lead to age discrimination.
- Some employers deny that it is discrimination. One catering employer quoted in the EOC report called it a skills mismatch rather than age discrimination.

**Caveats**

- Much of the research I found is a few years old. I could not confirm whether any age-discrimination legislation has been introduced recently, so check the EOC's website for the latest position.
- Labour shortages and an ageing population are increasing demand for mature workers, and the pressure for reform is growing.

If you're a jobseeker, the Labour Department's Employment Programme for the Middle-aged (ages 40+) and the EOC's enquiry service are practical places to start. I can say more about either if you like.
