## 2. The math behind earning ¥1 million from trading

Suppose you want to generate ¥1,000,000 in annual profit before taxes and expenses.

The capital required depends on your realized net return, not the return you hope to achieve.

| Annual net return | Capital needed for ¥1M profit |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 5%                | ¥20 million                   |
| 10%               | ¥10 million                   |
| 20%               | ¥5 million                    |
| 30%               | ¥3.33 million                 |

These are arithmetic scenarios, not predictions or recommended return targets. Returns are uncertain, and drawdowns can be substantial.

The formula is simple:

\\[ \text{Required capital}=\frac{\text{Target profit}}{\text{Net annual return}} \\]

If you start with ¥100,000, earning ¥1 million in profit requires a 1,000% return in one year. That is not a sensible financial plan to rely on.

A quant job changes the equation: instead of risking your own capital to produce ¥1 million, you earn compensation for your engineering or research skills.

## Your first quant research project

A 6–8 week experiment, not a promise of profitability

1. Choose one market. Start with liquid ETFs or futures, depending on your data access and trading permissions.
2. Build a reproducible data pipeline. Handle timestamps, missing values, corporate actions where applicable, and point-in-time data correctly.
3. Establish simple baselines. Buy-and-hold, momentum, and mean-reversion strategies before trying deep learning.
4. Build a walk-forward backtester. Use chronological train/validation/test splits, realistic transaction costs, slippage, and risk limits.
5. Test ML models. Compare linear regression, gradient-boosted trees, and a small neural network. Test whether the added complexity improves genuinely unseen results.
6. Publish the evidence. Report net returns, Sharpe ratio, maximum drawdown, turnover, sensitivity to costs, and performance across market regimes.

Why this matters: quantitative strategies can look profitable in historical tests because of overfitting, look-ahead bias, and unrealistic trading-cost assumptions.&#x20;
