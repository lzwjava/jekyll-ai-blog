Question: Are people’s lives fundamentally different—some winning medals or finding love, while others face health, family, or other problems?

Answer: **Yes. Very much so.**

People don't start from the same position, and they don't receive the same combination of luck, ability, health, family, opportunities, relationships, and setbacks.

You can think of a life as a high-dimensional state:

[
L = (health,\ family,\ love,\ money,\ career,\ ability,\ friends,\ location,\ luck,\ meaning,\ldots)
]

Two people can have completely different vectors.

For example:

* Someone wins an Olympic gold medal but has a terrible marriage.
* Someone is extremely wealthy but feels lonely.
* Someone has an ordinary career but has a wonderful partner and children.
* Someone has extraordinary technical ability but struggles with health.
* Someone loses their parents early but builds a great community around themselves.
* Someone appears extremely successful but is privately suffering.
* Someone gets unusually lucky at exactly the right moment and their entire trajectory changes.

And **there is no single scoreboard that correctly ranks all of these lives**.

That's an important realization because we often compare one dimension of our life against someone else's strongest dimension:

> "He has a gold medal; I don't."

> "She found love; I haven't."

> "He's rich; I'm not."

> "They're healthy; I'm not."

But you're comparing **one coordinate of your life vector against another person's coordinate**, while ignoring everything else.

Even more fundamentally, life isn't a fair optimization problem. There is substantial randomness:

[
\text{outcome} =
f(\text{choices},\text{ability},\text{environment},\text{luck},\text{time})
]

You control only part of it.

The useful response isn't to pretend everyone has the same life. It's almost the opposite:

**Accept that the distributions are radically different, then focus on improving the dimensions you can actually influence.**

And sometimes a person's greatest achievement isn't something visible like a medal or a huge company. It can simply be:

> "Given the hand I was dealt, I built a life I genuinely wanted."

That's a meaningful kind of winning too.
