Question: **How can I regret less?**

Answer:

You probably don't want to eliminate regret. You want to make **regret useful without letting it consume your present**.

A useful model is:

> **Regret = comparing reality with an imagined better timeline.**

The problem is that the imagined timeline cheats. It assumes you would have known then what you know now.

### 1. Separate "bad outcome" from "bad decision"

This is huge.

You can make a rational decision and still get a bad outcome.

For example:

```text
decision D
   ↓
information available at the time
   ↓
reasonable judgment
   ↓
uncertain outcome
```

Don't evaluate the decision using information that only became available afterward.

Ask:

> **"Given what I knew at that moment, was this a reasonable bet?"**

If yes, you don't owe your past self punishment.

---

### 2. Convert regret into information

For every major regret, write only three things:

```text
What happened?
What did I fail to notice?
What rule will I use next time?
```

Example:

```text
What happened?
I stayed too long in something that wasn't progressing.

What did I fail to notice?
I kept waiting for future evidence instead of acting on repeated present evidence.

New rule:
If the same problem persists for 3 months despite active effort,
re-evaluate the entire direction.
```

Now the regret has **paid rent**.

It gave you a rule.

Once you've extracted the lesson, repeatedly replaying the event provides almost no additional information.

---

### 3. Think in bets, not irreversible life decisions

A lot of regret comes from thinking:

> "I chose the wrong path."

Instead:

> "I ran experiment X for 12 months and learned Y."

Life is much closer to an optimization loop:

```python
while alive:
    observe()
    update_beliefs()
    choose_next_bet()
    act()
```

You don't need to find the perfect path.

You need to maintain a good **rate of correction**.

That's especially powerful for someone building things: your advantage isn't predicting the future perfectly; it's being able to change direction cheaply.

---

### 4. Optimize for future optionality

When two choices have similar expected value, prefer the one that keeps more doors open.

For example:

```text
A → +10, but locks you into one direction

B → +7, but gives you:
    skills
    money
    network
    reputation
    freedom
    information
```

B can actually be the better choice.

Optionality dramatically reduces future regret because you aren't betting your entire future on one prediction.

---

### 5. Don't confuse "I wish I had started earlier" with "it's too late"

This is one of the nastiest forms of regret.

You look backward:

```text
2020 ───── 2023 ───── 2026 ───── 2030
   "I should have started."
```

But from the perspective of 2030, **2026 will also look early**.

The correct question isn't:

> "Why didn't I start in 2020?"

It's:

> **"What would make 2030-me grateful that 2026-me started now?"**

That question turns regret into action.

---

### 6. Make peace with paths you didn't take

Every serious life choice creates counterfactuals.

If you choose startup:

```text
"What if I had stayed employed?"
```

If you choose employment:

```text
"What if I had started a company?"
```

If you move:

```text
"What if I stayed?"
```

There is no choice that eliminates this.

You're not choosing between:

```text
good life vs bad life
```

You're choosing between:

```text
one possible life vs another possible life
```

You will mourn some unlived lives.

That's normal.

You don't need to prove that your chosen path was objectively optimal. You need to **live it well enough that it becomes worth having chosen**.

---

### 7. Reduce the surface area for future regret

There are a few things that tend to create enormous regret:

* not spending enough time with people you love
* repeatedly ignoring obvious health problems
* never attempting something important because of fear
* living according to someone else's expectations
* staying indefinitely in situations you know are wrong
* sacrificing all present life for a hypothetical future

So a good rule is:

> **Take calculated risks on things that matter, but don't take unnecessary risks with things you can't recover.**

Money can often be rebuilt.

Career paths can change.

Skills can be learned.

But some time, relationships, and experiences are genuinely non-renewable.

---

### 8. Use a "regret budget"

You don't need zero regret.

Think:

```text
Expected regret
= missed opportunities
+ bad decisions
+ unavoidable losses
```

You can choose which category to tolerate.

For example:

> "I'd rather occasionally fail from trying than spend my life wondering what would have happened."

That's a coherent regret strategy.

---

### 9. The deepest trick: stop demanding that your past self be you

Your 20-year-old self didn't have your current knowledge.

Your 25-year-old self didn't have your current knowledge.

Your 30-year-old self won't have your future knowledge.

So judging your past self with your present brain is fundamentally unfair.

Your job isn't to have made perfect decisions.

It's to make **today's decision better because yesterday happened**.

That is probably the healthiest relationship with regret:

```text
past
 ↓
lesson
 ↓
better model
 ↓
better decision
 ↓
better future
```

Not:

```text
past
 ↓
rumination
 ↓
self-punishment
 ↓
lost present
```

**A good life isn't one with no regrets. It's one where your regrets gradually become wisdom instead of becoming a second source of suffering.**
