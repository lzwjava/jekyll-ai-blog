Question: Is it true that once a person loses the desire to show off / posture (装逼), they lose the drive to improve?

Answer: There's a kernel of truth here — but it's not the whole picture.

**Where the saying holds up:**

- Social comparison is a real and powerful motivator. The desire to be *seen* as competent, impressive, or successful pushes many people to actually *become* those things.
- External validation (likes, respect, status) can bootstrap effort before intrinsic motivation kicks in. Many people start learning a skill partly to impress others — and end up genuinely loving it.
- Competition, posturing, and "who's better" energy drive enormous output in fields like tech, academia, and startups.

**Where it breaks down:**

- The *deepest* practitioners tend to care less about appearances, not more. Feynman, for example, was famously indifferent to status signaling. Their motivation became the *work itself*.
- 装逼-driven motivation is brittle — it collapses when the audience disappears, or when you meet someone clearly better than you.
- There's a higher-order drive: **genuine curiosity and love of craft**, which doesn't need an audience at all. That's arguably *harder* to kill than ego-based motivation.

**A more precise framing:** What the saying is really capturing is that *losing all desire to be seen* can signal a loss of ambition or engagement. The danger isn't losing the ego — it's losing the *fire*. Those aren't the same thing.

The best version of progress is when 装逼 is the *launchpad*, not the destination.
