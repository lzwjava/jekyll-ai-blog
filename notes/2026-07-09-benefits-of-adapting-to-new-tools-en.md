Yes. I think your reasoning is largely sound, but I'd frame it slightly differently.

A vertical mouse is almost like a tiny experiment in human adaptability.

1. **The engineers probably discovered something real.**

   * It isn't just "making a weird mouse."
   * They spent years studying wrist pronation, forearm rotation, muscle activation, and repetitive strain injuries (RSI).
   * A vertical grip reduces forearm pronation, which can reduce stress for many people (though not everyone).

2. **Trying new tools expands your "movement vocabulary."**

   * Even if you eventually switch back.
   * Your brain learns another motor pattern.
   * Similar to learning Colemak, split keyboards, Vim, or standing desks.
   * The benefit isn't only the tool itself—it's becoming someone who adapts quickly.

3. **The human brain is surprisingly adaptable.**
   You already mentioned entrepreneurship, debt, war, and difficult life situations.

   Compared with those:

   * learning a vertical mouse,
   * changing keyboard layouts,
   * switching operating systems,

   are tiny adaptations.

   Neuroplasticity is remarkable. What feels "impossible" on day one often feels completely natural after enough repetitions.

4. **The first adaptation attempt doesn't predict the second.**
   Your own experience is a good example:

   * First few times: "This feels terrible."
   * Put it away.
   * Months later, you're deep into AI work, using the mouse for hours every day.
   * Suddenly it feels fine.

   That doesn't necessarily mean your hand changed—it could also mean:

   * you were more motivated,
   * you used it continuously instead of intermittently,
   * your motor system had already retained part of the previous learning.

5. **There's little downside if the cost is small.**
   If a new tool costs:

   * $30–100,
   * a few days of adaptation,

   and there's even a moderate chance it reduces fatigue or increases comfort over thousands of hours of work, it's often a worthwhile experiment.

The key is not to assume that "new" always means "better." Many ergonomic products help some people but not others. The value comes from running a low-cost experiment, giving yourself enough continuous time to adapt (often 1–2 weeks rather than a few minutes), and then evaluating based on your own comfort and productivity.

So yes—your broader observation is reasonable:

> Humans can adapt to extraordinarily difficult environments. A different mouse is a comparatively small adaptation. If the potential upside is better comfort over thousands of hours of AI work, it's usually worth giving it a fair trial rather than abandoning it after the first awkward day.
