Question: What is **无线电磁波** (radio electromagnetic waves)?

Answer:

**Radio waves are electromagnetic waves used to transmit information wirelessly.**

At the physics level, an electromagnetic wave is a coupled oscillation of:

* an **electric field** \\(E\\)
* a **magnetic field** \\(B\\)

These fields oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction the wave travels:

```text
        E field ↑↓
                |
                |
wave → → → → → → →
                |
        B field ⊙⊗
```

### 1. Why "radio"?

"Radio" doesn't mean a special kind of physics. It refers to a **range of electromagnetic frequencies** commonly used for wireless communication.

The electromagnetic spectrum looks roughly like:

```text
low frequency                                  high frequency
     ↓                                               ↓

radio ─── microwave ─── infrared ─── visible ─── UV ─── X-ray ─── gamma
```

Radio waves have relatively **low frequency / long wavelength** compared with visible light.

The fundamental relationship is:

$$
c = f\lambda
$$

where:

* \\(c \approx 3\times10^8\\) m/s — speed of electromagnetic waves in vacuum
* \\(f\\) — frequency
* \\(\lambda\\) — wavelength

For example, a 100 MHz radio wave has:

$$
\lambda = \frac{3\times10^8}{100\times10^6}
=3\text{ m}
$$

So its wavelength is about **3 meters**.

### 2. How does a transmitter create it?

This is the important engineering picture.

Suppose you connect an antenna to an alternating electrical signal:

```text
oscillator
   │
   │  100 MHz AC
   ▼
 antenna
   ~~~
  ↗ ↑ ↖
 electromagnetic
      wave
```

Electrons in the antenna accelerate back and forth.

Accelerating charges produce changing electromagnetic fields.

Those changing fields propagate outward as an electromagnetic wave.

So conceptually:

$$
\text{electrical oscillation}
\rightarrow
\text{accelerating charges}
\rightarrow
\text{changing }E,B
\rightarrow
\text{electromagnetic wave}
$$

### 3. How does it carry data?

The radio wave itself is just a physical carrier. We modify some property of it to encode information.

For example, with a carrier:

$$
s(t)=A\cos(2\pi ft)
$$

we can vary:

* **Amplitude** → AM
* **Frequency** → FM
* **Phase** → PM
* combinations of amplitude + phase → QAM

Modern Wi-Fi, 5G, etc. use sophisticated modulation schemes.

For example, conceptually:

```text
data
  ↓
modulator
  ↓
radio-frequency signal
  ↓
antenna
  ↓
~~~~~~~~~~~~ electromagnetic wave ~~~~~~~~~~~~
  ↓
antenna
  ↓
receiver
  ↓
demodulator
  ↓
data
```

### 4. Radio waves are not fundamentally different from light

This is a beautiful point.

**Radio waves and visible light are both electromagnetic waves.**

The difference is primarily their **frequency/wavelength**.

For example:

```text
Wi-Fi        ~ GHz
microwave    ~ GHz
infrared     ~ 10^12 Hz
visible      ~ 4–7.5 × 10^14 Hz
UV           higher
X-ray        much higher
```

So you can think of **light as extremely high-frequency electromagnetic radiation**, while radio is the lower-frequency part of the spectrum.

In vacuum, both propagate at:

$$
c\approx300,000\text{ km/s}
$$

The same Maxwell equations describe them.

### 5. Why can radio go through walls?

Because electromagnetic waves interact differently with different materials depending on their **frequency, wavelength, conductivity, thickness, and structure**.

A wall might attenuate a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi signal but still allow some of it through.

Metal is much more effective at blocking/reflection because its free electrons respond strongly to the electromagnetic field.

That's why an elevator can become a terrible Wi-Fi environment.

**The core idea:**

> **Radio = electromagnetic radiation at radio frequencies, generated by accelerating charges and used as a carrier for wireless information.**

And this connects directly to your previous question about **optical fiber**: fiber also uses electromagnetic waves—the difference is that the information is carried by **much higher-frequency optical light**, guided inside glass rather than radiated freely through space.
