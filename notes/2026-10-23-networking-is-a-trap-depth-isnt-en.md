**Question:** Is investing heavily in human relationships a trap for most people, except those in sales, marketing, or founder roles?

**Answer:**

There's a real insight here — but the claim is a bit too broad to hold fully.

**Where you're right:**
The self-help and productivity space wildly overgeneralizes "networking is everything." For a deep technical contributor — a solo researcher, a quant, an individual engineer — spending excessive time on social maintenance at the expense of skill-building is genuinely a poor trade. Your leverage comes from *capability*, not connections. In that sense, over-investing in broad relationship networks is a trap.

**Where it gets more nuanced:**

- **Most jobs aren't pure solo work.** Even engineers ship through teams. Getting resources, unblocking projects, promotions — these run on internal relationships whether you call it that or not.
- **There's a difference between types of relationships.** Broad *networking* (LinkedIn, conferences, "building your brand") is mostly noise for non-sales roles. But *working relationships* — trust with your team, rapport with your manager, credibility with a few key people — have outsized returns in almost any career path.
- **Personal relationships are a separate category entirely.** Family, close friendships — the research on life satisfaction and health is overwhelming here. That's not a trap; that's the actual game.
- **Career inflection points are relationship-dependent.** The higher you go (even in technical tracks), the more things like sponsorship, internal advocacy, and reputation networks matter. People who neglect this entirely often stall.

**The sharper version of your point:**
Most people should *ruthlessly deprioritize shallow, performative relationship-building* — the kind that wastes hours but produces no trust or depth. That *is* a trap. But a small number of deep, high-trust relationships pays off for almost everyone.

The real skill is knowing which relationships are load-bearing and which are just social noise.
