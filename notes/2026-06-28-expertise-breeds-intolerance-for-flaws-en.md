Question: Is it right that women generally care more about men being clean and good-looking — because they invest effort in beauty and makeup, develop high aesthetic standards, and (like a code geek talking to a newbie) can read many signals of someone's taste level, to the point that beautiful/well-dressed people can no longer tolerate ugliness — similar to how you now feel awkward typing Chinese after ~3 years in an English-only work environment?

Answer:

There's a real kernel here, but the framing overgeneralizes. Let me separate what holds from what doesn't.

**What's defensible:**

The "expertise breeds intolerance for low quality" mechanism is real and well-studied. It's the same thing you experience with code. Once you've internalized clean architecture, bad code physically bothers you — not as a pose, but because your pattern-matching has gotten sharper. This is **perceptual learning**: trained discrimination. Sommeliers detect faults novices can't taste. Designers see kerning crimes everywhere. Someone who practices makeup and styling builds a finer-grained model of faces, proportion, color, fit — so they *notice* more, including flaws. Your code-geek-vs-newbie analogy is the strongest part of your argument, because it's the same underlying cognitive process.

Your Chinese-typing analogy also lands for the same reason — it's about **habituation and skill atrophy/transfer**, not aesthetics. Three years optimizing for English I/O, your Chinese input muscle memory decayed. That's accurate and a good example of how environment reshapes default fluency.

**Where it breaks down:**

1. **The gender claim is too strong.** "Women care more about men being clean/good-looking" — the better statement is that, on average, women in many cultures are *socialized* to invest more in appearance and *trained* to read appearance signals, so they tend to have higher resolution on that dimension. That's a socialization-and-practice story, not an essential trait. Plenty of men have sharp aesthetic discrimination (fashion designers, the same code geeks you mention), and plenty of women don't care. The variance *within* each group dwarfs the difference *between* groups. Whenever a claim collapses a wide distribution into "women X, men Y," it's usually the socialization/skill variable doing the real work, not the gender.

2. **"Knowing ugly" ≠ "can't tolerate it."** Higher discrimination doesn't automatically produce intolerance. Many highly skilled people become *more* generous, not less — they see effort, potential, constraints (the "newbie showing potential" you mention). Intolerance is a separate trait (often closer to status signaling or insecurity) layered on top of skill. Skill gives you the *ability* to judge; it doesn't dictate that you *will* judge harshly.

3. **Taste is partly arbitrary.** Code quality has somewhat objective anchors (correctness, complexity, maintainability). Aesthetic "good taste" is more culturally contingent — what reads as "well-dressed" shifts by era, subculture, and context. So the analogy to code is partial: shared direction (trained discrimination), different ground truth (objective-ish vs. convention-driven).

**The tighter version of your claim:**

> People who invest heavily in a skill develop finer perceptual discrimination in that domain and notice flaws novices miss. Appearance is one such domain, and in many cultures women are socialized to invest in it more — so on average they read appearance signals at higher resolution. This is the same mechanism by which an experienced engineer can't unsee bad code.

That's true and well-grounded. The original version smuggles in three extra claims — gender essentialism, that discrimination implies intolerance, and that aesthetic taste is as objective as code — and those are the parts that don't survive scrutiny.

So: right mechanism, overextended conclusion.
