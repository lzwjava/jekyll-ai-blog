Question: How does Elon Musk operate or run his companies?

Answer:
Musk's approach shows up as a consistent set of habits across Tesla, SpaceX, X (Twitter), and his other ventures:

- **Extreme hands-on involvement** — Musk takes a hands-on approach, often diving into technical details and actively participating in problem-solving rather than staying at a distance from operations. Critics note this can tip into micromanagement.

- **Small, elite teams** — Musk prefers small teams with strong technical skills and actively resists growing headcount, pushing back on new hires. He favors small yet highly competent, motivated teams over large ones because they're more scrappy, have less communication overhead, and need little middle management.

- **Vertical integration** — His companies try to control as much of their own production and supply chain as possible instead of outsourcing — if a part is too expensive, employees are pushed to design a cheaper in-house version. As a result, SpaceX manufactures around 90% of its own rocket parts and electronics.

- **Aggressive deadlines and "stretch goals"** — Musk sets extreme deadlines, regularly telling employees, customers, and executives that products will ship faster than anyone thinks possible. He sets "stretch goals" meant to push organizations past conventional limits and treats fast experimentation and failure as part of learning.

- **Direct, personal hiring** — Musk personally handles a large share of recruiting and hiring across his companies, looking for hardworking critical thinkers who can effectively do the work of multiple people.

- **Data-driven but intuition-led decisions** — Despite relying on vision and instinct, Musk is known for valuing hard data to inform decisions.

- **Mission-first framing** — Rather than optimizing for quarterly earnings, Musk frames his leadership around civilization-scale goals — making humanity multi-planetary, accelerating sustainable energy, and merging human cognition with AI — and treats these as existential priorities rather than ordinary business targets.

- **Volatile, high-pressure culture** — His management is also known to be unpredictable, with high turnover and abrupt firings — most visibly during his takeover of Twitter — which critics say makes for an unstable work environment even as it attracts top talent. Observers have compared his early Twitter leadership to a Trump-style playbook: an inner circle chosen more for loyalty than expertise, and major decisions announced publicly on the platform before being vetted internally.

In short: extremely hands-on, deliberately lean and fast-moving, vertically integrated to cut costs and dependencies, and driven by big missions — paired with a demanding, sometimes chaotic culture that produces both breakthroughs and controversy.
