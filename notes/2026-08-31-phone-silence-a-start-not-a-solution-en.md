**Question:** Does closing or silencing your phone actually help with focus? Some people do this.

**Answer:**

Yes, it helps — but the picture is more nuanced than most people think.

**Silencing or closing does help:**

- After a distraction, it can take up to 23 minutes to regain full concentration. People who turn off notifications tend to respond to messages in batches, which saves significant time across the week, and over time the brain adapts to longer periods of deep focus.

- Even a 2.8-second interruption can cause a significant drop in performance and increase mistakes. Each time you check your phone, your brain must go through a process called "attention residue" — mentally switching back and forth creates cognitive fatigue.

- A 2020 study in *Computers in Human Behavior* found that people who intentionally limit notifications or use "Do Not Disturb" tend to experience lower stress, better focus, and improved time management.

**But just silencing isn't always enough:**

- Research from the University of Texas at Austin found that even when phones were turned off, flipped over, and silenced — if the phone was still *in the same room*, it occupied mental bandwidth and reduced cognitive capacity.

- A recent study found that moving the phone away reduced usage but didn't eliminate distraction — people simply shifted attention to other devices like laptops. The core issue is ingrained habits, not the device itself.

**Practical takeaway:**

Silencing is a good start, but the most effective approach is **getting the phone out of sight entirely** — ideally out of the room. Beyond that, changing your app habits and self-regulation strategies matter more than the physical location of the device.
