Question: How is Replit's success story, and what's the founder's story?

Answer:

**The founder — Amjad Masad**

Amjad Masad grew up in Amman, Jordan, in a family with Palestinian roots, and sold software for managing local internet cafés while still a teenager. He is a Jordanian-American entrepreneur and software engineer who studied computer science at Princess Sumaya University for Technology in Jordan from 2005 to 2010.

While still a student, he built something that changed his trajectory: he was compiling programming languages to run in the browser, a then-exotic idea, and by 2011 his work went viral on Hacker News — Brendan Eich, the creator of JavaScript, publicly acknowledged what he'd built. That reputation opened doors in the US. He came to the United States to be a founding engineer at Codecademy, and went on to run JavaScript infrastructure at Facebook before striking out on his own.

**Founding Replit**

Replit was co-founded on April 1, 2016 — April Fool's Day. Masad arrived in Silicon Valley without an elite network or much capital, and a product idea that most investors thought was too niche. The mission from day one was democratizing access to coding — making it so anyone can access technology that had historically been available to only a small, credentialed slice of the population.

**The long grind before the breakout**

The success story wasn't overnight. For years Masad muscled through multiple failed business models and got stuck at the same revenue plateau, and last year (2024) a reckoning forced him to cut half his staff.

The turnaround came with AI agents. The company closed a $250 million funding round led by Prysm Capital, nearly tripling its 2023 valuation, and revenue exploded from just $2.8 million to $150 million annualized in less than a year — driven largely by its AI Agent product, which quintupled revenue within months of launch.

Growth kept accelerating into 2026: Replit was valued at $9 billion after a $400 million fundraising round announced in March 2026, with backers including Andreessen Horowitz, Coatue, Georgian, and Qatar's sovereign wealth fund QIA. Its newest product, Agent 4, pushes "vibe coding" toward a more visual, whiteboard-like workflow where people sketch features and collaborate on app designs in real time.

**Masad's philosophy**

He's notably resistant to the "vibe coding" label that's now attached to his company. He says it was never really about coding — it was always about solving problems and making things, and putting "coding" in the name signals unnecessary complexity to newcomers. He also predicts a much bigger, not smaller, workforce: he expects a billion software engineers globally, just wielding far more powerful tools. One early demo captures his conviction — when Paul Graham instinctively checked the generated code during a demo, Masad told him not to bother, arguing the source code was just an unimportant byproduct and that programming would increasingly happen in plain English.
