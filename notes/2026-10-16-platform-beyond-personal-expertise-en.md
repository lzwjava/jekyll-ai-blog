## Question: Is inviting teachers and practitioners to share knowledge a powerful growth strategy, like GeekTime, Coursera, and 极客时间? Can a founder build a community that goes beyond the founder's own capabilities?

## Answer

**Yes. This is one of the most powerful ways to turn a founder's personal expertise into a scalable platform.** The important insight is that you don't have to be the best teacher, engineer, or expert in every subject. You can build a system that brings together people who have different expertise and gives them a reason to participate.

Think about the difference between these two businesses:

- **An expert business:** You personally teach, consult, and deliver projects. Revenue depends heavily on your time.
- **A platform business:** You attract experts, connect them with an audience, facilitate collaboration, and build products and services around those relationships.

The second model can grow beyond your individual capabilities.

### 1. Why the GeekTime and Coursera model works

Consider the different roles involved.

| Participant | Contribution | What they gain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder | Vision, product, distribution, partnerships | Business growth and ownership |
| Teachers and practitioners | Expertise, experience, content | Income, reputation, audience |
| Learners | Attention, questions, feedback, payments | Knowledge and career opportunities |
| Community members | Discussions, referrals, projects | Relationships and opportunities |
| Companies | Real problems, projects, sponsorships | Talent, solutions, access to expertise |

The founder doesn't need to personally produce all the knowledge.

Instead, the founder builds the infrastructure that allows knowledge to flow between people.

For example, you might invite:

- An AI infrastructure engineer to explain how their team serves billions of tokens.
- A Hong Kong fintech engineer to share lessons from building financial systems.
- A startup founder to explain how they acquired their first 1,000 customers.
- An experienced mobile developer to demonstrate how they ship applications with AI coding agents.
- An independent researcher to discuss training a language model on limited GPU resources.

Each guest brings something you couldn't produce as effectively on your own.

And each guest potentially brings their own audience, professional network, and credibility.

**Your job shifts from being the sole producer of value to being the person who organizes the production of value.**

### 2. The deeper business insight: the founder's scope is not the company's scope

Imagine you're building Fun Live again.

At the beginning, you might personally know 10 good engineers. You invite them to share their experiences.

Then something interesting happens:

1. One speaker introduces you to three other experts.
2. Those experts bring their own audiences.
3. Audience members start asking questions and sharing their own experiences.
4. Some participants become speakers.
5. Companies begin approaching you to sponsor events or find technical talent.
6. Some companies become consulting clients.
7. Your community develops a reputation that attracts experts without requiring you to recruit every person individually.

Eventually, **the network becomes more valuable than any individual piece of content.**

This is a form of leverage: other people's knowledge, time, reputation, and distribution contribute to the value of your business.

GeekTime provides a concrete example. Its official materials describe a model combining industry experts, structured educational content, communities, live interaction, and enterprise learning services. Its business has expanded beyond individual courses into corporate training. [Geekbang Technology](https://www.geekbang.org/index?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Notice the distinction: it isn't simply a website where teachers upload videos. It coordinates the entire experience.

You can think of the business as:

\\[
\text{Platform Value}
=

\text{Experts}
+
\text{Audience}
+
\text{Distribution}
+
\text{Relationships}
+
\text{Trust}
\\]

The software is only one component.

### 3. Why this strategy is particularly interesting for you

You already have several assets that could support this model:

- **Engineering credibility:** 12 years of engineering experience, including work in financial technology and AI.
- **Entrepreneurial experience:** You previously built Fun Live, attracting approximately 30,000 users.
- **Technical content:** Your website, [lzwjava.com](https://lzwjava.com/), contains thousands of AI and engineering notes.
- **Distribution:** You have existing WeChat groups and an international professional network.
- **Commercial capabilities:** You can deliver AI, agent, backend, and mobile development projects.

These assets fit together naturally.

I would combine three business models rather than choosing just one.

| Business | Purpose | Revenue model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fun Live community | Attract people and establish relationships | Sponsorships, events, memberships |
| Technical content platform | Build long-term organic distribution | Courses, sponsorships, premium content |
| AI/software consultancy | Solve real business problems | Project fees, retainers, ongoing support |

The community attracts people. Content compounds your reach. Consulting monetizes your ability to solve expensive problems.

You don't need millions of users before generating revenue.

One company that needs a $20,000 AI automation project could be more commercially valuable than 10,000 people who read your content but never buy anything.

The community and content help you discover those companies.

### 4. How I would execute it: start with a series, not a platform

I wouldn't begin by building a complicated app, recruiting dozens of teachers, or producing a library of hundreds of courses.

I'd start with a recurring event series.

For example:

**Fun Live: How Engineers Build Real AI Products**

Invite one practitioner each week.

Potential topics:

- How to serve 1 billion tokens per day.
- How to build production AI agents.
- How to run LLM inference on limited GPU budgets.
- How to build AI products for financial institutions.
- How to sell software development services to international customers.
- How a solo founder acquires their first paying customers.

Your existing experience gives you a starting point for finding relevant speakers and asking substantive questions.

Each session becomes multiple content assets:

```text
One expert interview
    |
    +-- Full video
    +-- Technical article
    +-- Short clips
    +-- Practical implementation notes
    +-- Community discussion
    +-- Follow-up interview
    +-- Potential consulting opportunity
```

AI agents can automate much of the transcription, editing assistance, summarization, translation, publishing, and content repurposing.

The important human work is selecting good speakers, asking valuable questions, establishing trust, and maintaining relationships.

**One good conversation can produce content, distribution, relationships, and business opportunities simultaneously.**

### 5. Why would experts agree to participate?

This is one of the most important questions.

You cannot build a sustainable platform by assuming everyone will contribute simply because your project is interesting.

Give contributors concrete benefits.

| What you offer | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Professional exposure | They reach an audience beyond their existing network. |
| High-quality content | They receive edited clips and articles they can share. |
| Reputation | They become associated with valuable technical discussions. |
| Professional relationships | They meet other experts, founders, and potential customers. |
| Direct compensation | Paid teaching, sponsorships, or revenue sharing when appropriate. |
| Business opportunities | Relevant introductions to companies and collaborators. |

Not every guest needs to be paid for every appearance. Some people genuinely enjoy sharing knowledge and meeting peers.

But if you want consistent, high-quality contributions, you need a repeatable exchange of value.

GeekTime's model demonstrates one way to structure this: combine expert contributions with a professional content-production process, distribution, and learning experiences. [Geekbang](https://www.b.geekbang.org/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Your advantage could be making participation exceptionally easy: the expert spends an hour talking, and your team handles the rest.

One important boundary when inviting colleagues from Hong Kong, Singapore, or the United States: invite them to discuss their personal technical experience, public knowledge, and approved projects. Don't ask them to disclose confidential employer information or proprietary systems.

### 6. The biggest mistake: confusing an audience with a community

These are different things.

An audience consumes what you publish.

A community develops relationships with you, with one another, and around a shared purpose.

Consider three stages:

**Stage 1: Media**

You publish articles, videos, and interviews.

Your primary goal is distribution.

**Stage 2: Community**

Members attend recurring events, ask questions, help one another, and introduce other people.

Your primary goal is trust and repeated participation.

**Stage 3: Business ecosystem**

Members collaborate on projects, hire one another, recommend your consultancy, become instructors, or create products.

Your primary goal is enabling valuable transactions.

You shouldn't assume that a large audience will automatically become a community or that a community will automatically generate revenue.

You need to deliberately create opportunities for members to interact.

For Fun Live, that could mean monthly technical sessions, small discussion groups, project demonstrations, and introductions between people working on similar problems.

The ultimate goal is not merely to have a large WeChat group. It is to become a trusted place where capable people meet and useful things happen.

### 7. My recommendation: use Fun Live as your distribution and relationship engine

Given your background, I would organize the business around this loop:

```text
                  Fun Live
                      |
             Attract practitioners
                      |
             Publish their knowledge
                      |
              Grow the audience
                      |
          Build trust and relationships
                      |
          +-----------+-----------+
          |                       |
     Paid courses          Consulting leads
          |                       |
          +-----------+-----------+
                      |
               Revenue and growth
                      |
          Invest in better content,
          distribution, and community
```

Initially, I would prioritize consulting revenue over building a large course business.

Why?

Consulting can generate revenue from a relatively small number of customers. Courses and communities generally require more experimentation to discover what people will consistently pay for.

You can use the community to identify real problems, meet decision-makers, and demonstrate your technical abilities. When you repeatedly encounter the same problem, you can turn your solution into reusable software, a paid workshop, or a course.

Over time, you may discover that a particular technical niche deserves a much larger platform.

For example, you might start with AI engineering practitioners, then develop a strong community around AI infrastructure, production agents, and practical AI adoption for businesses.

That would be a more defensible starting point than trying to build a platform for every kind of technology and every kind of learner.

### 8. What I would measure during the first 90 days

Start with a small experiment.

| Metric | Initial target |
| --- | ---: |
| Expert interviews | 12 |
| Distinct practitioners involved | 10+ |
| Published content pieces | 40+ |
| Recurring live sessions | 8–12 |
| Qualified business conversations | 10 |
| Paid consulting projects | 1–3 |

These are experimental targets, not predictions.

The most important questions are:

1. Can you consistently attract interesting practitioners?
2. Does their participation bring new people into your network?
3. Do those people return and participate?
4. Can you turn some of those relationships into paying customers?

If the answers are yes, you have evidence that the model is working.

If people watch the videos but never return, you may have a media business rather than a community.

If people return but nobody has a willingness to pay for anything, you still need to identify a viable business model.

## Final thought

Your original insight is correct: **a founder doesn't have to personally possess all the expertise that their company delivers. They can build an organization that connects expertise, talent, customers, and opportunities.**

The transition is from:

- Being the person who knows the answers to building a place where people with answers gather.
- Being the person who creates every piece of content to organizing a network of contributors.
- Finding every customer yourself to building a reputation and network that help customers discover you.
- Selling only your own time to creating systems and relationships that generate value beyond your individual output.

But don't lose sight of the commercial foundation. A community is not automatically a business.

For you, I would start with **Fun Live as a media and community platform, use your AI expertise to establish credibility, and use consulting to generate revenue from the relationships you build.**

You can expand into courses, memberships, events, and other products once you have evidence that the audience wants them.

The long-term opportunity is to build something where you no longer need to personally create every piece of value. You organize the people, systems, and relationships that make the value possible.
