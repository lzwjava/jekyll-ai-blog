## 2. The missing layer might be human relationships

GitHub is excellent for code collaboration, but code hosting alone doesn't ensure that a developer in Guangzhou develops a meaningful professional relationship with an engineer in San Francisco.

Imagine this workflow:

A developer in China builds an impressive inference optimization project.

An American engineer discovers it through your community. They discuss the implementation, invite the developer to present at an online event, and eventually collaborate on a production system.

Later, a startup hires the Chinese developer, or the two developers launch a company together.

Your platform would facilitate that journey.

```text
Discover people
      |
Learn from them
      |
Discuss ideas
      |
Collaborate on open source
      |
Build professional relationships
      |
Find jobs, clients, or cofounders
      |
Build products and companies
```

**The valuable asset is not simply content or code. It is the network of trusted relationships formed around building things together.**

This is also why I wouldn't start by competing directly with GitHub. Developers already have repositories, issue trackers, pull requests, and established workflows.

Instead, build the layer that helps people discover one another, understand each other's work, and find reasons to collaborate.

## 3. What I would build: a global community for builders

I'd position it around a simple idea:

> **Connect builders across borders. Share knowledge. Build together.**

Not a Chinese platform trying to expand overseas. Not a Western platform trying to acquire Chinese users. A community where both groups are contributors from the beginning.

I'd start with AI and open-source engineering because you already have relevant expertise, content, projects, and relationships.

### Four core products

**A. Global technical knowledge**

Invite Chinese and Western engineers to explain what they are building.

- Bilingual technical articles.
- Interviews with AI researchers and engineers.
- Open-source project demonstrations.
- Tutorials based on real implementations.
- Discussions comparing different engineering approaches.

For example, a Chinese engineer explains how they trained a small language model on a limited GPU budget. An American researcher explains a different approach. Your platform brings both perspectives together.

**B. Cross-border open-source collaboration**

Let people discover projects and find contributors.

Tasks:
  - CUDA optimization

  - Benchmarking
  - Documentation
  - Model evaluation

Integrate with GitHub instead of rebuilding its version-control functionality.

The differentiated product would be helping projects recruit contributors across language and geographic boundaries.

**C. A global technical community**

Organize recurring online events:

- AI engineering sessions.
- Open-source project showcases.
- Founder interviews.
- Technical debates.
- Collaborative coding sessions.

Make the content bilingual where useful, and ensure that participants can join without having to belong to a particular national or professional network.

**D. Business opportunities**

Eventually, connect the community to real economic activity:

- Companies find engineers and technical partners.
- Founders discover potential cofounders.
- Open-source maintainers find sponsors.
- International customers discover capable Chinese development teams.
- Chinese startups find international contributors and customers.

This last component is particularly relevant to your consulting business.

A community that helps people do real work together has a clearer path to sustainable revenue than one that only publishes content.

## 4. How do you bridge the Great Firewall?

This requires a practical distinction: **connecting communities is a product problem; network accessibility is an infrastructure and regulatory problem.**

You cannot assume that one website, messaging application, or social network will be equally accessible to everyone.

I'd design the community to be distributed across existing channels.

| Function | Possible approach |
|---|---|
| Public content | A globally accessible website with bilingual pages |
| Source code | GitHub integration, with appropriate alternatives for participants who cannot reliably access it |
| Chinese discussions | WeChat groups and other locally accessible channels |
| International discussions | Discord, Slack, forums, or email |
| Technical updates | RSS, newsletters, and email |
| Events | Online sessions with recordings and written summaries |
| Identity | A consistent profile linking a person's projects, articles, and contributions |

The goal isn't necessarily to force everyone into the same application. It's to let people participate through channels they can actually use.

You would also need to validate connectivity from mainland China, hosting requirements, cross-border data handling, and applicable content and service regulations before selecting the infrastructure.

Don't promise that your platform will bypass network restrictions. Build a useful, resilient cross-border community using lawful and accessible distribution channels.

## 5. The business model: community first, infrastructure later

I'd avoid monetizing every interaction at the beginning.

First, establish a community that people genuinely want to participate in. Then monetize services that create additional value.

| Stage | Product | Potential revenue |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bilingual content and expert interviews | Sponsorships |
| 2 | Technical workshops and courses | Ticket sales and course fees |
| 3 | Developer and project network | Recruitment and project-matching fees |
| 4 | Cross-border engineering services | Consulting and delivery fees |
| 5 | Mature developer ecosystem | Memberships, enterprise services, and other products |

Your consultancy can serve as the initial commercial engine.

For example, suppose your community connects you with 20 experienced AI engineers. A company approaches you for an AI agent project. You assemble the appropriate team, manage delivery, and charge for the service.

You don't have to employ every engineer full-time. You can work with independent contributors under clear agreements, paying them fairly for their work.

Over time, you may build a repeatable network for delivering projects internationally.

The community creates relationships; the consulting business demonstrates that those relationships can produce commercial value.

## 6. What I would do in your position

You already have Fun Live's historical experience, your AI notes, your engineering portfolio, and a network of technical contacts.

I would not spend six months building a new social network.

I'd run a 30-day experiment.

**Week 1: Establish the identity**

Create a simple landing page for a global developer community.

Explain the mission, publish your first technical interviews, and invite interested engineers to participate.

**Week 2: Recruit the first contributors**

Personally invite 10–20 engineers from China and overseas.

Start with people who have built interesting products, contributed to open source, trained models, or delivered real AI systems.

Ask each person to share one project or practical lesson.

**Week 3: Run the first global event**

Host an online session with two or three engineers from different regions.

Choose a concrete topic, such as building efficient LLM inference systems.

Publish the recording, transcript, and implementation references afterward.

**Week 4: Create the first collaboration**

Ask participants what they want to build together.

Choose one small open-source project, document the tasks, and help contributors collaborate through existing tools.

Measure whether participants return, invite others, contribute code, and develop relationships.

At the same time, talk to potential consulting customers to see whether the network can generate commercial opportunities.

After 30 days, you'll know more about the actual demand than you would from months of designing platform features.

### The most important strategic decision

I would make the community **international by design, rather than Chinese-first with international expansion as an afterthought**.

That means:

- English is a first-class language, with Chinese support.
- Chinese and Western engineers both contribute and lead.
- Technical quality matters more than nationality.
- People retain ownership of their projects and receive appropriate credit.
- The community helps people find collaborators, not merely consume content.

You don't need to start with every country, every programming language, or every technology.

Start with a small group of serious AI builders and make their collaboration genuinely valuable.
