**Question:** Is it true that many people become bitter and short-tempered in their 30s and 40s, especially those who haven't achieved the success they wanted, in modern society?

**Answer:**

Yes — there is real psychological and scientific evidence behind this, though it's more nuanced than a simple rule.

**The "Happiness U-Curve" is well-documented**

Large-scale research by economists David Blanchflower and Andrew Oswald, analyzing data across dozens of countries, found that happiness tends to follow a U-shaped curve, with its lowest point hitting somewhere in midlife — for many people, their early to mid-40s.

For both sexes, the probability of depression peaks around age 44. One researcher's best explanation is that people eventually learn to confront their unrealistic aspirations — they must bring their expectations into line with what they can actually achieve.

**Why bitterness specifically develops**

By their 30s, unresolved stress, trauma, or relationship patterns may begin to surface. By the time people reach their 40s, the psychological themes often shift again — there's deeper reflection about meaning and purpose. If people feel disconnected from purpose or meaning, distress can increase.

Emotional bitterness refers to accumulated feelings of sadness, resentment, and anger over time. It is a secondary emotion that results from feeling invalidated and unappreciated across too many situations and relationships.

**Unmet ambitions are a key driver**

Following the concept of the "midlife crisis," it became a popular way of describing how men and women around age 40 became disillusioned with work, disenchanted with relationships, and detached from family responsibilities. The gap between what people *hoped* to achieve and what they *actually* achieved is a central cause of this bitterness.

**But it's not inevitable, and the picture is shifting**

A large 2025 study spanning 44 countries found that the classic U-shaped pattern has effectively disappeared — young adults now report the highest levels of psychological distress, and ill-being actually falls steadily with age. This suggests modern pressures (social media, economic anxiety, comparison culture) are hitting younger generations harder than before.

Importantly, bitterness isn't something you're born with — it's the result of certain behaviors and mindsets taking root over time. These patterns are avoidable if you know what to look for.

**In short:** Yes, midlife bitterness and bad temper are real and well-researched, and unmet success expectations are a major trigger. But it is not universal — it's an *average* trend, not a destiny. And interestingly, those who push through the midlife low tend to report *greater* happiness in their 50s, 60s, and 70s than in their 30s and 40s.
