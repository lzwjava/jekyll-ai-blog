Question: **Why do Chinese engineers move to Singapore and then later return to China?**

Answer: The key is that **Singapore is often a bridge, not necessarily the final destination**.

For an engineer, the decision can look roughly like:

**China → Singapore → China**

because each move solves a different problem.

### 1. Singapore is a very good “internationalization layer”

For a Chinese engineer, Singapore gives you:

* English-speaking work environment
* International company experience
* Easier access to Southeast Asia
* More internationally recognized employment history
* Geographic proximity to China
* Relatively straightforward work authorization compared with the US

Singapore's Employment Pass is explicitly designed for foreign professionals, and experienced tech workers in shortage occupations can get up to a **5-year EP**. ([Ministry of Manpower Singapore][1])

So someone might spend 2–5 years there building:

```text
China
  ↓
Singapore
  ├── multinational experience
  ├── English
  ├── international network
  ├── regional business exposure
  └── stronger CV
  ↓
China
```

It's particularly attractive if the alternative is trying to get into the US immigration system.

---

### 2. But Singapore is a tiny engineering market

This is probably the biggest reason.

Singapore is an excellent **regional HQ / finance / sales / operations / international business** location.

But if you're a hardcore engineer, the number of companies and technical problems available to you is much smaller than in:

* Shenzhen
* Shanghai
* Beijing
* Hangzhou
* Guangzhou
* Chengdu

China simply has a much larger domestic technology and manufacturing ecosystem.

For example, if you're doing AI/robotics/hardware, China gives you an enormous surrounding ecosystem:

```text
models
  ↓
compute
  ↓
manufacturing
  ↓
components
  ↓
factories
  ↓
distribution
  ↓
millions of users
```

Singapore cannot reproduce that scale.

So after getting international experience, some engineers realize:

> "The interesting technical problems I actually want to work on are back in China."

That is especially relevant now in AI, robotics, autonomous driving, semiconductors, and advanced manufacturing. Recent reporting shows an increase in overseas Chinese talent returning specifically toward AI and advanced manufacturing opportunities. ([South China Morning Post][2])

---

### 3. The salary calculation is different from the US

Singapore salaries can look impressive in RMB.

But you have to compare:

```text
after-tax income
- rent
- childcare
- food
- transportation
- opportunity cost
```

rather than:

```text
SGD salary × exchange rate
```

And Singapore has extremely expensive housing.

For a senior engineer, the interesting comparison isn't necessarily:

```text
Singapore: S$150k
China:     RMB 1m
```

It's:

```text
Singapore:
    high salary
    + international exposure
    - very high housing
    - small tech ecosystem

China:
    sometimes lower cash salary
    + cheaper living
    + enormous tech ecosystem
    + family/network
    + potentially much larger equity upside
```

At the very high end, Chinese companies can also pay aggressively for people with overseas experience. Recent reporting has documented Chinese tech companies recruiting engineers and researchers back with competitive compensation and incentives. ([The Wall Street Journal][3])

---

### 4. Singapore can be especially useful for Chinese founders

This is a slightly different phenomenon.

For an engineer who eventually wants to build a company:

```text
China
  ↓
Singapore
  ↓
international customers / capital / company structure
  ↓
China + Singapore + global
```

Singapore is increasingly being used as a neutral base for companies operating between China and the US. Reuters reported that more than 50 Chinese AI-related firms had expanded there since 2024, partly because Singapore provides access to international customers and a comparatively neutral business environment. ([Reuters][4])

So the person isn't necessarily thinking:

> "I am emigrating to Singapore."

They may be thinking:

> "I need a Singapore node in my career/company."

That's a very different strategy.

---

### 5. There is also a life-cycle effect

For a 25-year-old engineer:

```text
Singapore
    ↓
international exposure
    ↓
learn English / management / global business
    ↓
build network
```

For a 35-year-old with parents, spouse, children, house, and a startup:

```text
Singapore
    ↓
"Why am I paying Singapore prices
 for a much smaller market?"
    ↓
China
```

And China is only a short flight away.

That's one reason Singapore behaves differently from the US as an emigration destination. Returning to China doesn't feel like completely abandoning the international career you built.

---

### The interesting part

I would think about it as **option value**:

```text
        ┌── US
        │
China ──┼── Singapore ──┐
        │               │
        └── HK          ↓
                      China
```

Singapore gives a Chinese engineer a relatively low-friction way to acquire **international optionality** without making the much larger commitment of moving to North America.

And Singapore itself is deliberately trying to capture this population: from 2027, it is replacing Tech.Pass with a new **ONE Pass (AI and Tech)** track aimed at attracting high-end AI/tech talent. ([Economic Development Board][5])

So if you're seeing **Chinese engineers → Singapore → China**, I wouldn't interpret it as "Singapore failed."

Often the objective was never permanent migration.

It was more like:

**get global exposure → increase career capital → return when China's opportunity set becomes more attractive.**

I can also break this down specifically for **AI engineers**—e.g. why someone at OpenAI/Google/ByteDance might choose **China vs Singapore vs US** at age 25, 30, and 35.
