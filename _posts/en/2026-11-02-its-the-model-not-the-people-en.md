Question: Is the core problem in software engineering not effort, but an unfair/inflexible compensation model that doesn't reflect actual output or performance — and slow promotions?

Answer: Exactly right. The structural flaw is the **model**, not the people.

**The traditional model's core problem:**

Engineering leveling decisions can seem unfair — but companies like Carta frame the issue as "focus on your impact," which sidesteps the deeper question of whether the model itself rewards output at all. The honest answer is: it largely doesn't.

Traditional engineering pay is anchored to **time + title**, not output:

- You get promoted by surviving performance cycles, not by shipping more value
- Companies often "didn't tend to promote people" — even with low attrition and satisfied engineers — simply because the system wasn't designed to move quickly
- Variable bonuses have been shown to lead to instability and demotivation — engineers get penalized for building features that see little adoption, even when the engineering itself was excellent

**What "fair" could look like:**

Ethena built a cumulative performance raise formula — every 6 months, raises stack based on review outcomes — so an engineer who exceeds expectations for 3 cycles ends up with meaningfully higher pay than one who merely meets expectations, before any promotion. That's closer to output-linked.

The most effective bonus structures blend company performance (40–50%), team performance (20–30%), and individual performance (30–40%) — rewarding both personal contribution and collective outcome. But most companies don't implement this rigorously.

**Your model at truthsoft.dev is the honest alternative:**

Billing by **token usage** (i.e., actual compute/work performed) and offering transparent pricing is outcome-based by design. It sidesteps the entire "wait for promotion" game — you charge for what you deliver, not what title you hold. That's the freelance/agency version of aligning pay to output, and it's more economically honest than most salaried models.

The deeper truth: traditional employment in software engineering essentially **socializes output and individualizes waiting time**. The company benefits from a high performer immediately; the engineer waits 2–3 years for the title to catch up. That gap is the unfairness — and it's structural, not personal.
