Question: Why do Indians often have darker/black skin — what's the biological explanation?

Answer:

First, the premise needs a correction before the mechanism: India isn't uniformly dark-skinned. Skin tone across the subcontinent ranges from very fair (parts of Kashmir, Punjab) to very dark (parts of Tamil Nadu, Kerala), and it's genetically one of the most variable regions on Earth. A 2013 study measuring melanin index across South Asia found a spread of 28–79 MI units within the subcontinent alone — a wide variation in skin color (MI 28–79) in South Asia, comparable to the range seen going from Northern Europe to sub-Saharan Africa globally.

**The core mechanism (same for all humans):** Skin color = melanin density, and melanin production is a UV-radiation trade-off:

- High UV (near equator) → selection favors more melanin → protects folate (UV degrades folate, critical for fetal development) and prevents skin cancer.
- Low UV (high latitude) → selection favors less melanin → skin needs to let UV-B through to synthesize vitamin D.

India spans low latitudes (8°N–35°N) with generally high year-round UV, so on pure "vitamin D vs folate" logic you'd expect dark skin to dominate everywhere. But it doesn't — and that's the interesting part.

**Why South Asia specifically is so variable — it's population genetics, not just climate:**

1. **Ancestry mixture (ANI/ASI).** Modern South Asians are a mix of Ancestral North Indians (ANI, related to West Eurasian/Iranian farmer + steppe pastoralist ancestry, lighter-skin alleles) and Ancestral South Indians (ASI, deeper indigenous South Asian lineage, higher melanin). The ANI:ASI ratio varies by region and caste/community, which is why you see gradients correlating with geography *and* with endogamous group.

2. **SLC24A5 — the biggest single-gene driver.** The same light-skin allele (rs1426654-A) that lightened Europeans is present at high frequency across South Asia and explains about 27% of total phenotypic variation in a South Indian cohort. Genetic dating shows this allele is identical-by-descent between Europeans and South Asians — the light skin associated allele... reveals that the genetic architecture of skin pigmentation in South Asia is quite complex — meaning it was likely introduced via shared ancestral migration (West Eurasian gene flow into India ~4,000+ years ago via steppe/Iranian farmer ancestry), not independently evolved in India.

3. **It's puzzling under pure vitamin-D selection.** Populations of South Asia live at lower latitudes than would be expected to require selection for lighter skin color on the basis of improved vitamin D synthesis — yet the light allele spread widely anyway. This suggests migration/admixture history, not just local adaptation, shaped the distribution.

4. **Endogamy locked in regional patterns.** Strict caste/community-based endogamy over thousands of years prevented alleles from mixing freely, so pigmentation genetics ended up geographically and socially structured rather than smoothly clinal — the effect of geographical, linguistic, socio-cultural boundaries further shaped by strict endogamy... was so strongly reflected in the complex patterning of this light skin allele.

5. **Other loci contribute smaller effects** — OCA2, SLC45A2, TYR, and region-specific SNPs like rs2470102 in Gangetic populations each add incremental variance on top of SLC24A5.

**TL;DR:** Darker skin in much of India follows the standard human pattern (high UV near equator → melanin selected for). But India's *unusual range* — from very fair to very dark within one country — comes from a layered history of ANI/ASI admixture, a shared-with-Europeans light-skin allele that spread via ancient migration, and millennia of endogamy that kept regional/caste allele frequencies from homogenizing.
