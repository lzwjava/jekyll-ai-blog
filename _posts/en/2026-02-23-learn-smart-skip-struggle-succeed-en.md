That is a very powerful and effective learning strategy. It's often described by experienced programmers, mathematicians, and self-taught individuals. What you've articulated touches on several key learning principles.

Let's break down why this method works so well:

1.  **Avoiding the "Frustration Wall":** The biggest enemy of learning is often not a lack of intelligence, but frustration. When you hit a wall and spend hours banging your head against it, you get tired, demoralized, and your brain stops absorbing information effectively. By limiting your struggle to "like one hour," you protect your motivation and keep learning a positive experience.

2.  **The Power of "Passive Learning" / Hintergrund Processing:** When you stop actively thinking about a hard problem and move on, your brain doesn't necessarily stop working on it. Your subconscious mind continues to process the information in the background. This is why you often have "aha!" moments while doing something completely unrelated, like showering or walking. You've fed your brain the question; now let it work.

3.  **Building a Mental Scaffold:** Sometimes a concept is hard because you lack a piece of foundational knowledge that comes later in the book, course, or project. By moving forward, you're actually building a broader mental framework. When you later encounter a practical application of that earlier, confusing concept, it suddenly has a place to live in your mind. The abstract theory clicks because you've now seen its purpose.

4.  **The "Big Picture" Effect:** Imagine doing a jigsaw puzzle. If you focus on one tiny section of the sky and can't find the right piece, you can get stuck. But if you move on and connect other parts of the puzzle—the house, the tree, the ground—you create a context. Suddenly, the shape and color of that sky piece make more sense, and you know exactly where it fits. Skipping ahead lets you build the bigger picture, which then illuminates the details.

5.  **Learning by Osmosis:** As you move forward, you will inevitably encounter new concepts that indirectly relate to or re-explain the old ones. A later chapter might define a term that was used in passing in the earlier, confusing chapter. A later tutorial might show a practical example of the abstract principle you were struggling with. You're learning the language and context of the subject, which makes everything easier.

This technique is a form of **"Iterative Learning"** or **"Holistic Learning."** It acknowledges that learning is rarely a perfectly linear path.

**To make this method even more effective, you can add a small step:**

- When you decide to "skip" something, take **30 seconds** to write down *one sentence* about what confused you. For example: "I'm confused about how the 'this' keyword works in JavaScript inside nested functions."
- Then, let it go completely.
- A few days or weeks later, when you come back to it, you have a precise question to guide your research, rather than just a vague memory of being confused.

This approach requires trust—trust that your brain will figure it out. And it almost always does. It's a fantastic way to learn complex subjects without burning out.
