### 4. What should Fun Live actually become?

I wouldn't start by rebuilding the entire original application.

The original product focused on live knowledge-sharing sessions. You can preserve that spirit without immediately recreating the entire technical infrastructure.

I'd evolve the concept in three stages.

**Stage 1: Fun Live as a community**

Start with what you already have.

- Continue sharing technical content.
- Invite engineers to give talks.
- Organize online discussions.
- Publish recordings, summaries, and implementation notes.
- Connect people who have complementary technical interests.

Use your existing website and communication tools.

The objective is to establish a consistent rhythm of valuable activity.

**Stage 2: Fun Live as a global technical network**

Once you have participants from several countries, introduce more structured collaboration.

For example:

- A directory of speakers and contributors.
- Public project demonstrations.
- Collaborative open-source projects.
- Technical discussions organized by topic.
- Introductions between founders and engineers.
- International engineering events.

At this stage, Fun Live becomes more than a publishing channel. It becomes a network of people who can help one another build things.

**Stage 3: Fun Live as a platform**

Only build more substantial software when you see repeated demand.

Perhaps you discover that speakers need a better way to organize events, developers want to find collaborators, or companies want to sponsor technical education.

Those observations can guide product development.

The principle is simple: **build the platform around activities people already want to do, rather than building a platform and hoping people arrive.**

You have already experienced the cost of building a startup. There's no need to repeat every part of that experience.

The next step is to make your offer specific enough that a customer can understand what they are buying.

For example:

> I help startups and small businesses turn AI ideas into working software. I can take a project from initial architecture through agent implementation, backend integration, testing, and deployment.

You can refine that positioning as you learn which customers are willing to pay.

#### How to price the work

Initially, I'd use three commercial models:

1. **Paid discovery:** investigate a business problem, evaluate feasibility, and produce an implementation plan.
2. **Fixed-scope project:** agree on a deliverable, timeline, acceptance criteria, and price.
3. **Ongoing engineering engagement:** provide a defined amount of engineering capacity or a recurring service.

Avoid competing primarily on lines of code or commits. Customers care about business outcomes, delivery reliability, and the cost of solving their problems.

AI can reduce your implementation time, but that doesn't mean you must charge less for solving an important problem.

You can use token consumption and infrastructure costs internally to understand your margins. They don't need to be the main unit of value presented to customers.

#### A practical initial revenue target

Suppose you aim for RMB 30,000 in monthly consulting revenue.

One possible model is:

- One project generating RMB 20,000.
- One smaller project generating RMB 10,000.

That gets you to the target without requiring a large team.

These are illustrative figures, not a forecast of what the market will pay. The actual prices will depend on the customer, scope, and business value.

Given your previous experience generating consulting revenue, I would validate demand through direct customer conversations before hiring employees or committing significant capital.
