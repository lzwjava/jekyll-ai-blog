**Question:** How did Liu De (刘德) persuade Inventec (英华达) to help manufacture Xiaomi's first phone?

---

**Answer:**

The story is one of mutual desperation — and it was indeed Liu De who personally made the pivotal visit.

**The context: a company on the verge of collapse**

By December 2010, Xiaomi had been rejected by nearly every supplier. Zhou Guangping's hardware team drew up a list of the world's top contract manufacturers — including Foxconn — visited every one of them, but came away with nothing. The more prestigious the company, the more cautious they were about taking on a new, unknown brand; their capacity was fully allocated to long-established clients.

After checking their list, the team found only one manufacturer that had not yet flatly refused them: Inventec in Nanjing. If this subsidiary of Taiwan's Inventec Group also walked away, it would be a fatal blow — because what use was a locked-in component supplier if there was no factory to assemble the phone?

**Why Inventec was open to the deal**

Inventec had ridden high on the PAS ("Little Smart") phone boom of 2005, but by 2010 that market was entering its end-of-life countdown, and Inventec was actively searching for new opportunities. Unlike the bigger manufacturers, they were not in a position to be arrogant — they accepted Xiaomi's business.

**The February 2011 meeting**

On the morning of February [of 2011], Zhang Feng, the General Manager of Inventec Nanjing, was sitting in his office waiting for a visitor named Liu De — from a company he had never heard of: Xiaomi.

Liu De later reflected: "The initial negotiations were far from smooth. A company that had never made a phone could only talk about the future — and as we kept talking, we ended up moving even ourselves." Over five months, they met more than a thousand people, and Liu De lost 20 pounds.

**What clinched the agreement**

The deal worked because both sides needed each other. Inventec was Xiaomi's only hope; Xiaomi, in turn, was Inventec's hope for the future. Xiaomi agreed to cover the upfront R&D costs, and Zhang Feng gave Xiaomi a very generous price. For Xiaomi, this was the critical first handshake between the software world and the hardware world.

**Securing the rest of the supply chain**

Even after Inventec agreed, Xiaomi still had to convince component suppliers. In March 2011, when the Japan earthquake struck, Liu De, Lei Jun, and Lin Bin flew directly to Japan to persuade Sharp to supply them screens. Sharp's response: after the earthquake, countless manufacturers sent emails, calls, and faxes — but not one came in person. "You are the first and only ones. If you hadn't come, we would never have paid attention, but since you did, we will support you."

They used Sharp's commitment to then persuade other suppliers, and by May 2011 they had completed the entire supply chain for Xiaomi's first phone.

From the very first Xiaomi phone, Inventec was Xiaomi's contract manufacturer. What started as a single assembly line grew to ten SMT production lines and five assembly lines by 2012. Inventec's Nanjing factory was essentially rescued by the partnership — by 2013, roughly 80% of Inventec's phone output was Xiaomi.
