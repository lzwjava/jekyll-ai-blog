**Question:** Why do people fail in startups, according to Y Combinator?

---

**Answer:**

Y Combinator co-founder Paul Graham wrote a definitive essay on this topic. At its core, there is really just one overarching mistake that kills startups: not making something users want. Everything else is downstream from that. Below are the 18 specific failure patterns he identified:

---

### 🧑 Founder-Level Mistakes

**1. Single Founder**
Starting a startup is too hard for one person. Even if you can do all the work yourself, you need colleagues to brainstorm with, to talk you out of stupid decisions, and to cheer you up when things go wrong. The low points in a startup are so low that few could bear them alone.

**2. Bad Location**
Startups prosper in some places and not others. Silicon Valley dominates, then Boston, then Seattle, Austin, Denver, and New York. The reason startups prosper in hubs is the same as for any industry — that's where the experts are, standards are higher, and people are more sympathetic to what you're doing.

**3. Obstinacy**
Don't get too attached to your original plan, because it's probably wrong. Most successful startups end up doing something different than they originally intended — often so different that it doesn't even seem like the same company.

**17. Fights Between Founders**
The two biggest reasons startups fail are running out of money and founding team implosions. About 20% of startups in a YC batch can blow up in the first 9–12 months due to founder issues. Graham adds: most disputes are not due to the situation but the people — which means they're often inevitable. Most founders who've been burned by disputes probably had misgivings, which they suppressed, when they started the company.

**18. A Half-Hearted Effort**
The most common type of failure is not the spectacular flameout — it's the startup that doesn't do much of anything. Most founders of failed startups don't quit their day jobs, and most founders of successful ones do.

---

### 💡 Idea & Product Mistakes

**3. Marginal Niche**
Most groups that apply to Y Combinator suffer from a common problem: choosing a small, obscure niche in the hope of avoiding competition. You can only avoid competition by avoiding good ideas.

**4. Derivative Idea**
If you look at the origins of successful startups, few were started in imitation of some other startup. The best problems to solve are ones that affect you personally — Apple happened because Steve Wozniak wanted a computer; Google because Larry and Sergey couldn't find things online.

**10. Having No Specific User in Mind**
A surprising number of founders assume that someone — they're not sure exactly who — will want what they're building. If you're trying to solve problems you don't understand, you're hosed.

**15. Sacrificing Users to (Supposed) Profit**
Making something people want is so much harder than making money from it. In version 1, solve the core problem. The companies that win are the ones that put users first — Google made search work, then worried about how to make money from it.

---

### 👩‍💻 Execution Mistakes

**6. Hiring Bad Programmers**
When business guys think the way startups work is that you have a clever idea and then hire programmers to implement it, they can't tell which are the good ones. Their startup lumbers along like a World War II bomber while competitors scream past like jet fighters.

**7. Choosing the Wrong Platform**
The scary thing about platforms is that there are always some that seem to outsiders to be fine, responsible choices and yet will destroy you if you choose them.

**8. Slowness in Launching**
Startups make all kinds of excuses for delaying their launch. The reason you need to launch is that it's only by bouncing your idea off users that you fully understand it.

**9. Launching Too Early**
The danger is that you ruin your reputation. You launch something, the early adopters try it out, and if it's no good they may never come back. Identify a core that is both (a) useful on its own and (b) something that can be incrementally expanded into the whole project.

**16. Not Wanting to Get Your Hands Dirty**
If you want to attract users, you'll probably have to get up from your computer and go find some. At least one hacker will have to spend some time doing business stuff.

---

### 💰 Money Mistakes

**11. Raising Too Little Money**
Too little money means not enough to get airborne. If you take money from investors, you have to take enough to get to the next step, whatever that is.

**12. Spending Too Much**
The classic way to burn through cash is hiring a lot of people. This bites you twice: in addition to increasing your costs, it slows you down — so money that's being consumed faster has to last longer.

**13. Raising Too Much Money**
Once you take several million dollars of VC money, the clock is ticking. More dangerously, once you take a lot of money it gets harder to change direction. The more people you have, the more you stay pointed in the same direction.

**14. Poor Investor Management**
As a founder, you have to manage your investors. You shouldn't ignore them, because they may have useful insights. But neither should you let them run the company — that's supposed to be your job.

---

### 📊 YC's Own Data

Of the 1,100+ startups Y Combinator has funded, only about 1% made it over $1 billion USD, and about 5% made it over $100 million USD. The odds are brutal — but as Graham concludes, most startups that *could* succeed fail because the founders don't devote their whole efforts to them. The biggest mistake you can make is not to try hard enough.
