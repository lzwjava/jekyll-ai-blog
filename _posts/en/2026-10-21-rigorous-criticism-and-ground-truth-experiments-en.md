**Question:** Is adopting a critical stance like Wang Yin (yinwang.org) genuinely beneficial? And since "genius" may be a myth, is firsthand experimentation the real path to mastery?

---

**Answer:**

These two ideas are related but deserve separate treatment.

**On being a critic like Wang Yin**

There's real intellectual value in his approach: he refuses to accept authority uncritically, demands clarity over jargon, and calls out hype in technology. That posture — *epistemic independence* — is genuinely healthy. Scientists and engineers who can say "I don't believe this because X" rather than deferring to consensus are exactly the people who push fields forward.

However, there's a distinction worth making:

- **Constructive criticism** identifies flaws *and* points toward better alternatives. It's open to revision when evidence changes.
- **Contrarian posturing** rejects things reflexively, sometimes substituting one's own ego for someone else's authority — equally unfounded.

Wang Yin's blog is an interesting case of both. His critiques of Java, C++, and certain CS education practices often contain genuine insight. But he also sometimes dismisses entire fields or people categorically, which is its own form of dogmatism. The takeaway: *systematic skepticism is a virtue; reflexive dismissal is not*.

**On "genius" and learning by experiment**

The evidence here tilts heavily toward your intuition. Anders Ericsson's research on deliberate practice showed that what we call "genius" is very often the product of accumulated hours of focused, effortful practice — not innate magic. Carol Dweck's growth mindset research reinforces this: people who believe abilities are fixed perform worse over time than those who treat skill as trainable.

That said, "genius doesn't exist" is a slight overcorrection. Neuroscience does show genuine variance in working memory capacity, processing speed, and early aptitude. Innate factors *influence* the trajectory, but they rarely determine the ceiling.

**So, learning by experiment?**

Largely yes — but with two caveats:

1. **Experiment + reflection beats experiment alone.** Pure trial-and-error without stepping back to revise your mental model is slow and can entrench wrong assumptions.
2. **Prior knowledge makes experiments more efficient.** Reading critically what others found *before* you lets you design better experiments and avoid dead ends. Wang Yin himself was deeply trained before he became a critic.

The honest synthesis: be a rigorous, humble critic; assume your abilities are trainable; and treat experiments as your ground truth — but read widely so you know *which* experiments are worth running.
