Question: What is Mohist thought (墨家思想)?

Answer:

Mohism (墨家) is one of the major philosophical schools of ancient China, founded by **Mozi (墨子, ca. 470–391 BCE)** during the Warring States period. It rivaled Confucianism as the most influential school of its era before eventually declining under the Han dynasty.

**Core Doctrines:**

- **Jiān ài (兼愛) — Universal Love:** The most distinctive Mohist idea. Unlike Confucian graded love (loving family more than strangers), Mozi argued one should love all people equally and impartially, regardless of relationship.
- **Jiē lì (節利) — Mutual Benefit:** Actions and governance should be judged by whether they produce concrete benefit for the people — wealth, population growth, and social order.
- **Fēi gōng (非攻) — Anti-aggression:** Mohists strongly opposed offensive warfare, viewing it as wasteful and harmful. They were famous for actively defending weaker states against aggressors.
- **Shàng tóng (尚同) — Conformity to Superiors:** Society needs unified standards and authority, from the local level up to Heaven (天), to avoid chaos.
- **Shàng xián (尚賢) — Meritocracy:** Officials should be selected by ability and virtue, not by birth or heredity.
- **Jiē yòng (節用) / Fēi yuè (非樂) — Frugality & Anti-Music:** Mohists condemned lavish ritual, music, and extravagance as wasteful diversions from productive work.
- **Fēi mìng (非命) — Against Fatalism:** People should not resign themselves to fate; human effort matters.
- **Tiān zhì (天志) — Will of Heaven:** Heaven (天) is a moral agent that rewards righteousness and punishes evil.

**Historical Significance:**

Mohism also developed remarkably early contributions to **logic, epistemology, geometry, optics, and mechanics**, recorded in the *Mozi* text. The school was highly organized, almost militaristic in structure, with its members renowned as skilled craftsmen and defenders.

It declined after the Qin unification (221 BCE), but has seen renewed scholarly interest for its proto-utilitarian ethics and its parallels to Western philosophy (e.g., Bentham's utilitarianism).
