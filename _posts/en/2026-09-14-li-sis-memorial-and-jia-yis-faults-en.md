**Question:** Introduce the classical Chinese essays *谏逐客书* (Memorial Against Expelling Guest Officials) and *过秦论* (The Faults of Qin).

---

**Answer:**

## 谏逐客书 — *Memorial Against Expelling Guest Officials*

**Author & Context**

Li Si (李斯, c. 280–208 BCE) was the influential Chancellor of the state and later dynasty of Qin, serving from 246 to 208 BCE. Originally from the state of Chu, he utilized the political philosophy of Legalism to formulate Qin's state policies.

In 237 BCE, the King of Qin ordered the expulsion of all foreign advisors from his court. Li Si remonstrated against this decree with the argument that the rulers of the remote western state of Qin had, for generations, relied on foreign advisors. The king accepted Li Si's remonstrance, and Li Si was subsequently promoted.

**Purpose & Argument**

The memorial makes a pragmatic, Legalist case: Qin's greatness was built on talent imported from other states. To expel foreign scholars and officials would be to "discard what is useful and strengthen one's enemies." Li Si argues by analogy — just as the king prizes foreign treasures, music, and beautiful things without caring about their origin, so too should he prize talented men regardless of their birthplace.

**Literary Significance**

The essay is celebrated as one of the finest persuasive prose works in classical Chinese. It is notable for its rich parallelism, sweeping historical examples from Qin's own past, and its powerful rhetorical momentum. It is often considered an ancestor of the ornate *pianwen* (parallel prose) style.

## 过秦论 — *The Faults of Qin*

**Author & Context**

Jia Yi (贾谊, c. 200–169 BCE) was a Chinese essayist, poet, and politician of the Western Han dynasty, best known as one of the earliest writers of *fu* rhapsody and for his essay *Guò Qín Lùn* (过秦论), which criticizes the Qin dynasty and describes his opinions on the reasons for its collapse. Since he wrote favorably of social and ethical ideas attributed to Confucius and focused on the failings of the Legalist-based Qin dynasty, he was classified by other scholars as a Confucian scholar.

**Purpose & Argument**

Jia Yi concluded his essay with what became the standard Confucian judgment on the reasons for Qin's collapse. He explained Qin's ultimate weakness as the result of its ruler's ruthless pursuit of power — the very factor that had made Qin so dominant — because, as Confucian teaching held, the strength of a government ultimately rests on the support of the people and the virtuous conduct of the ruler.

Jia Yi criticizes the First Emperor as a ruthless, short-sighted tyrant who abolished the ways of ancient sage kings and burned the writings of the Hundred Schools in an attempt to keep the people in ignorance. The First Emperor believed he had created an imperial structure that would last ten thousand generations — yet it survived only one.

**Literary Significance**

The essay, admired as a masterpiece of rhetoric and reasoning, was copied into two great Han histories and has had a far-reaching influence on Chinese political thought as a classic illustration of Confucian theory. Its most celebrated closing line — *"仁义不施，而攻守之势异也"* ("Because humaneness and righteousness were not practiced, the posture of offense and defense changed") — became one of the most quoted sentences in Chinese political writing.

## Side-by-Side Comparison

| | 谏逐客书 | 过秦论 |
|---|---|---|
| **Author** | Li Si (李斯) | Jia Yi (贾谊) |
| **Dynasty** | Late Warring States / Qin | Western Han |
| **Audience** | King of Qin (Qin Shi Huang) | Han Emperor (as political advice) |
| **Purpose** | Persuade the king to reverse an edict | Analyze why Qin collapsed |
| **Philosophy** | Legalist | Confucian |
| **Tone** | Eloquent, pragmatic advocacy | Sweeping historical narrative + moral judgment |
| **Genre** | Memorial (书/奏疏) | Political essay (论) |

Both essays are staples of Chinese high school and university curricula, and together they offer a fascinating dialogue: Li Si defends the open, talent-absorbing Qin state at its height, while Jia Yi, writing after its fall, diagnoses the moral rot that destroyed it.
