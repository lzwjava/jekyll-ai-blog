Question: **How has AI made AI talent in the Bay Area rich in recent years?**

Answer: **AI has created an unusually strong “talent → equity → wealth” flywheel in the Bay Area, especially since ~2023.** The important point is that most of the wealth isn't coming from salary. It is coming from **ownership of AI companies whose valuations exploded.**

### 1. The basic mechanism

Think of a strong AI researcher/engineer as having three ways to get paid:

```text
AI talent
   │
   ├── $300K–$500K salary
   │
   ├── annual bonus
   │
   └── equity / stock options  ← huge wealth creation
             │
             ▼
       company valuation ↑
             │
             ▼
       employee net worth ↑
```

The third component became extraordinary in the current AI cycle.

For example, Anthropic went from a **$183B valuation in September 2025** to **$380B in February 2026**, then **$965B in May 2026**. ([Anthropic][1])

OpenAI announced a **$852B post-money valuation** in March 2026 after raising $122B. ([OpenAI][2])

If you joined early and received meaningful equity, you don't need a $2M salary to become extremely wealthy.

---

## 2. Why AI talent gets so much equity

Because frontier AI talent is extraordinarily scarce.

There are probably thousands of very good ML engineers.

But there are **far fewer people who can actually contribute to frontier-model training**:

```text
ordinary software engineer
        ↓
ML engineer
        ↓
excellent ML engineer
        ↓
distributed training / inference / systems expert
        ↓
frontier-model researcher
        ↓
person who can materially improve the model
```

The last group is tiny.

So companies compete aggressively for them.

The result is almost an auction:

```text
Google
  ↕
Meta
  ↕
OpenAI
  ↕
Anthropic
  ↕
xAI
  ↕
AI startups
```

And the scarce worker can say:

> "If you want me, give me enough ownership."

Recent reporting describes top AI compensation reaching **hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars**, while some extreme recruiting packages reportedly reach much higher levels. ([Business Insider][3])

# 3. The really important thing: equity appreciation

Suppose you joined an AI startup in 2023.

Imagine:

```text
company valuation: $5B
your equity:       $2M
```

Then the company becomes:

```text
$5B → $50B
```

Your paper equity potentially becomes:

```text
$2M → $20M
```

Your salary might have been:

```text
$350K × 3 years = ~$1M
```

But your wealth creation came primarily from:

```text
$2M equity → $20M
```

That's the Silicon Valley wealth machine.

---

# 4. And then there is the IPO / secondary-market event

This is where **paper wealth becomes actual money**.

Employees can sometimes sell shares through:

```text
private secondary
       ↓
IPO
       ↓
public-market liquidity
```

This is already happening around the major AI labs.

The Financial Times reported that OpenAI distributed nearly **$11B of equity during 2024–2025**, creating a new class of "paper millionaires." ([Financial Times][4])

That's an enormous number.

Imagine hundreds or thousands of employees holding equity in a company that eventually reaches hundreds of billions or trillions in value.

# 5. The second-generation wealth effect is even more interesting

This is where Bay Area AI becomes different from a normal high-paying industry.

Someone gets:

```text
$10M from OpenAI
```

Then they don't necessarily retire.

They become:

```text
angel investor
       +
startup founder
       +
VC LP
       +
advisor
       +
AI recruiter/network node
```

So:

```text
OpenAI employee
      ↓
$10M liquidity
      ↓
starts AI startup
      ↓
hires 5 excellent engineers
      ↓
raises $100M
      ↓
employees become wealthy
      ↓
some of them start another company
```

You get **capital + talent + knowledge compounding inside the same geographic network**.

This is one reason Silicon Valley has historically been so difficult for other regions to replicate.

# 6. Founders have an even bigger upside

There are roughly three wealth tiers.

### Tier 1 — AI engineer

```text
salary: $300K–600K
equity: $500K–5M+
```

Potentially very wealthy.

### Tier 2 — early employee / research leader

```text
salary: $400K–1M+
equity: $5M–50M+
```

Now you're potentially in serious wealth territory.

### Tier 3 — founder

```text
ownership: 5–30%+
```

If:

```text
startup = $10B
ownership = 10%
```

then:

```text
paper wealth = $1B
```

That's why AI has created a much larger **founder lottery** than ordinary software.

# 7. The interesting part: AI increased the value of *technical leverage*

This is probably the most important economic change.

Previously:

```text
1 engineer → produces X
```

Now:

```text
1 excellent AI engineer
        +
GPU cluster
        +
frontier model
        +
agents
        ↓
can produce 5X / 10X / 20X more economic output
```

So the market is willing to pay an exceptional engineer disproportionately more.

A recent example is forward-deployed AI engineering: OpenAI roles have reportedly offered compensation up to **$345K plus equity**, while demand for this hybrid engineering/product role has exploded. ([Business Insider][5])

---

# 8. But there's a nasty asymmetry

This is **not**:

> "AI engineers are becoming rich."

It's more like:

> **The distribution of wealth inside AI talent has become extremely heavy-tailed.**

Something like:

```text
             wealth
               ↑
               │                         *
               │                       *
               │                    *
               │                *
               │            *
               │        *
               │     *
               │  *
               │ *
               └────────────────────────────→ talent
```

The top 0.1% can capture enormous amounts of value.

Meanwhile ordinary software engineers can actually be worse off because AI makes their work easier to automate.

That's already visible in the Bay Area: companies are simultaneously **cutting ordinary roles and aggressively recruiting elite AI talent**. ([Financial Times][6])

## My takeaway for you

Given your background, **the interesting lesson isn't "get an AI job in San Francisco."**

It's:

```text
                 ownership
                    ↑
                    │
research → engineer → founder
                    │
                    ↓
                 leverage
```

If you're technically strong enough to understand **training + inference + agents + systems**, the Bay Area AI economy gives you several paths:

```text
1. elite AI engineer
        ↓
   high TC + equity

2. early AI startup employee
        ↓
   asymmetric equity

3. AI infrastructure/product founder
        ↓
   founder ownership

4. consultant → product
        ↓
   services cash flow → startup equity
```

The **largest wealth creation is increasingly at #2–#4**, not from maximizing salary.

And that's the key distinction: **AI didn't merely create high-paying jobs. It created extremely valuable equity around scarce technical talent.** ([Financial Times][4])
