**Question:** Introduction to *我与地坛* ("I and the Temple of Earth"), and what are its three main thematic parts?

**Answer:**

*我与地坛* (Wǒ yǔ Dìtán, "I and the Temple of Earth") is a landmark essay by Chinese writer Shi Tiesheng (史铁生, 1951–2010). It is an autobiographical reflection on disability, suffering, maternal love, life and death, fate, and spiritual transcendence, framed through the author's repeated visits to Beijing's Temple of Earth park after becoming paralyzed at age 21. It was first published in 1991 and is regarded as one of the most profound and influential works of 20th-century Chinese prose.

The essay contains seven sections and fifty paragraphs, opening with the author's deep connection to the Temple of Earth, then sketching his mother's figure, followed by delicate descriptions of the garden's seasonal changes and the author's experiences there. Through a dialogue with the "garden spirit," it explores philosophical questions about existence, death, and the meaning of writing.

The essay is commonly understood in three broad thematic parts:

**Part 1 — The Author and the Temple of Earth (我与地坛)**
Shi describes his discovery of the Temple of Earth as a place of refuge after the onset of his disability — an "abandoned" yet eternal space where he could confront despair without societal judgment. He recounts years spent observing seasonal changes, ancient cypresses, and passersby, using these encounters as points of reflection on human diversity, suffering, and the search for meaning.

**Part 2 — The Author and His Mother (我与母亲)**
A pivotal section addresses his mother's hidden anguish and sacrificial love, revealed only after her death, expressing profound regret for his earlier self-absorption. These first two parts, which focus on the Temple of Earth and the author's remorse toward his mother, were selected for inclusion in the People's Education Press high school curriculum.

**Part 3 — Reflections on Life, People, and Meaning (对生命的思考)**
The author observes the park changing through the seasons — the trees, the sunlight, the people — as well as encounters with a persistent elderly couple, a dedicated runner, children at play, and a young woman with intellectual disabilities, all of which serve as meditations on the meaning of existence. The narrative progresses toward acceptance of fate, viewing disability as revealing deeper truths about vulnerability, resilience, and the necessity of difference in human existence. Death is addressed as an inevitable aspect of life, emphasizing endurance and spiritual insight over despair.
