**Question:** As a Chinese citizen working at a mainland Chinese company, can you register a Hong Kong company in your own name?

**Answer:**

**Short answer: Yes, from Hong Kong's side — but you need to comply with several mainland Chinese rules and check your employment contract.**

---

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong Law — No Restrictions on Mainland Citizens

A mainland China citizen can own 100% of a Hong Kong company. A Hong Kong private limited company requires at least one shareholder, who can be the same person as the director.

100% foreign ownership is allowed, so non-residents of Hong Kong may be a shareholder. A sole director can also act as a shareholder.

The local statutory requirements include a registered office in Hong Kong, a company secretary, and a designated representative for the significant controllers register. Registration can also be done fully remotely — no need to visit HK in person.

---

## 🇨🇳 Mainland China Side — Three Things to Watch

### 1. New ODI (Outbound Investment) Regulations — Effective July 1, 2026

This is the biggest recent development. China's first State Council-level outbound investment regulation (Order No. 837) took effect on 1 July 2026, consolidating previously fragmented rules from NDRC, MOFCOM, and SAFE into a unified framework.

Critically: Individual residents are now formally brought within the unified regulatory framework for outbound investment. Previously, individuals relied on SAFE Circular 37 to complete foreign exchange registration for round-trip investment structures. Standalone individual overseas investment and non-round-trip cross-border investment had long lacked explicit legislation.

In practice, this means investing in a HK company now requires you to complete applicable filing, reporting, and cross-border fund registration procedures, and penalties apply for failure to fulfil filing obligations, including orders to suspend investment activities, dispose of assets, surrender unlawful gains, and pay financial penalties.

### 2. Foreign Exchange Controls (SAFE)

Individuals were already subject to Chinese foreign-exchange restrictions. A Hong Kong holding company that must receive its investment capital from the mainland presents the full ODI problem. Moving money from mainland into a HK company requires proper channels and registration.

### 3. Employment Contract & Conflict of Interest

This is the most immediate practical risk. Under China's Employment Contract Law (Article 39.4):

If the employee's second job presents a conflict of interest with his/her job with the employer, is likely to result in the disclosure of the employer's confidential information, or competes with the employer's activities, the employer may terminate the contract.

Being a mere passive shareholder may be lower risk, but if a senior executive derived personal income or caused the company to incur an economic loss through conflicts of interest, the company may additionally institute legal action demanding compensation or transfer of illegal income.

A real legal precedent: a financial manager who set up an outside company without disclosure was terminated by Beijing Labour Arbitration Commission's ruling, which supported the employer's decision — and any income from the breach of loyalty must be returned to the employer.
