Question: Why can tomatoes go in the freezer (冷冻) but do poorly in the fridge (冷藏), and what happens to them when frozen ("ice tomato")?

Answer:

**Fridge (冷藏, ~1-4°C) is actually the worse option** — this seems counterintuitive but it's well documented:

- Tomatoes are a subtropical fruit. Below ~10-13°C, their cell membranes (rich in certain lipids that stay fluid only above this range) start to gel/solidify unevenly. This is called **chilling injury**.
- Chilling injury breaks down membrane integrity slowly over days, but the tomato doesn't fully collapse — it just goes mealy, loses flavor compounds (esters, aldehydes responsible for tomato aroma stop being produced), and the ripening enzymes get disrupted permanently even after you take it out. This is a *slow, partial, irreversible* degradation.
- So fridge storage gives you a tomato that looks okay but tastes like cardboard — worst of both worlds.

**Freezer (冷冻, <0°C) is a different failure mode**, and it's actually fine if you plan to cook with it:

- Water inside tomato cells freezes and expands into ice crystals, which mechanically punctures the cell walls.
- On thawing, those ruptured cells can't hold structure anymore — the tomato goes soft/mushy and releases water. This is **freeze damage**, not chilling injury.
- BUT: flavor compounds are largely preserved (freezing halts enzymatic degradation immediately, unlike the slow fridge damage). The texture is ruined but taste survives — which is why frozen tomatoes are perfectly good for sauces, soups, purees where texture doesn't matter, but useless for fresh salads.

**Practical rule:**
- Fresh eating soon → counter, room temp, out of direct sun
- Going to cook it later (sauce/soup) → freezer is fine, skin even peels off easily after thawing (blanching effect from ice crystal rupture)
- Never fridge unless already overripe and you need to buy 1-2 days — and even then, bring back to room temp before eating to partially recover flavor
