Question: Is it accurate to say that the baseline information a person needs to perform their job is a fixed prerequisite, while the information they produce and consume over a year of work constitutes their "output" — with that output being a function of both the significance and the volume of that information?

Answer: That's a reasonable first approximation, but it collapses a few distinct things that are worth separating.

**Where the idea holds up:**

- There is a real distinction between *threshold knowledge* (what you must already know or acquire to be competent — onboarding knowledge, domain fundamentals, tacit know-how) and *flow* (the information you generate, transform, and pass on while actually doing the job).
- Framing output as a function of both volume and significance is intuitive: someone who writes one report a year that redirects company strategy has "produced" more than someone who writes fifty routine status updates, even though the latter has higher volume.

**Where it needs refinement:**

1. **Significance and volume aren't independent variables you can just multiply.** Significance is usually *emergent* from context — the same piece of information (e.g., a sales number) can be trivial in one meeting and decisive in another. So "output" isn't a stable property of the information itself; it's assigned retrospectively based on what happened after it was used.

2. **Consumption vs. production aren't symmetric.** Peter Drucker's classic work on knowledge-worker productivity argued that a knowledge worker's output should be judged by results/effectiveness, not by inputs consumed or hours worked — reading and absorbing information is a precondition for output, not output itself. Counting "information consumed" as part of your yearly output risks confusing effort with contribution.

3. **There's a transformation step missing from the model.** The valuable thing usually isn't raw information at all — it's the synthesis, decision, or artifact that gets produced *from* it (a design, a judgment call, a piece of code, a resolved ambiguity). Two people can consume identical baseline information and produce wildly different value depending on how they transform it.

4. **Diminishing and non-linear returns.** Beyond a point, more volume doesn't add output — it can even subtract from it (noise, dilution of attention, lower signal-to-noise for whoever consumes your output next).

So a tighter version of your claim might be: *baseline information is the entry cost of the job; a person's output is better measured by the value-added transformation they apply to information — decisions made, problems resolved, artifacts created — rather than by the raw volume or self-assessed significance of information passing through them.*
