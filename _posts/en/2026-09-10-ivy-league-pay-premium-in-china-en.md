Question: Why can graduates of prestigious U.S. universities—especially Ivy League graduates—earn extremely high salaries in China when they are only around 30, sometimes ~¥1 million/year, even in fields like art, marketing, or brand management?

Answer: Because they are often **not being paid primarily for their academic major**. They are being paid for a combination of **scarcity, signaling, network, foreign-market knowledge, and business leverage**.

A typical case might look like:

> Ivy League / top U.S. school → fluent English + Chinese → U.S./global brand experience → returns to China → joins luxury/consumer/tech company → becomes China market lead / marketing director / VP → ¥1M+ total compensation.

### 1. The degree is a strong market signal

An Ivy degree can act like a very expensive **Bayesian prior** for employers.

Suppose a company sees two 27-year-olds:

```text
Candidate A:
Art major, unknown university
3 years marketing experience

Candidate B:
Yale/Columbia/Penn
Art/Design/History major
3 years global-brand experience
Native-level Chinese + English
```

The company may infer that B has:

* strong communication ability
* international cultural literacy
* elite social/network access
* ability to represent the company externally
* ability to communicate with headquarters
* ability to work with wealthy/high-status customers
* ability to recruit other elite people

The employer isn't necessarily saying:

> "Art history is worth ¥1M."

It's closer to:

> "This person can operate in a very valuable social/business environment."

---

### 2. Certain jobs have enormous leverage

A VP/market lead can influence hundreds of millions or billions of RMB of revenue.

If someone manages a China market doing:

```text
¥1B revenue
× 10% gross margin
= ¥100M gross profit
```

then paying the person:

```text
¥1M–2M/year
```

isn't economically crazy.

Compare that with an individual contributor whose output is mostly internal:

```text
engineer → produces software internally
```

The engineer can be extremely technically skilled but have less direct control over revenue.

Meanwhile:

```text
China Market VP
        ↓
brand strategy
        ↓
distribution
        ↓
partnerships
        ↓
customer acquisition
        ↓
¥100M+ business
```

The compensation ceiling can therefore become much higher.

---

### 3. China has a particular premium for "global ↔ China" people

This is especially important.

A person who understands both:

```text
China
   ↕
US / Europe
```

can solve coordination problems that ordinary employees cannot.

For example:

```text
US headquarters
       │
       │ strategy / brand / product
       ↓
China executive
       │
       ├── Chinese consumers
       ├── local media
       ├── KOLs
       ├── distributors
       ├── government/regulatory environment
       └── local business partners
```

That person isn't simply a marketer.

They are effectively a **translation layer between two business systems**.

That skill was particularly valuable during China's huge consumer/luxury expansion.

A strong market executive might already know:

```text

```

That network can have monetary value.

### 6. ¥1M at 30 is high—but not necessarily as extraordinary as it sounds

¥1M/year is about:

```text
¥83K/month
```

before considering bonuses/equity and taxes.

For ordinary Chinese employees, that's very high.

But among:

* multinational executives
* luxury brands
* investment banking
* private equity
* consulting
* major tech
* successful startups
* sales/business development
* senior product/marketing

it's a different labor market.

And "¥1M compensation" may mean:

```text
base       ¥600K
bonus      ¥200K
equity     ¥200K
----------------
total      ¥1M
```

rather than a ¥1M cash salary.

### 7. There is also a selection effect

You hear:

> "28-year-old Ivy League graduate → ¥1M."

You don't hear about:

```text
Ivy graduate → ordinary job
Ivy graduate → couldn't find China role
Ivy graduate → ¥300K
Ivy graduate → moved to US
Ivy graduate → career changed
```

So the visible examples are heavily selected.

Likewise, among people with ¥1M compensation, you're disproportionately seeing the people who entered **high-leverage industries and positions**.
