**Question: Is it right that important decisions require deep investigation, looking at both positives and negatives, and staying cautiously optimistic?**

**Answer:**

Yes. This is actually a very mature way of making decisions.

For important things — education, immigration, business cooperation, products, investments, architecture refactors — the biggest mistakes usually do not come from lack of effort. They come from **making decisions with incomplete information and emotional bias**.

A good decision process is usually:

```
1. Understand the goal
        ↓
2. Collect positive evidence
        ↓
3. Search for failure cases
        ↓
4. Estimate risks and reversibility
        ↓
5. Make a small commitment
        ↓
6. Update based on feedback
```

The key is not "be pessimistic". It is **avoid being blindly optimistic**.

For example:

### Applying for a US master's program

A beginner may only see:

* US university
* AI degree
* better opportunities
* possible immigration path

But a more experienced approach asks:

**Positive side**

* Does the school have good AI faculty?
* Can it improve credibility?
* Can it expand network?
* Can it open doors that are currently closed?

**Negative side**

* Total cost?
* Visa uncertainty?
* Opportunity cost?
* Age/career interruption?
* Will the degree actually change outcomes?
* What if the job market is bad after graduation?

Then you decide with more complete information.

---

Your example of spending 4–5 years finishing the associate degree is actually a useful learning experience.

Many people underestimate "small" administrative or learning tasks:

> "I can finish quickly."
> "The process is easy."
> "I will handle it later."

Then reality happens:

* exams require preparation
* paperwork has details
* motivation fluctuates
* life creates interruptions

The same pattern appears in engineering:

A large refactor:

```
Old system
   |
   | "Let's rewrite, it will be cleaner"
   ↓
New system
```

looks attractive.

But experienced engineers first investigate:

* hidden dependencies
* edge cases
* traffic patterns
* data migration
* rollback strategy
* operational risks

Because the cost of discovering a mistake halfway is huge.

---

A useful mental model:

**Cheap decisions → move fast.**

Examples:

* try a new library
* prototype a UI
* run a small experiment

**Expensive decisions → investigate deeply.**

Examples:

* moving country
* choosing university
* starting a company
* hiring partners
* rewriting core infrastructure

Because:

```
Decision quality ≈ Information quality × Thinking quality
```

More information does not automatically help, but **high-quality information reduces blind spots**.

---

The best state is probably:

> "I believe this may work, but I know why it may fail."

That is what you called "谨慎地乐观".

Many successful builders have this mindset:

* optimism gives energy to start
* skepticism prevents catastrophic mistakes
* experiments provide reality feedback

The goal is not to avoid mistakes completely. That is impossible.

The goal is:

```
small mistakes early
        ↓
learning
        ↓
larger successes later
```

A person who has experienced setbacks often becomes better at this because they stop relying only on confidence and start relying on **process**.
