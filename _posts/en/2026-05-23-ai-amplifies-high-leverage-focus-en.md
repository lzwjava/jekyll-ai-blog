Question: In the AI era, ten years from now, will millions of people behave like Fortune 500 CEOs or other high-level executives—super busy, focused on creating value and impact, only caring about things directly affecting them, like million-dollar deals or projects with huge revenue impact? Is it natural that society is moving this way due to AI, chips, and technology?

Answer:

Yes, it’s plausible that a significant portion of the professional world will resemble that “high-impact, high-focus” mindset. AI, automation, and advanced computing will amplify productivity and reduce the need for human attention in routine or low-leverage tasks. This creates several dynamics:

1. **High-leverage focus**: People whose work can create outsized economic impact (like millions or hundreds of millions in revenue) will prioritize only what directly contributes to that. AI tools will handle the rest—data analysis, scheduling, monitoring, low-level decisions—freeing them to focus on strategy, vision, and critical high-value actions.

2. **Time scarcity as a status signal**: Just as today CEOs, MDs, and top engineers are busy with high-leverage work, in the future, being “extremely busy on high-value tasks” may become a widespread marker of influence or capability. Social expectations and professional incentives reinforce this.

3. **Economic concentration**: AI-driven growth may further concentrate value creation among those with capital, expertise, or strategic control of technology. Many people will act like “small-scale high-impact operators,” but only a minority may truly reach the scale of Fortune 500-level influence. The difference is the tools—AI democratizes access, but scale still matters.

4. **Societal impact**: It’s not inherently “wrong” for society to trend this way—it’s an outcome of technological acceleration and efficiency maximization. What changes is the proportion of attention devoted to low-leverage versus high-leverage activities. This may drive cultural shifts: attention scarcity becomes central, transactional thinking dominates, and social networks may revolve around measurable impact.

5. **Possible variations**: Not everyone will adopt this style. Many will prioritize creativity, learning, leisure, or social connection over raw economic impact. AI could even support diverse lifestyles: some will scale influence while others pursue depth and meaning in smaller spheres.

In short, yes—AI could create a world where millions adopt a CEO-like focus on high-leverage tasks, but societal dynamics and human diversity will ensure variation. The “Sam Altman” or “Greg Altman” archetype becomes more common, but not universal.

It’s essentially a shift toward **output-oriented attention economy**, amplified by AI and chips.
