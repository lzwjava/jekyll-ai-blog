This is a real dynamic worth thinking clearly about, not just accepting cynically.

**The core claim is basically true, with a specific mechanism.**

Relationships in a hierarchy are held together by two different things: personal rapport and structural incentive. When they point the same direction, things feel warm and genuine. When they conflict, structural incentive usually wins — because B's own position depends on A, not on C. B executing A's order isn't necessarily "betrayal" in the moral sense; from B's side it's often just risk management. Refusing A to protect C puts B's own job/standing on the line for someone who has *no formal power over B's outcomes*.

This is why "investing in relationships" up the chain (with the person who has power over your outcomes) is structurally different from investing sideways or down. It's not that lateral relationships are worthless — they affect how much friction, guilt, or hesitation exists in the moment B is asked to hurt C. A person with strong loyalty to C might slow-walk it, warn C in advance, do it with visible reluctance, or push back once. But rarely will they *refuse outright* if the cost to themselves is high enough. Effort changes the manner and cost of betrayal, not whether it happens.

**A few refinements to the model, since you framed it in absolutes:**

1. **It's probabilistic, not deterministic.** The relationship investment shifts the threshold — how big does the interest conflict need to be before B throws C under the bus. Strong trust raises that threshold; it doesn't remove it.

2. **Repeated games change this.** If B and C interact repeatedly and B's reputation among peers (not just with A) matters, betraying C too easily becomes costly in its own right — other people learn B is unreliable sideways, which eventually reaches A too, since A also needs to trust B. This is why some organizations are actually less brutal than the raw hierarchy model predicts — reputation is its own hierarchy.

3. **A's power over B is what matters, not A's rank per se.** If A doesn't actually control B's compensation/promotion/survival, the "order" carries less weight. Formal rank charts often lag real power structures.

4. **This is exactly why some people build outside hierarchies.** You've done this yourself — running your own company means you're not exposed to this dynamic as an employee. It's a rational response to the pattern you're describing, not paranoia.

**Practical implication for how you operate:** don't rely on goodwill from people who have a boss who can override them regarding you. Rely on it from people who are structurally aligned with you (co-founders, direct reports whose interests are tied to yours, or people outside the hierarchy entirely). For everyone else, goodwill is a modifier on behavior at the margin, not a guarantee.
