Question: **Is the U.S. legal system / rule of law better than China’s?**

Answer: **If by “better” you mean stronger protection against arbitrary government power and stronger individual legal rights, I would say yes: the U.S. system is substantially stronger.** But that does **not** mean “U.S. courts are always better” or “the U.S. legal system is more efficient.”

The key distinction is **what the legal system is optimized for**.

| Dimension                            | 🇺🇸 United States                        | 🇨🇳 China                                                 |
| ------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Judicial independence                | **Stronger**                              | More constrained by political/state structure              |
| Government vs individual             | **Strong checks and balances**            | State has greater institutional power                      |
| Freedom of speech                    | **Very strong constitutional protection** | Much more restricted                                       |
| Due process                          | **Strong constitutional protection**      | Exists, but practical protection is weaker                 |
| Property/business disputes           | Strong                                    | Generally strong for ordinary commercial matters           |
| Contract enforcement                 | Strong                                    | Strong and improving                                       |
| Government regulation                | More legally contestable                  | Government has broader discretion                          |
| Criminal defense                     | Stronger procedural protections           | Less adversarial                                           |
| Court cost                           | **Very expensive**                        | Generally cheaper                                          |
| Litigation speed                     | Often slow                                | Often faster                                               |
| Predictability for ordinary business | Generally high                            | Can be high, but policy/regulatory changes can matter more |

The important part is **institutional structure**.

### 1. The U.S. system puts law above the government

The American model is essentially:

```text
Constitution
    ↓
Legislature ── Executive
      ↕            ↕
    Courts ─────────
```

The government itself can be challenged in court.

For example, the U.S. Constitution's Due Process Clause says the government cannot deprive a person of life, liberty, or property without due process, and the Equal Protection Clause imposes additional limits on government discrimination. ([Congress.gov][1])

This is a very important difference.

If you have a dispute with a government agency in the U.S., the legal question can genuinely become:

```text
Government: "We have authority to do X."

Citizen:
    "No.
     The Constitution/statute doesn't give you that authority.
     I'll sue you."

Federal/state court:
    "Let's determine whether the government is legally allowed to do X."
```

That mechanism is one of the core strengths of the American system.

---

### 2. China also has courts and rule of law — but the institutional relationship is different

China's Constitution explicitly says people's courts independently exercise adjudicatory power and should not be interfered with by administrative organs. ([Ministry of Justice][2])

But the courts are still part of the **state institutional structure** and the Supreme People's Court is responsible to the National People's Congress and its Standing Committee. ([National People's Congress][3])

So there is an important distinction between:

> **judicial independence in law**

and

> **judicial independence as a system of checks and balances against the political leadership.**

The latter is much stronger in the U.S.

---

### 3. But don't romanticize America

This is where many people get the wrong idea.

The U.S. legal system has **serious problems**.

The 2025 World Justice Project index ranks the U.S. **27th out of 143 countries** overall—not remotely "perfect." ([World Justice Project][4])

And one particularly interesting weakness is **access to justice**.

The U.S. civil-justice score was 0.62, but its "people can access and afford civil justice" subfactor scored only **0.47**, ranking **112/143**. ([World Justice Project][5])

In practice:

```text
US legal system

        strong rights
             ↑
             │
      ┌──────┴──────┐
      │             │
 independent      expensive
 courts           lawyers
      │             │
      │             ↓
      └──────→ litigation
                   │
                   ↓
              very expensive
```

If you have $500 million, excellent lawyers, and ten years of runway, the American legal system can be incredibly powerful.

If you have $5,000 and need a lawyer, **very different story**.

The WJP also gives the U.S. only **0.58** for criminal justice, with particularly poor scores for criminal-system impartiality. ([World Justice Project][6])

So "America has better rule of law" ≠ "America always treats everyone better."

## 4. The biggest difference for someone like you

For an **engineer / entrepreneur / AI company founder**, I think the more relevant comparison is:

### China

You can often operate very efficiently:

```text
build product
   ↓
hire people
   ↓
sign contracts
   ↓
sell
   ↓
move fast
```

Chinese commercial law has become quite sophisticated.

But you generally have **less institutional protection against sudden changes in government policy or administrative power**.

### U.S.

You have a stronger framework for:

```text
property rights
IP
contracts
corporate governance
shareholder rights
employment agreements
constitutional rights
government accountability
```

And if someone violates your legal rights, the court system can be a genuinely independent counterweight.

That's extremely valuable for entrepreneurs.

## 5. One way to think about it

Imagine the government itself becomes your opponent.

### China

Conceptually:

```text
You
 │
 ▼
Court
 │
 ▼
State legal system
```

The court is part of the state system.

### U.S.

Conceptually:

```text
You
 │
 ▼
Independent court
 │
 ├── Government
 ├── Corporation
 ├── Police
 ├── Agency
 └── Individual
```

The court can potentially tell **the government itself**:

> "You cannot do that."

That is the fundamental advantage I'm talking about.

## My bottom line

If you're asking:

> **"Which country has a stronger rule-of-law system for protecting an individual from arbitrary government power?"**

**USA, clearly.**

If you're asking:

> **"Which country has cheaper/faster/easier courts for ordinary commercial disputes?"**

**China can often be better.**

If you're asking:

> **"Which country gives me stronger long-term institutional protection as a technology entrepreneur?"**

I'd generally choose **the U.S.**, especially once your assets, IP, company equity, contracts, and international business become significant.

But the U.S. tradeoff is substantial:

**stronger rights + stronger checks on government + stronger litigation culture → much higher legal complexity and cost.**

That tradeoff is one of the things you should understand before deciding whether moving to the U.S. is actually worth it.
