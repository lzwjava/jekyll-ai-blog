Question: **How did DJI actually get founded, and what was the founder story?**

Answer: DJI is a particularly good founder story because it **didn't start as “let's build a drone company.”** It started as one engineer obsessing over a technical problem.

### 1. Frank Wang was obsessed with flying machines

Frank Wang grew up in Hangzhou/Shenzhen and became fascinated with model aircraft.

At 16, his parents gave him an RC helicopter after he did well on an exam. He crashed it, and replacement parts took months to arrive. That experience apparently stuck with him. His childhood dream was essentially:

> build a flying machine that can autonomously fly and carry a camera.

He later entered HKUST and studied electronic engineering. ([Forbes][1])

### 2. The key moment was actually a university project

In his senior year, Wang worked on a **helicopter flight-control system**.

This is the interesting part:

```text
RC helicopter
      ↓
"Why can't this thing stabilize itself?"
      ↓
flight-control computer
      ↓
autonomous hovering
      ↓
better flight controller
      ↓
DJI
```

His team managed to demonstrate automatic hovering in January 2006. Professor Li Zexiang noticed that Wang had unusually strong technical understanding and leadership, and subsequently became an advisor/investor in DJI. ([Forbes][1])

That is probably the **real origin of DJI**: not drones as a business category, but **flight control as an engineering obsession**.

### 3. He tried Hong Kong first — and failed

Wang founded DJI in **2006**, initially while at HKUST.

He wanted to establish the startup in Hong Kong, but funding/support and operating constraints made it difficult. He eventually moved the operation across the border to **Shenzhen**. ([South China Morning Post][2])

This turned out to be enormously important.

Shenzhen gave him:

```text
HKUST engineering talent
        +
Shenzhen electronics supply chain
        +
cheap prototyping
        +
manufacturing
        +
hardware suppliers
        =
DJI
```

This is one of the strongest examples of **geography being part of a startup's technology stack**.

### 4. The first DJI wasn't selling consumer drones

This is another misconception.

Early DJI was basically a **B2B flight-control company**.

Wang and his classmates moved into a tiny Shenzhen office/apartment and built flight controllers and related components. One early controller reportedly sold for around **RMB 50,000 (~US$6,000)** while costing roughly RMB 15,000 to build. Customers included universities and power companies building their own UAVs. ([Forbes][1])

So:

```text
2006
flight-control R&D
        ↓
2007–2011
sell components/controllers
        ↓
accumulate engineering + cash
        ↓
integrate everything
        ↓
2013 Phantom
        ↓
mass consumer market
```

That's actually a very smart hardware startup progression.

### 5. Then came the huge insight: don't sell the controller — sell the robot

Initially DJI could sell:

> "Here is a really good flight controller. Build your own aircraft."

But eventually the company realized:

> **Why make customers assemble the aircraft? We can build the whole thing.**

That led toward the **ready-to-fly drone**.

Then in 2013 DJI launched the **Phantom**.

That was the inflection point.

Before Phantom:

```text
drone = engineering hobby
       + soldering
       + DIY
       + RC knowledge
```

After Phantom:

```text
drone =
take out of box
      ↓
charge
      ↓
fly
      ↓
take beautiful aerial photos
```

That's a **mass-market product transformation**, not merely a better drone.

SCMP reports DJI's Phantom became its first major mass-market hit in 2013. ([South China Morning Post][3])

### 6. The company was extremely rough in the beginning

The romantic version is:

> genius student → builds drone → becomes billionaire.

The real story was much messier.

Wang was extremely demanding and reportedly had a difficult management style. Forbes described him as an abrasive perfectionist, and much of the original founding team eventually left. ([Forbes][1])

He also had serious problems with employee departures and IP theft. In one case, former employees allegedly copied DJI's flight-controller technology. Wang described this as one of the major difficulties of the early company. ([Forbes][4])

So DJI wasn't born as a polished startup.

It was closer to:

```text
young engineer
   ↓
obsessive prototype
   ↓
tiny team
   ↓
customers barely keep company alive
   ↓
technical breakthrough
   ↓
iterate relentlessly
   ↓
product-market fit
   ↓
vertical integration
   ↓
global dominance
```

### 7. And the scale-up was insane

By 2017, DJI reportedly had around **14,000 employees**, offices around the world, and approximately **¥18B (~US$2.7B) in sales**. ([South China Morning Post][5])

That's roughly:

```text
2006: student project
        ↓
2007: tiny hardware startup
        ↓
2013: Phantom
        ↓
2015: global drone leader
        ↓
2017: ¥18B revenue
        ↓
2026: one of the world's dominant civilian-drone companies
```

And Forbes still lists Wang as DJI's founder/chairman, with an estimated **$5.1B net worth as of September 2026**. ([Forbes][6])

### The most interesting lesson

I think the deepest part of the DJI story isn't **"Frank Wang built a drone company."**

It's this:

**He started with a hard technical capability that he personally understood extremely deeply.**

```text
technical obsession
       ↓
flight controller
       ↓
component business
       ↓
system integration
       ↓
consumer product
       ↓
ecosystem
       ↓
global company
```

That pattern is very different from:

```text
"I want to start a company"
        ↓
find market
        ↓
make pitch deck
        ↓
raise money
        ↓
hire engineers
```

DJI is much closer to the **engineer → technology → product → company** path.

And that's why I think DJI's founding story is particularly relevant to someone building **AI infrastructure/agents**: the initial thing doesn't necessarily need to be a huge company idea. It can be a **technical capability that is 10× better than what most people can build**, followed by progressively moving up the stack. ([Forbes][1])
