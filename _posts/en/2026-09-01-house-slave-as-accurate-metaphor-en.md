**Question:** Is the Chinese term "house slave" (房奴, fángnú) an accurate description of the phenomenon it refers to?

**Answer:**

Yes, it's a pretty apt and vivid term — and it resonates far beyond China.

**房奴 (fángnú)** literally means "house slave," and it refers to homeowners in China who spend more than 70% of their disposable income repaying mortgage loans.

The metaphor of *slavery* captures several real constraints:

- A "house slave" dare not easily change jobs, dare not spend on entertainment or travel, and worries constantly about falling ill or losing their income — essentially unable to enjoy life freely, all in service of the mortgage.

- Continually rising real estate prices and strong cultural incentives for homeownership push people into restrictive loans, leaving them with little money to invest or improve their standard of living.

- Studies show that over 70% of new homeowners in China felt extremely burdened and unhappy after buying — they described themselves on social media as feeling like a slave to their house due to excessive monthly payments.

The term also reflects uniquely Chinese pressures: in China, housing is tied to the *hukou* (户口) system, which links residency registration — and access to schools, public services, and social benefits — to property ownership, making the pressure to buy far more intense than in most other countries.

The concept is hardly unique to China though. "Mortgage slave" or "house slave" resonates globally wherever housing costs consume a crushing share of income — whether in Los Angeles, London, or Sydney. The Chinese term just gave it a blunt, honest name.
