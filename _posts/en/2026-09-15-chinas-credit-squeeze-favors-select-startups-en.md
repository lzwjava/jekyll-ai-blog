**Question: Is China’s current liquidity/credit contraction a good time to start a business?**

**Answer: Yes — but only for a particular type of business.**

My read of China in **September 2026** is:

> **Bad environment for capital-intensive, domestic-demand-dependent startups. Very interesting environment for cash-flow-positive, productivity-enhancing, export-oriented businesses.**

The distinction matters more than “bull vs bear.”

### 1. First, I’d slightly correct the premise: this is not simply “no liquidity”

China's monetary system is still being supported. The PBOC says it is maintaining a moderately loose policy, including potential RRR/rate tools. ([State Council of China][1])

But **credit transmission is broken/weak**.

The latest August data are pretty striking:

* New RMB loans: only **¥60B**, versus **¥400B expected**
* Jan–Aug new loans: **¥10.44T**, down from ¥13.46T a year earlier
* Outstanding RMB loans: only **+4.9% YoY**, a record-low growth rate
* M2: **+7.5%**
* TSF: **+7.2%**
* Household borrowing contracted for the **sixth consecutive month** ([Reuters][2])

So the interesting phenomenon is:

```text
                     Liquidity
                         ↓
              ┌──────────────────┐
              │      Banks       │
              │  have liquidity  │
              └────────┬─────────┘
                       ↓
                "Who wants it?"
                       ↓
              households / SMEs
                       ↓
                    weak
                       ↓
              credit multiplier ↓
```

That's very different from a classic banking liquidity crisis.

China has money. **People don't have enough confidence in future returns to borrow and invest.**

---

## 2. And that's exactly why starting a company can be attractive

In a liquidity expansion:

```text
cheap money
    ↓
more competitors
    ↓
asset prices ↑
    ↓
talent expensive
    ↓
CAC expensive
    ↓
everyone raises money
```

In a liquidity/credit contraction:

```text
weak demand
    ↓
companies cut costs
    ↓
talent becomes available
    ↓
vendors become negotiable
    ↓
competitors disappear
    ↓
customers demand ROI
    ↓
efficient companies gain share
```

The opportunity isn't:

> "Let's build something and wait for the next bull market."

It's:

> **"What painful economic problem becomes much more valuable when everyone is short of money?"**

That is a much better startup filter.

# 3. I'd divide opportunities into four quadrants

|                       | Domestic demand | Export/global |
| --------------------- | --------------: | ------------: |
| **Capital intensive** |               ❌ |            ⚠️ |
| **Capital light**     |              ⚠️ |        **🔥** |

### ❌ Avoid

Things requiring:

* lots of inventory
* real estate
* restaurants/physical stores
* consumer discretionary spending
* long sales cycles
* huge upfront hiring
* VC funding to survive
* 3–5 years before revenue
* dependence on property recovery

Property is still a major drag: a recent Reuters survey expects **China property investment to fall ~20% in 2026** and floor-space sales ~10%. ([Reuters][3])

That's not the environment I'd choose to build a property-adjacent startup.

# 4. But AI is unusually interesting right now

This is where I'd be much more bullish.

China currently has a weird combination:

```text
AI capability ↑↑
GPU / compute constraints ↑
labor cost pressure ↑
corporate margins ↓
management pressure ↑
credit availability ↓
```

That creates a very strong demand function:

> **"Can you reduce 10 people's work to 2 people?"**

Much easier to sell than:

> "Here's a cool AI product."

And there is evidence that AI/high-tech exports are one of the healthier parts of the economy. August exports rose **25% YoY**, while high-tech exports rose **42.9%**, with semiconductors and autos particularly strong. ([Reuters][4])

So I'd particularly like:

### AI × cost reduction

Examples:

```text
sales agents
customer support
software engineering
QA
data labeling
document processing
procurement
finance ops
legal ops
manufacturing intelligence
```

But not generic "AI SaaS."

I'd sell:

> **¥X/month saved**

rather than:

> **AI-powered workflow platform**

# 5. The really interesting opportunity: sell Chinese engineering globally

This is where I think the macro situation becomes asymmetric.

China has:

```text
huge engineering labor pool
        +
excellent manufacturing ecosystem
        +
cheap/abundant software talent
        +
AI capability
        +
weak domestic demand
```

Meanwhile outside China:

```text
high software salaries
+
AI adoption pressure
+
companies desperate for productivity
```

That creates an arbitrage.

Conceptually:

```text
US/EU customer
      │
      │ $100k problem
      ↓
your company
      │
      ├── AI agents
      ├── Chinese engineering
      └── automation
      │
      ↓
$20k-$40k delivery cost
```

The important part is **not outsourcing**.

It's:

> **AI-native delivery company**

where humans + agents produce something that previously required a 10–50 person team.

That's much more interesting.

# 6. For someone with your skill set, I'd be even more aggressive

Given that you're already building agents/model infrastructure rather than learning AI from scratch, I wouldn't optimize for:

> "What startup should I start?"

I'd optimize for:

> **"Where is there currently a giant mismatch between the cost of a human process and the cost of an agent?"**

Then attack it.

For example:

```text
Customer pays       $50k/year

Old solution:
5 humans × $30k
= $150k/year

AI-native solution:
1 operator
+ 20 agents
+ infrastructure
= $30k/year

Gross value created
≈ $120k/year
```

You don't need a giant market.

You need **10 customers with $50k ACV**.

That's a ¥3–4M-ish business.

Then 100 customers becomes serious.

# 7. The biggest trap right now

Don't confuse **cheap assets** with **cheap opportunities**.

China has lots of things that look cheap:

```text
office space
factories
hardware
talent
companies
inventory
```

But the most valuable thing is still **distribution**.

In a liquidity contraction:

> **Customer acquisition becomes more important than technology.**

Because technically excellent products can die if nobody has budget.

So I'd actually run the business backwards:

```text
1. Find customer with budget
2. Find painful recurring workflow
3. Sell outcome
4. Deliver manually
5. Agentize it
6. Productize
7. Scale
```

Not:

```text
1. Build model
2. Build agent framework
3. Build SaaS
4. Launch
5. Hope
```

## My actual conclusion

**Yes, I think this can be a very good time to start a company — but a bad time to start a company that requires the macro environment to improve.**

The ideal startup in this environment has:

```text
              STARTUP

      low initial capital
              +
      revenue immediately
              +
      measurable ROI
              +
      low domestic demand exposure
              +
      AI leverage
              +
      global customers
              +
      tiny team
```

In other words:

> **Don't bet on China recovering. Build something that becomes more valuable because China is weak.**

That's a much stronger macro position.

And there's an especially interesting second-order effect: **China's domestic weakness is pushing capital, talent and companies toward exports**, while global demand for Chinese high-tech products is still strong. ([Reuters][4])

If I were allocating **your next 12 months**, I'd seriously investigate the **China → global AI/software arbitrage** rather than another purely domestic AI product. ([Financial Times][6])
