Question: **How has the Bay Area job market changed in recent years, especially for tech/AI engineers?**

Answer:

The short version: **the Bay Area tech market got much harder from 2022–2024, but AI has created a very strong new hiring cycle from 2024–2026.** It is not the old “every software engineer gets 5 offers” market anymore. It is becoming a **high-skill, AI-heavy, extremely selective market**.

### 1. The rough timeline

| Period        | Bay Area tech market                                                               |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **2020–2021** | 🔥 Huge hiring boom, remote work, cheap money                                      |
| **2022**      | 🔥 Still strong early, then Fed tightening                                         |
| **2023**      | 🥶 Layoffs, hiring freezes, normalization                                          |
| **2024**      | 🟡 Recovery, but much more selective                                               |
| **2025**      | 🟢 AI becomes major hiring engine                                                  |
| **2026**      | 🟢/🔥 AI infrastructure + frontier-model ecosystem is driving another strong cycle |

The broader Bay Area economy lost more than **280,000 jobs between 2019 and 2021**, then recovered toward roughly **4.1M jobs in 2024**. ([Vital Signs][1])

But the interesting part is **where the recovery happened**.

---

## 2. AI has fundamentally changed the Bay Area market

This is the biggest story.

CBRE's 2025 data shows:

* Bay Area AI tech talent: **76,079**
* up from **61,497** one year earlier
* roughly **+24%**
* Bay Area AI-related postings reached **42% of tech postings**
* AI postings reached **11,400**, a record at the time of the report. ([CBRE][2])

Compare that with the overall US tech market:

> US tech employment grew only **1.1% in 2024**, versus **7% growth in 2022**.

So this isn't another broad-based 2021-style software hiring boom.

It's much more like:

**old tech hiring ↓
AI hiring ↑↑↑**

CBRE ranks the Bay Area as the **#1 North American tech-talent market**. ([CBRE][3])

---

## 3. The Bay Area is becoming disproportionately AI-heavy

This is what makes the Bay Area different from, say, Austin or even NYC.

The region has an enormous concentration of:

* OpenAI
* Anthropic
* Google DeepMind
* Meta AI
* xAI
* NVIDIA ecosystem
* Scale AI
* Databricks
* Cursor/Anysphere
* Perplexity
* robotics startups
* AI infrastructure startups
* frontier-model startups
* AI-native application companies

And the capital concentration is enormous.

CBRE reported that the Bay Area had attracted roughly **three-quarters of US AI VC funding since 2019** in its 2025 analysis. ([CBRE][2])

So the Bay Area isn't merely a place where AI companies exist.

It has become something closer to:

**capital → researchers → engineers → founders → models → startups → capital**

That feedback loop is extremely powerful.

# 5. AI is raising the bar rather than simply eliminating jobs

This is an important 2026 observation.

Despite all the predictions about AI destroying software jobs, there hasn't been evidence of mass AI-driven unemployment yet. Recent research discussed by Anthropic and economists found **no systematic increase in unemployment among highly AI-exposed workers since late 2022**. ([The Guardian][4])

Instead:

```text
before:

Engineer
 ├── write code
 ├── debug
 ├── tests
 └── deploy


now:

Engineer
 ├── use AI
 ├── design system
 ├── supervise agents
 ├── evaluate outputs
 ├── debug AI-generated code
 ├── understand models
 └── ship much faster
```

So companies can hire fewer people **while expecting much more output from each person**.

That's why the market can simultaneously have:

> fewer openings

and

> extremely high salaries for exceptional engineers.

# 6. Remote work also changed dramatically

This is another major Bay Area shift.

In June 2025, only about **10% of Bay Area tech postings were remote**, down from **24% in mid-2022**.

AI companies were particularly likely to require employees to work in the office. ([CBRE][5])

This is actually important if you're thinking about moving there.

The Bay Area's competitive advantage isn't only salary.

It's:

```text
OpenAI
Anthropic
Google
Meta
xAI
Cursor
AI startups
      ↓
same physical ecosystem
      ↓
researchers + engineers + founders + investors
      ↓
information/network effects
```

That is difficult to reproduce remotely.

# 7. San Francisco itself is recovering

Interestingly, SF has been doing much better than the 2022–2023 narrative suggested.

San Francisco's seasonally adjusted unemployment rate was **4.1% in June 2026**, and the city added approximately **14,500 jobs year-over-year**. ([Axios][6])

There's also evidence that the center of gravity of the technology ecosystem has shifted somewhat **north toward San Francisco proper**, rather than being exclusively Palo Alto/South Bay.

Recent commentary points to companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic driving this concentration. ([Business Insider][7])

So today I'd think of:

```text
San Francisco
   ↕
South Bay / Palo Alto
   ↕
Mountain View
   ↕
Sunnyvale
   ↕
Santa Clara / San Jose
```

as one giant labor market rather than separate ecosystems.

## 9. The brutal part: competition at the top is insane

There's another side.

The Bay Area contains:

* Stanford PhDs
* Berkeley PhDs
* ex-Google researchers
* ex-Meta researchers
* ex-OpenAI researchers
* ex-Anthropic researchers
* people who built CUDA kernels
* people who wrote major ML papers
* exceptional startup engineers

So you're not competing against the median US software engineer.

For frontier AI roles, you're competing against **the global top few percent of technical talent**.

But there's a useful asymmetry:

**you don't necessarily need to beat the frontier researchers.**

There are huge numbers of jobs between:

```text
CRUD SWE
        ↓
AI application engineer
        ↓
AI systems engineer
        ↓
ML infrastructure
        ↓
training/inference engineer
        ↓
research engineer
        ↓
frontier research scientist
```

The middle of this ladder is probably particularly attractive.

Not literal employment counts — just the **relative opportunity direction**.

The important transition is:

**2021:** "tech is hot"

→ **2023:** "tech crashed"

→ **2025–26:** "AI is hot, but only certain kinds of technical talent are extremely hot."

And that last distinction is crucial.

For someone capable of **actually training a 760M model, working with GPUs/ROCm/CUDA, understanding PyTorch internals, building agents, and shipping production systems**, I would consider the **2026 Bay Area market substantially more interesting than the 2023 market**.

The hard part isn't whether there is demand.

The hard part is getting **US work authorization / immigration + getting through the extremely selective hiring funnel**.
