Reflection helps us make a better start. Although many of my old friends already know me well, let me take another look back at the past five years of hard work.

In the summer of 2014, I earned ¥4,000 a month as an intern. By 2018, my software outsourcing business had generated ¥3 million in annual revenue. It took roughly four and a half years to make that journey.

Over the past year or so, I've taken things a little easier. I haven't worked as hard as I used to. But I've accumulated methods, experience, and friendships over the years. If I put my mind to it, I believe I can still make some money. Let's review what I've learned along the way.

It was the summer of 2014 when I joined LeanCloud, an internet startup, as an intern. If I remember correctly, the internship paid around ¥4,000 a month. It was the first time I'd earned money through a job, and I was thrilled to receive my first paycheck.

As an intern, I wrote code, fixed bugs, and prepared documentation for the company. I worked from 10 a.m. to 8 p.m. I was based at the Kunshan office, where I didn't have many friends. I'd often stay at the office until one or two in the morning before heading home to sleep. I remember that my rented apartment cost just ¥350 a month.

At the time, I was a pretty typical programmer: focused on technology, with few ambitions outside work, and fairly casual about life in general.

The company was excellent. The founder had a PhD from Yale and had previously worked at Google. My managers were great, too, and many of my colleagues had worked at Alibaba, Baidu, or Wandoujia. It was a small, high-quality team with a strong reputation in the industry.

Perhaps my managers noticed that I volunteered to work overtime every day and had a good attitude. Although my coding skills weren't particularly strong and I wasn't careful enough with my work, they decided to offer me a full-time position.

It was a great company, and they offered me a salary of ¥14,000 a month.

I told the founder that it was too much money for me.

He explained that the company had a transparent compensation plan and treated everyone equally. All I needed to do was keep improving my skills.

I was incredibly fortunate to have met such a good founder.

At the time, I'd only just finished my first year of university. My parents and I argued constantly. Several times, I cried while asking them to let me try working. I genuinely didn't want to keep skipping classes to write code anymore. I'd found such a good company and such a great opportunity, and I wanted to give it a shot.

My uncle eventually called to persuade them, too. My father even contacted one of his entrepreneurial friends to talk me out of it. But that friend ended up persuading my father instead, saying, "Your son seems pretty capable. Let him give it a try."

Eventually, we reached an agreement: I could take a year off from university to work.

I cherished this hard-won opportunity. After work, I'd voluntarily put in extra hours because my colleagues had years more experience than I did, and my abilities were nowhere near theirs.

To prove to my parents that the company really did offer good compensation, and that taking a year off was the right decision, I transferred ¥5,000 to my father every month during the first six months or so.

I'd ride my electric scooter to work every day and spend my time writing code.

The company had initially been selected for the Microsoft Accelerator program and had worked out of its offices for a while. Microsoft Accelerator even gave us temporary employee badges.

My performance reviews during my first few quarters were pretty poor. Out of more than 20 people at the company, I ranked near the bottom.

I felt deeply embarrassed and ashamed.

One day, I approached my managers. I almost started crying again, unable to hold back my emotions. I asked them whether I was holding everyone back and apologized for not being good enough. I told them I could return to school or even accept a pay cut if necessary. They shouldn't feel awkward about it, I said. I would find a way to take care of myself after leaving the company.

My managers later met to discuss my situation. They decided to help me improve my coding skills by spending more time mentoring me, and they still wanted me to stay.

I was incredibly grateful.

I secretly bought some books and decided to work harder. I'm still not sure whether my managers felt sorry for me or whether my skills had genuinely improved, but eventually my performance reviews began placing me around the middle of the pack.

A few months passed, bringing us to November 2015.

Ye Gucheng, a well-known figure in the iOS developer community, visited our office. We started talking about a startup idea.

I'd read books such as Hackers & Painters and quite a few articles by Paul Graham, the legendary Silicon Valley entrepreneur and co-founder of Y Combinator. Beijing also had a vibrant startup scene at the time. The idea excited me, and I wanted to give entrepreneurship a try.

But the company had treated me so well. What should I do?

In the end, I chose to follow my heart.

When I resigned, I remember it being announced at what was probably a birthday party. I couldn't help shedding tears. It was my first time leaving a job.

I'd found so many wonderful colleagues there. We played table tennis together during lunch and reviewed one another's code. We had such a great founder.

I was torn between my own ambitions and a team that had treated me like family. I felt terrible about leaving.

I'm grateful for that experience. I met so many talented colleagues and made friends at neighboring companies in the accelerator. I learned a great deal technically, and I also worked on several open-source projects during that period.

So Gucheng and I started working on our startup idea.

My experience had primarily been in mobile development, particularly Android and iOS. We wanted to build a website for code review. I had no experience with frontend or backend web development, so what did I do? I learned it as I went.

Gucheng later told me that he'd only been casually discussing the startup idea with me. He never expected me to resign just two weeks later.

He reached out to some friends, and together we began building the project.

Eventually, we launched it. We attracted several thousand users, primarily iOS engineers, and held an offline event on March 13, 2016.

The following day, I went to the United States.

I visited Y Combinator, the startup accelerator founded by Paul Graham. I secretly slipped inside and met the team behind an incubated startup called Mailtime, whose founder was Huang He.

I believe it was the day before Demo Day. Everyone was rehearsing their presentations for the next day's startup showcase.

After the rehearsals, the startup founders and prominent mentors began chatting. I wanted to get closer and listen in.

Unfortunately, I was discovered.

One of the prominent mentors asked who I was. I explained that I was from China, had admired them for a long time, and wanted to learn from them.

He said, "Please go out."

I said, "Okay."

But he insisted on escorting me all the way outside. Only after I'd completely left did he say, "OK."

A few days later, Huang He told me that the mentor had even written to all the members of the YC accelerator, warning them to be cautious about people like me because we might leak startup ideas or other confidential information.

A few days later, I contacted Huang He and asked whether I could work at his company. I ended up spending a short week there. We had lunch together every day in San Francisco, tried all sorts of interesting Western food, and I made a few more friends.

That weekend, I attended a peer-learning and programming meetup in San Francisco.

Eventually, I decided that I still wanted to build my own startup, so I returned to China and continued working with Gucheng. We started running small-group training classes.

By June 2016, however, I was feeling quite lost. I wasn't sure what to do next.

I briefly returned to employment, joining a startup called Paidian, founded by Ye Fei, a former vice president at Jumei who was starting another business.

I worked on a project called Paipaijiang, began learning Go, and implemented features such as red-envelope giveaways and transaction processing. Whenever the company's investors visited, I'd look for opportunities to chat with them.

After a little over a month, I found myself back in the familiar routine of working overtime and writing code every day. It was fulfilling, and the founder was paying me ¥25,000 a month.

That was an incredible salary for someone my age. I was only 21, roughly the age of a third-year university student.

Paidian was still in its early stages and exploring its business model. As the days went by, my entrepreneurial fire began burning again.

I eventually registered Beijing Square Root Technology Co., Ltd. and started building Fun Live (趣直播), a livestreaming platform for sharing technical knowledge within the internet industry.

I built both the frontend and backend of the website myself.

At the time, I had around 600 WeChat contacts. I wrote an article titled something like "Achieving Small Goals with a 21-Year-Old CEO," telling the story of my journey so far and looking for a co-founder.

The article received around 15,000 views. Many friends, along with Yuyan's classmates and teachers, helped share it.

We'd built a livestreaming platform, but someone had to actually do the broadcasting.

So I started streaming myself.

After a few sessions, I invited well-known friends from the tech community and engineers from major technology companies to give talks.

After about a year, the platform had accumulated hundreds of thousands of visits and nearly 30,000 users, most of them engineers. Our official WeChat account had also reached around 10,000 followers.

I didn't think much of these numbers at the time. Looking back, I realize what a valuable asset that audience was, especially in today's world, where attention is so expensive.

At the time, I lacked experience and handled almost everything myself: engineering, product development, and operations. My energy was limited, too.

Later, I helped Ye Fei find a technical lead and paid a ¥10,000 recruitment fee. His company also transferred ¥50,000 to mine.

I published an article to generate publicity, masking the investment amount because I was afraid my friends would laugh at me.

About a month later, Li Xiaolai invested ¥500,000 in our company.

I realized that I couldn't keep doing everything alone. I wasn't good at management, so I began hiring two teammates, followed by several classmates and friends.

Eventually, we had a team of nine.

Some people handled operations and received salaries of ¥5,000 a month. I worried that this wasn't enough, so I raised their salaries to ¥5,500.

Many were recent graduates and former classmates. I was concerned that they might struggle to afford rent, so I gave everyone a ¥3,000 housing allowance when they joined.

We also moved into an office in Wangjing, Beijing, paying ¥13,000 a month in rent.

Together, we organized a technology community event called the Developer Growth and Exchange Conference.

The venue cost more than ¥7,000, the buffet cost several thousand, livestreaming equipment cost several thousand more, and we paid the speakers honorariums.

The total cost exceeded ¥20,000. Ticket sales brought in about ¥20,000, leaving us with a loss of roughly one or two thousand yuan.

As our team grew to nine people, our expenses increased rapidly.

I didn't want everyone to struggle with ordering meals individually, so I decided the company would arrange everyone's food.

Each meal cost ¥35 per person. At two meals a day, that meant ¥70 per person, or ¥630 a day for nine people. Over a month, that added up to around ¥13,000.

Initially, I thought this was simply part of treating my employees well. Only at the end of the month did I realize how significant the expense was.

In just a month and a half, our nine-person company had spent around ¥150,000 on salaries, meals, and office rent.

I was deeply worried. After consulting some friends, I called Wu Xing, an experienced serial entrepreneur. His advice was straightforward: cut our losses immediately.

I later sat down with everyone to discuss the situation.

Our team shrank from nine people to four. Two months later, I was back to working alone.

I'd paid dearly for my own mistakes: being overly optimistic, lacking management experience, and failing to control expenses.

There were many other costs, too. The ¥550,000 we'd raised had dwindled to roughly ¥150,000.

Losing money felt terrible.

I remembered that during the first six months of my startup, my parents had initially given me ¥20,000–30,000 and later supported me with ¥7,000 a month.

I calculated what I'd spent and moved money around in the company accounts so I could return ¥60,000–70,000 to them. My thinking was that even if I eventually lost everything, my parents would still be able to help me one more time.

When you borrow money, you should repay it.

Toward the end of 2017, Ye Fei asked me to return to his company to help rebuild a project.

I wanted to earn back the money I'd lost, and a friend had also approached me with a project, so I took on a second job.

After finishing work at 9 p.m. every day, I'd head to my buddy's place to work together on a livestream quiz app. My buddy and I were responsible for the backend.

The app later became hugely popular and topped the App Store charts for a while. Eventually, the project raised a substantial amount of funding.

That success didn't directly benefit us, but it showed me that software outsourcing might be a viable business model.

At the time, I felt that earning tens or hundreds of thousands of yuan from a software project could be more attractive than trying to sell courses through Fun Live.

So I began transitioning into software outsourcing.

Thanks to my existing network, including the hosts and users I'd met through Fun Live, I started receiving project leads. As demand grew, I recruited many freelance engineers from my network to help deliver the work.

I had accumulated two WeChat accounts filled with engineers and internet-industry professionals through Fun Live, with around 8,000–9,000 contacts in total.

Perhaps half of them were reasonably engaged with what I was doing.

I kept receiving inquiries from friends. I created hundreds of WeChat groups, met with roughly 100 prospective clients, and eventually closed nearly 50 projects.

That was how the business reached ¥3 million in annual revenue, with approximately ¥700,000 in profit.

Our clients included Yuanfudao, Fenbi, Beijing International Studies University, Super Brain, and SinoVision Technology, among others.

Most of the revenue flowed through the company's bank accounts. Running a company involved many transfers, including borrowing money from the company and repaying it, so the balances in my personal bank accounts could also look substantial at times.

We paid a considerable amount of corporate value-added tax, and outsourced our accounting to a professional firm that handled everything through the proper procedures.

Over a little more than a year, I negotiated and managed nearly 50 projects.

Some friends told me this was an impressive achievement. I thought it was reasonably manageable.

If you have ten projects running simultaneously, perhaps three or four are still being negotiated, another three or four are halfway through development, and the remaining projects are nearing delivery and final payment.

That meant the workload wasn't necessarily overwhelming.

I also had two or three dozen designers and engineers helping me deliver the projects.

Starting in early 2019, I began reading more books and reflecting on whether there were other directions worth pursuing.

Software outsourcing was a good business, but repeatedly building similar projects from scratch had become tiresome.

The second million yuan was just as difficult to earn as the first. There wasn't much long-term value accumulating in the business.

Some clients were also extremely demanding, making service delivery emotionally exhausting.

Quite a few of our clients were startup founders. We'd help them build their products, but their businesses wouldn't take off. Seeing all that code sitting unused on their computers felt like such a waste.

Over the following year or so, I started referring projects to a friend's team called Youfan.

They built more than 100 WeChat groups, negotiated around ten projects, and secured projects worth approximately ¥700,000 to ¥1 million in total.

In return, they paid me around ¥50,000–60,000 over time.

They were working hard, so I told them that if they delivered the projects successfully and earned a profit, they could share a reasonable amount with me. I knew how difficult the work was, so I didn't demand much.

They provided excellent service, and the feedback from my friends was positive. Their service quality was comparable to what I'd provided myself.

This arrangement made things much easier for me. I could introduce a client through a WeChat group and leave the delivery to them.

That freed up my time and energy to think about other things.

Their business development skills were strong, and their management philosophy was similar to mine. The founder also had a background and set of skills comparable to my own.

At the end of 2019, I deregistered my company and moved back from Beijing to Guangzhou.

My parents were in Guangzhou. I couldn't afford to buy a home in Beijing, and it was also time to settle down and start a family.

My then-fiancée and I returned to Guangzhou together. We got married in early October, so she's my wife now.

Of the ¥550,000 invested in my company, I'd repaid ¥275,000.

When I closed the company, there was ¥390,000 left in its accounts.

As I mentioned earlier, I'd paid a lot of tuition fees in the school of entrepreneurship. Living in Beijing was expensive, and I hadn't always been frugal when traveling or enjoying myself. When outsourcing clients paid their deposits, I'd sometimes feel flush with cash and treat myself to Haidilao every few days.

Those habits, among other things, contributed to the situation.

Now I finally understand that making money is hard.

We have to plan for the future and think long term. When the weather is sunny, we should prepare for the rain.

We need to live frugally, keep earning, and eventually repay what we owe. We should be honest and do our best to live up to everyone's trust, especially the people who lent or invested their money in us.

Looking back on my journey, my main takeaway is this: take on greater responsibility, do more, build long-term trust with friends, and do every job well.

## Making Money Isn't as Difficult as It Seems

Once you understand how the world works, there are many ways to earn money by meeting other people's needs.

Ask yourself: What are your core strengths? What value can you provide to others? What knowledge and abilities do you possess that are genuinely scarce in the market?

Suppose a company pays an operations specialist ¥8,000 a month.

What results does that person deliver? Could I do the job better?

If that employee gains 1,000 followers for the company, could I gain more?

If I deliver better results while charging only ¥5,000 and working remotely, which person would the employer choose?

Smart employers will recognize that money matters. Once an employee leaves, the relationship often becomes little more than an ordinary friendship.

Helping business owners save money is one way of caring for them and genuinely serving their interests.

The world is harsh. Our jobs can be taken by people who work harder and take more initiative.

There are barriers everywhere.

Without even spending a single yuan, people may no longer bother to like or share your posts. Unless you have a strong relationship with someone, they may not even reply to a WeChat message.

Everyone is working desperately to make money.

Open your WeChat contacts and scroll through the entire list. How many people would actually help you?

If you started selling products through WeChat, how many people would buy them?

Ask yourself honestly. Do you feel how frightening reality can be?

And how many people have we actually helped ourselves?

It's hard, isn't it?

Over the years, I've managed to survive in this world.

Since my second year of university, I've essentially supported myself financially.

With a combination of luck and hard work, I've accumulated more resources, earned the trust of more friends, and gained more knowledge, experience, and practical methods.

I went from earning ¥4,000 a month as an intern to running a business that generated ¥3 million in annual revenue.

Along the way, I've faced countless rejections and closed doors.

Ultimately, I've come to understand more about the world and human nature. I've learned how people respond to different actions, how to become well-liked, how to make money, and how to persuade people to willingly pay for what you offer.

None of this is easy.

You have to treat people well, serve your customers, constantly consider other people's needs, and keep moving forward even when you face rejection and indifference.
