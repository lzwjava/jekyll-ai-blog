Question: Do people generally experience lots of failure and rejection, but become disproportionately afraid of failure?

Answer: **Yes. And there’s an important asymmetry: failure is usually much more common than people’s visible success makes it look.**

For people doing ambitious things—research, startups, engineering, creative work—the process often looks more like:

> try → fail → understand → try again → get rejected → modify → fail differently → eventually succeed

rather than:

> try → succeed.

A few things happen psychologically:

* **Success is highly visible; failure is hidden.** You see the accepted paper, funded startup, shipped product—not the 20 rejected papers, failed prototypes, or unanswered emails.
* **People overestimate the cost of failure.** A rejection often means “this attempt didn't work,” but the brain interprets it as “I am not capable.”
* **Avoiding failure becomes more dangerous than failing.** If you optimize for never being rejected, you naturally choose safer projects, ask fewer ambitious questions, and make fewer attempts.
* **Repeated exposure changes your relationship with failure.** After enough experiments, a failed result becomes information: *hypothesis H was wrong under conditions C*. That's very different from an emotional judgment about yourself.
* **High performers aren't necessarily people who fail less.** Often they're people who can run **more iterations** because each failure is cheaper psychologically and operationally.

This is particularly obvious in science.

A researcher might spend months on an experiment and get a negative result. That's not necessarily wasted work—the result updates the model of the world. The same mindset applies to startups:

```text
10 attempts × 10% probability of success
```

is often much better than:

```text
1 attempt × 100% emotional commitment
```

because the first strategy produces **learning + optionality**.

The deeper principle is:

> **You don't need to become fearless. You need to make failure cheap enough that you can keep going.**

And eventually rejection becomes almost mundane:

```text
rejected
  ↓
why?
  ↓
what did I learn?
  ↓
change one variable
  ↓
try again
```

That is one of the biggest differences between **fear-driven behavior** and **researcher/engineer behavior**.

The goal isn't *“I must not fail.”*

It's:

**“I can survive failure, learn from it, and run the next experiment.”**
