Question: Why does exploring hardware around home feel more interesting after COVID, and will AI make society more digital/home-based?

Answer:

I think your observation matches a real trend, but the future is not simply "everyone stays home." It is more like **the boundary between home, workplace, and laboratory becomes thinner**.

COVID accelerated several things:

1. **Home became a serious production environment**

Before COVID:

* home = rest
* office/factory/lab = creation

After COVID:

* home can be office
* home can be classroom
* home can be AI lab
* home can be electronics workshop

A person with:

* a PC
* GPU
* internet
* AI assistants
* tools

can now do work that previously required a company.

For example, 20 years ago:

* training neural networks required university/company clusters
* electronics repair required a professional shop
* publishing required a publisher

Now:

* one person can train models
* repair GPUs
* publish globally
* build software businesses

The home becomes a "personal factory."

---

2. Hardware exploration fits this era very well

Your interest in:

* broken GPUs
* motherboards
* oscilloscopes
* Arduino/ESP32
* repairing instead of replacing

is actually the physical version of what programmers have always done.

A programmer sees:

```
bug -> inspect -> measure -> modify -> test -> improve
```

A hardware engineer does:

```
broken board -> measure voltage -> inspect signal -> replace component -> test
```

Same mindset.

The interesting thing about AI era is that AI helps bridge the two worlds:

```
AI knowledge
      |
      |
software engineer
      |
      |
hardware understanding
      |
      |
physical world
```

A single person can now understand much more of the stack.

---

3. Going downstairs is underrated

Modern internet culture sometimes creates a strange illusion:

"Everything important happens online."

But humans are physical.

Sunlight, walking, seeing buildings, shops, people, trees, machines — these provide exploration signals.

Many great engineers have this pattern:

* deep computer work
* then walk outside
* observe reality
* return with ideas

The neighborhood can become your "open-world game."

A broken appliance, a computer shop, a factory nearby, a construction site — all are engineering lessons.

---

4. AI probably increases home-based work, but not isolation

I expect:

```
1990s:
office + factory

2000s:
office + internet

2020s:
remote + cloud

2030s:
personal AI + home laboratory
```

But humans will still need:

* customers
* collaboration
* manufacturing
* communities
* travel

The future may look like:

Morning:

* AI agent handles software tasks
* you experiment with hardware

Afternoon:

* meet customers
* visit factories
* go outside

Evening:

* research, learning, building

Less commuting, more autonomy.

---

5. Your situation is actually a good example

You have already experienced:

* Guangdong cities
* USA
* Hong Kong
* Macau

You have enough "world exposure."

The next marginal value may not come from visiting another city.

It may come from going deeper:

```
your apartment
    |
    +-- computer
    |
    +-- GPU
    |
    +-- electronics
    |
    +-- AI models
    |
    +-- local businesses
    |
    +-- people around you
```

A person who can deeply understand one small environment can discover opportunities others miss.

A good analogy:

A traveler sees 100 places superficially.

An engineer spends 3 years studying one machine and discovers how the whole industry works.

Both are exploration.

---

I think the AI era will create a new type of engineer: **the home-based generalist builder**.

Someone who can:

* code
* use AI
* repair hardware
* understand manufacturing
* communicate globally

That combination was difficult before because knowledge was fragmented. AI reduces the cost of connecting those domains.
