The core issue highlighted in the interview aligns with well-established challenges in the global fruit supply chain: fruits picked for long-distance transport (often thousands of kilometers) are harvested at an immature stage to withstand shipping stresses like vibration, temperature fluctuations, and ethylene gas exposure from neighboring produce. ### Why This Happens: Key Factors in Fruit Transportation and Juice Quality

- **Harvest Timing and Maturity**: Fruits like oranges, apples, or berries are picked green or semi-ripe for export to survive 2–4 weeks of transit (e.g., from South America to Asia or Europe). Tree-ripened fruit, by contrast, accumulates optimal soluble solids (measured by Brix levels for sweetness) and enzymes that enhance juiciness and nutrient density.
- **Post-Harvest Degradation**: During transport, even in refrigerated "reefer" containers (maintained at 0–5°C with controlled humidity and gases like CO2 or nitrogen), fruits lose 10–30% quality due to bruising, moisture loss, or microbial growth. This results in lower vitamin C retention (up to 50% loss) and muted flavors in extracted juice.
- **Processing Impact**: Conventional juice production involves pasteurization or concentration, further stripping volatiles. Overmature fruit exacerbates browning (Maillard reactions) and off-flavors, making the juice taste "flat" compared to fresh-squeezed.

In short, the "thousands of kms" journey prioritizes shelf life over taste and nutrition, creating a gap between farm-fresh potential and supermarket reality.
