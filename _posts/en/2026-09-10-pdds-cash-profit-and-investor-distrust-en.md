**Question:** Is your friend basically right that PDD looks absurdly cheap because of its cash pile and ~$100B RMB annual profit, and that foreign investors may distrust it because it doesn't return cash to shareholders?

**Answer:** **The core observation is right, but the “4× P/E after cash” and “foreigners think it's a scam” parts are too simplistic.**

The numbers are genuinely striking:

* PDD reported **~RMB 99.4B net income attributable to ordinary shareholders in 2025**. ([Yahoo Finance][1])
* 2025 revenue was **RMB 431.8B**. ([Stock Titan][2])
* Q2 2026 still produced **RMB 27.2B net income**, despite profit falling 12% YoY. ([Reuters][3])
* And yes, PDD has historically **paid no cash dividend**, and its 2025 20-F says it had no current plan to pay one. ([SEC][4])

So the interesting investment thesis is:

```text
PDD market cap
    ≈ value of operating business
      + enormous financial assets/cash
```

If the market is assigning, say, ~8× earnings to the whole company while the company has hundreds of billions RMB of net cash, then the **implied multiple on the operating business can indeed become dramatically lower**.

But there's an important catch:

### 1. Cash is not automatically worth 100% of face value

The "RMB 400B cash → subtract RMB 400B from market cap" calculation is a useful **net-cash valuation heuristic**, but you have to distinguish:

```text
cash & cash equivalents
+ short-term investments
+ other financial assets
- debt
```

And more importantly, PDD's corporate structure is complicated. Its SEC filing explicitly discusses restrictions and regulatory requirements around moving funds between the offshore holding company, mainland subsidiaries and VIE structure. ([SEC][4])

So an investor shouldn't simply say:

> "PDD has 400B cash, therefore shareholders can immediately receive 400B."

That's not how it works.

### 2. The bigger issue is actually **what happens to future earnings**

This is probably why the market is giving PDD a low multiple.

PDD isn't a boring company generating a stable RMB100B forever.

It has:

```text
Pinduoduo
    ↓
very profitable domestic commerce

Temu
    ↓
huge international opportunity
    ↓
but potentially huge future investment / regulatory / tariff costs
```

And the latest quarter gives investors a reason to be cautious: Q2 revenue grew only 8%, missed expectations, while net income fell 12%. Reuters specifically points to domestic competition and increasing overseas regulatory/tariff pressure on Temu. ([Reuters][3])

So the market isn't necessarily saying:

> "PDD is worth only 4× earnings."

It's closer to:

> **"We don't know how durable today's earnings are, and we're not willing to capitalize them at a high multiple."**

That's a much more reasonable bear case.

### 3. The dividend point is actually very interesting

Your friend's intuition here is good.

Imagine:

```text
PDD:
market cap       = X
net cash         = huge
annual earnings  = ~100B
dividend         = 0
```

A Western investor naturally asks:

> **"If this cash really belongs to shareholders, why isn't management returning it?"**

That's an important governance question.

But **no dividend ≠ no shareholder return**.

A company can return cash through:

```text
dividends
share repurchases
special dividends
acquisitions/investment
organic reinvestment
```

PDD's management has historically preferred retaining capital and investing for growth, particularly internationally.

The crucial question is therefore:

> **Can management eventually convert the enormous retained cash balance into higher per-share intrinsic value?**

If yes, today's low valuation could be very attractive.

If management keeps accumulating cash indefinitely while the operating business deteriorates, then the "cash-rich bargain" thesis becomes much weaker.

---

### The really interesting way to think about PDD

I'd frame it as a **sum-of-the-parts + optionality** situation:

```text
PDD value
=
domestic Pinduoduo cash generation
+
Temu option value
+
net financial assets
-
future investment / regulatory / competitive risk
```

The market is currently assigning a very low value to some combination of those components.

That's why PDD can look **crazy cheap** while simultaneously being a completely rational stock to avoid.

The distinction is:

> **Cheap ≠ mispriced.**

For it to be a great investment, you need to believe that **the market is overestimating the deterioration of PDD's future earnings and/or underestimating the value of its retained cash and Temu option.**

And I would **not** phrase the foreign-investor situation as "they think PDD is a scam." The more sophisticated version is:

> **Investors apply a governance/geopolitical/VIE/liquidity discount because they don't have the same confidence that retained Chinese corporate cash will ultimately be distributed to U.S. shareholders.**

That's a much stronger explanation.
