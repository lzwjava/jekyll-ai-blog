Exactly. Your journey is not just about exploring the world and building a company. It's also about discovering the right people to build it with.

For your long-term goal of becoming a global AI founder, this may be one of the highest-leverage things you can do.

You can learn new technologies, travel to new places, and change business models. But the people you choose as employees, cofounders, and business partners will profoundly influence what kind of company you build—and whether you can sustain it for decades.

The principle associated with 段永平 (Duan Yongping) that fits here is to focus on fundamentals, values, and long-term thinking rather than short-term gains. I would apply that principle to collaboration this way:

## 1. Don't just look for people to hire. Discover people worth building with

There are different levels of collaboration.

Level 1 — Project collaborators

People who can deliver a particular piece of work reliably, communicate clearly, and treat customers fairly.

Level 2 — Long-term teammates

People who learn, take ownership, share information honestly, and become more effective as the team works together.

Level 3 — Potential partners and cofounders

People who share your fundamental values, exercise good judgment independently, handle difficult situations constructively, and want to build something durable.

Not everyone needs to become a cofounder. A good contractor can be a great contractor. A good employee can be a great employee. The objective is to find the right relationship for each person, rather than expecting everyone to share the same ambition.

## 2. Your company should be a filter for finding your people

This is the interesting part: you don't have to identify every great collaborator in advance.

You can design your work so that collaboration reveals character.

For example, publish your principles openly:

- Think about users first.
- Prefer existing open-source solutions when they solve the problem well.
- Be honest about what you know and don't know.
- Use AI to increase leverage, not to hide poor work.
- Think long term; don't sacrifice trust for short-term money.
- Take ownership and communicate problems early.
- Build things you would want to use yourself.

People who resonate with these principles may approach you. People who don't will often self-select out.

Then your actual projects help you distinguish between people who merely agree with your ideas and people who consistently live by them.

This is consistent with Duan Yongping's emphasis on selecting people with aligned values and allowing time to reveal whether a collaboration works.&#x20;

## 3. How I'd filter collaborators in practice

| What to evaluate   | What to observe                                                     |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Integrity          | Do they admit mistakes? Do their claims match reality?              |
| User orientation   | Do they solve the user's problem or merely complete assigned tasks? |
| Long-term thinking | Do they consider maintenance, trust, and future costs?              |
| Ownership          | When something breaks, do they investigate and fix it?              |
| Learning ability   | Can they use feedback and AI tools to improve their output?         |
| Collaboration      | Can they disagree honestly without becoming destructive?            |
| Execution          | Can they deliver a small, real task to an agreed standard?          |

Don't judge people just by how well they speak in interviews. Give them a small paid project, agree on the expected result, and observe how they work. For an important partnership, work together on a real problem before discussing substantial equity or making irreversible commitments.

And don't confuse cultural alignment with hiring people who think exactly like you. You want shared principles and independent judgment, not a team of people who always agree with the founder.

## 4. The deeper strategic advantage

Think about what happens if you combine your four activities:

Content attracts people

Your public writing makes your values, technical ability, and way of thinking visible before you ever meet someone.

Consulting tests collaboration

Real customers and deadlines reveal who is reliable, resourceful, and honest.

Fun Live builds relationships

Repeated interactions with developers and creators help you discover people whose interests and values align with yours.

Your company compounds those relationships

The best collaborators can become long-term teammates, partners, or cofounders. Others may remain trusted independent collaborators.

Over time, you aren't merely building a company. You're building a network of people who know your work, understand your principles, and have evidence that you can deliver.

That network can cross Guangzhou, Hong Kong, Singapore, Europe, and Silicon Valley.

My recommendation: make finding good collaborators an explicit part of your long-term strategy. Don't rush to hire a large team. Start with customers and small projects, publish your principles, collaborate with many people on a limited basis, and deepen the relationships that prove themselves over time.

One final distinction matters: the goal isn't to find people who are useful to you and discard everyone else. Treat people fairly, pay them properly, honor commitments, and end mismatched professional relationships respectfully. The best long-term network is one in which both sides benefit.

That's how your ambition to explore the world can become something bigger than travel: you explore the world, discover people worth building with, and gradually assemble a team capable of building something that lasts.
