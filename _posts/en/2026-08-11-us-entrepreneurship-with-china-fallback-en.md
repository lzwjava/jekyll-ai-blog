Question: Is the idea of trying the US, prioritizing entrepreneurship, while keeping China employment as a fallback a rational strategy?

Answer:

Your thinking has several correct observations, but there are also a few hidden risks.

The core idea:

> "Go to the US, increase surface area, meet people, find opportunities; if it fails, return to Guangzhou/Shenzhen and continue."

This is actually a classic **option value strategy**. You are not only buying education; you are buying:

* geographic exposure
* network expansion
* credibility signal
* chance encounters
* access to startup ecosystems
* understanding of another market

For someone young with no family obligations, this can be a pure exploration bet.

For someone with mortgage, family, existing engineering experience, and a working business capability, the calculation changes. The question is not "is America good?" but:

**Does spending $50k-$80k increase your probability of reaching a better outcome enough to justify the opportunity cost?**

---

### 1. The degree itself may not be the main ROI

For a person starting from zero:

```
Bachelor/Master -> internship -> job -> immigration
```

makes sense.

But your situation is different.

You already have:

* 10+ years engineering experience
* backend/full-stack background
* LLM training experience
* open-source/project output
* entrepreneurship experience

The marginal value of another AI master's degree is probably lower.

The real value is:

```
US Master's
    |
    +-- professors
    +-- classmates
    +-- startup connections
    +-- local credibility
    +-- internship opportunity
    +-- physical presence
```

The certificate is probably only 20% of the value.

The network and environment are 80%.

---

### 2. But the tourist visa idea is a dangerous assumption

This part:

> "拿着旅游签证在美国工作 打点散工 做点项目"

Needs caution.

A US visitor visa (B1/B2) generally does **not** allow employment in the US.

You can:

* attend meetings
* explore business opportunities
* negotiate contracts
* visit companies

But doing local paid work for a US employer while on tourist status can create immigration problems.

The correct model is more like:

```
B1/B2:
    explore
    network
    meet founders
    understand market

Then:
    F-1 student
    O-1
    H-1B
    startup/company route
```

---

### 3. Your observation about China success stories is partly psychological

You said:

> "待在国内 看多了负面新闻 周边的人 这些年感觉有很成功的吗"

This is a common perception problem.

The distribution changed.

2014-2016:

```
mobile internet explosion

WeChat
O2O
sharing economy
e-commerce

many startups raised money
```

A random engineer could see many startup stories.

Now:

```
AI era

fewer companies
higher technical requirements
larger capital concentration
```

The winner distribution is more extreme.

Before:

```
100 startups
10 got financing
1 became big
```

Now:

```
100 startups
maybe 3 get serious attention
1 creates huge value
```

But the upside is still there.

AI is actually one of the few areas where a small team can create disproportionate value.

Examples:

* one engineer can build an agent product
* one person can fine-tune models
* one person can create developer tools

The cost of experimentation dropped dramatically.

---

### 4. Your current China fallback is actually strong

Many people underestimate this.

Your fallback is not:

```
No job
No skills
No network
```

Your fallback is:

```
Guangzhou/Shenzhen AI engineer
+
bank/foreign company experience
+
consulting
+
AI projects
+
content/community
```

That is already a decent base.

So your risk is asymmetric:

Worst case:

```
Return to China
continue engineering
build AI consulting
```

Best case:

```
US network
startup opportunity
higher-value AI market
```

This is a much better position than someone taking $200k debt with no technical background.

---

### 5. The biggest danger: endless exploration

Your sentence:

> "每天晚上想着千条路，白日睡醒走原路"

is actually the biggest risk.

Many ambitious engineers get trapped here:

```
idea A
US master
startup
content
consulting
job
investment

=
high mental activity
low execution
```

The antidote is a decision framework.

Example:

## Next 12 months

Option A:

US Master's

Cost:

```
$50k-$80k
1-2 years
```

Expected gain:

```
US network
credential
possible startup/job
```

Option B:

China AI OPC

Cost:

```
$5k-$10k
```

Expected gain:

```
customers
revenue
portfolio
```

Option C:

Foreign company AI engineer

Cost:

```
almost zero
```

Expected gain:

```
salary
stability
network
```

Run experiments before committing.

For example:

Before spending $70k:

```
Spend 3 months:

- publish 10 AI engineering articles
- build 2 serious open-source projects
- contact 50 US founders/researchers
- try remote AI consulting
- apply to US companies
```

Then update your probability.

---

My view:

Your "America first, entrepreneurship first, China fallback" philosophy is reasonable.

But the optimal version is not:

```
pay $80k -> hope something happens
```

It is:

```
create momentum first
then use America as an accelerator
```

Your advantage is not the degree. Your advantage is that you can already build.

A person who can train GPT models, ship products, and talk to customers has more leverage than someone who only has an AI master's certificate. The US environment can amplify that, but it cannot replace execution.

The key question is:

**Can you prove within the next 6 months that your AI ability creates external value (users, revenue, reputation)?**

If yes, the US becomes a multiplier. If no, the US may become an expensive reset.
