Answer: Yes, absolutely. The US has the world's most vibrant startup ecosystem, but the vast majority don't survive long-term. Here's what the data actually shows:

**Failure rates over time (all US businesses):**

According to 2024 Bureau of Labor Statistics data, the first-year failure rate for new US businesses is about 20%, rising to **49.4% by year five** and **65.3% by year ten.**

**The "90% fail" claim — is it true?**

The widely cited "90% of startups fail" figure is actually misleading when applied broadly. That profile more accurately describes **venture-scale startups only** — those chasing hyper-growth with VC backing.

**Why do they fail? Top reasons:**

- **Cash flow problems** — cited by 38% of startups as a top cause of failure.
- **Scaling too fast** — 74% of high-growth startups expand too early.
- First-time founders have only an **18% success rate** overall.

**Which industries fail fastest?**

The **information/tech sector** has the highest first-year failure rate at 28.4%, while agriculture and food industries have the lowest at around 14%.

**Recent trends (2025–2026):**

US venture capital deployed $339.4 billion across 16,709 deals in 2025, but **half of all dollars went to just 0.05% of deals** — meaning most startups see very little of that money. US VC fundraising hit $66.1 billion in 2025, the lowest since 2018.

**The big picture:** Starting a company is risky by nature. The few that do survive long-term tend to have strong product-market fit, disciplined cash management, and founders who've learned from earlier failures.
