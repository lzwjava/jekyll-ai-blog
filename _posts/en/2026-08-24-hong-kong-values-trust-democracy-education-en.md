Question: **Do Hong Kong people generally value trust, democracy, truth, science, knowledge, and education?**

Answer: **Generally yes — but I would separate “Hong Kong society” from “Hong Kong politics.”** Your intuition is directionally right, especially if you are thinking about doing software/AI business there.

### 1. Trustworthiness: relatively high

Hong Kong has a strong **commercial/institutional culture of contracts, reputation, professional services, and rule-following**.

A useful hard metric: Transparency International's 2025 Corruption Perceptions Index ranks Hong Kong **12th out of 182 jurisdictions**, with a score of 76/100. ([Transparency.org][1])

The World Justice Project's 2025 Rule of Law Index also ranks Hong Kong **9th/143 for absence of corruption**. ([ICAC][2])

That doesn't mean *every Hong Kong person is trustworthy*. It means the **institutional environment has historically made dishonesty relatively costly**.

For business, that's much more meaningful than judging individuals by nationality.

---

### 2. Democracy: historically strong support, but politically complicated now

This is where I would **not** generalize too much.

Pew's research found substantial support for democratic government in Hong Kong, and education strongly correlates with democratic preferences. For example, less-educated Hong Kong respondents were about twice as likely as more-educated respondents to support an election system with only one party (26% vs. 12%). ([Pew Research Center][3])

But the political environment has changed substantially since 2020. Freedom House currently classifies Hong Kong as **“Partly Free”**, citing deterioration in political rights, media freedom, and academic freedom. ([Freedom House][4])

So:

> **Many Hong Kong people can personally value democracy while simultaneously being politically cautious about expressing those views.**

That's an important distinction.

---

### 3. Truth / rationality / science

I wouldn't say Hong Kong people are uniquely "truth-loving" or scientifically minded — that's too broad a claim.

But Hong Kong has several structural characteristics that are favorable to **evidence-based professional culture**:

* British-derived legal/institutional traditions
* international finance
* universities
* engineering and professional services
* strong English-language exposure
* international business
* high competition in education
* relatively strong emphasis on credentials and competence

And the university ecosystem is substantial. The HKDSE system itself is highly standardized and data-driven, with official statistics covering subject performance and university-admission thresholds. ([Data.gov.hk][5])

So if you walk into a Hong Kong software company and say:

> "Here is the benchmark. Here is the latency. Here is the GPU utilization. Here is the cost per million tokens."

that's generally a **very natural business language** there.

---

### 4. Education: definitely valued

This is probably the strongest part of your intuition.

Hong Kong has a deeply established **education → university → professional career** pipeline.

People often care considerably about:

* university reputation
* professional qualifications
* English
* technical competence
* salary
* international experience
* professional reputation

There is also an interesting interaction between education and political attitudes: Pew found that more-educated Hong Kong respondents were substantially more supportive of multiparty democracy. ([Pew Research Center][3])

But again, **educated ≠ automatically rational**. Highly educated people can still be status-driven, conservative, politically cautious, etc.

---

### 5. One thing I would be careful about

Don't construct this mental model:

> Hong Kong = educated + democratic + honest + scientific
> Mainland China = opposite

Reality is much more continuous.

You will find:

**Hong Kong**

* highly educated people
* extremely pragmatic people
* traditional people
* politically conservative people
* sophisticated international professionals
* people who don't care about politics at all
* people who strongly value democracy
* people who strongly identify with China

And the same kind of diversity exists in Guangzhou/Shenzhen/Shanghai.

The bigger difference is often **institutional incentives**, not the "character" of individual people.

---

### 6. For *you* specifically

For an AI/software consultant, I think the relevant question isn't:

> "Are Hong Kong people good people?"

It's:

> **"Can I build professional relationships there based on competence, reputation, contracts and results?"**

I'd say **yes, quite plausibly**.

Your potential positioning is actually quite compatible with Hong Kong:

```text
You
 │
 ├── AI / LLM engineering
 ├── GPU / AI infrastructure
 ├── model training
 ├── agent development
 └── software consulting
          │
          ▼
Hong Kong SMEs / finance / logistics / trading /
professional services / manufacturers / mainland-linked companies
```

And Hong Kong's relatively clean institutions and international business environment are genuine advantages. At the same time, don't assume every client will be intellectually honest or technically sophisticated—you still need **contracts, milestones, deposits, references, and technical proof**.

Interestingly, even government-trust surveys show that Hong Kong people themselves are not blindly trusting institutions: a CUHK survey in February 2025 found **21.8% trusted the Hong Kong government, 34.1% distrusted it, and 42.1% were in-between**. ([CUHK Comm & Public Relations][6])

That actually fits your question well: **a society can have relatively strong rule-of-law/business institutions without its population simply trusting authority.**

So my rough characterization would be:

> **Hong Kong is relatively educated, commercially pragmatic, internationally oriented, institutionally trustworthy, and historically receptive to liberal/democratic values — but today's political environment is much less liberal than Hong Kong's older reputation suggests.**

For your purpose, **I'd be more interested in Hong Kong's professional/business culture than trying to assign a moral character to "Hong Kong people."**
