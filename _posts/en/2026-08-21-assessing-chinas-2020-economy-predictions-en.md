Answer:

Yes, looking back from 2026, some of the observations in your 2020.03 article were surprisingly aligned with later trends, although not every prediction can be considered "accurate" in a strict forecasting sense. Your post was published around March 2020, during the COVID shock period, and your blog archive shows the article "The Present and Future of China's Economy" was indeed from that period. ([Zhiwei Li][1])

The parts that aged well were mainly **structural observations**, not short-term predictions:

### 1. Economic slowdown and the end of the high-growth era

Around 2020, many people still expected China to continue the previous 30-year growth trajectory. The more structural view was:

* demographic dividend was ending
* real estate could not continue infinite expansion
* debt accumulation was becoming a constraint
* productivity growth would matter more than investment-driven growth

Those themes became much more visible after 2021:

* property sector crisis
* weaker consumer confidence
* slower GDP growth
* youth employment pressure

So if your article argued that China's economy would enter a more difficult adjustment period, that direction was broadly correct.

### 2. "溃而不崩" idea

He Qinglian's argument in *China: A Country That Collapsed but Did Not Fall* was essentially:

* not an instant collapse like the Soviet Union
* rather, gradual deterioration of institutions, incentives, trust, and economic foundations
* the system can continue operating while problems accumulate

This is a useful framework because countries rarely behave like a binary:

```
Healthy country
       |
       v
Sudden collapse ❌
       |
       v
Long stagnation / structural decline / adaptation ✅
```

Japan after 1990 is a good comparison: not collapse, but decades of adjustment.

China after 2020 has shown some similar characteristics:

* still huge industrial capability
* still many successful companies
* still strong infrastructure
* but facing slower growth and structural problems

So "not collapse, but pressure accumulation" was a reasonable lens.

### 3. COVID as a turning point

One interesting thing is that March 2020 was exactly at a historical transition point.

Many people underestimated:

* US-China technology competition
* supply-chain restructuring
* AI becoming a major industrial competition field
* geopolitical fragmentation

The later rise of:

* semiconductor restrictions
* domestic AI models
* industrial policy competition
* global manufacturing diversification

made many of those issues much clearer.

### 4. Where predictions were probably less accurate

The difficult part of forecasting countries is timing.

A common mistake is:

```
problem exists
      |
      v
collapse soon
```

But reality is:

```
problem exists
      |
      v
adaptation + resistance + policy response
      |
      v
slow change over decades
```

China has significant strengths:

* manufacturing ecosystem
* engineering talent
* infrastructure
* large domestic market

These factors make sudden collapse unlikely.
