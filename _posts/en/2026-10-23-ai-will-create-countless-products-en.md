## 2. The deeper shift: the cost of creating a product is falling

Consider a simplified model:

\\[ \text{Expected profit} = N \times (P-C) - F \\]

Where:

- \\(N\\) = number of paying customers
- \\(P\\) = revenue per customer
- \\(C\\) = variable cost per customer
- \\(F\\) = fixed development and operating costs

AI can reduce \\(F\\), accelerate development, and sometimes reduce \\(C\\). That means a product serving only 500 or 5,000 customers might become economically viable when it previously wasn't.

But there is an important distinction:

The cost of building software is falling faster than the cost of earning customer attention and trust.

Generating an app is not the same as building a business. Distribution, reliability, security, product judgment, and customer relationships remain critical.

## 3. What this means for you

Given your experience across software engineering, LLM training and inference, and building Fun Live, I would think about the opportunity as three connected layers.

Layer 1 — Models and infrastructure

Pretraining, post-training, inference optimization, GPU infrastructure, evaluation, and agent runtimes.

Your technical research foundation.

Layer 2 — Products and applications

Developer tools, AI-native apps, agent systems, vertical SaaS, and AI-enabled hardware.

Where you turn technical capabilities into something people use.

Layer 3 — Distribution and business

Communities, open source, content, consulting, partnerships, and recurring customer relationships.

Where you find users, generate revenue, and discover what to build next.

These layers reinforce each other. Consulting can generate cash and expose real customer problems. Your technical work can give you a competitive advantage. Your community and content can bring users to the products you build.

And physical devices should not be an afterthought. Understanding computer architecture, GPUs, networking, electronics, sensors, and robotics alongside software and AI could become valuable as intelligence increasingly enters the physical world.

## 4. The strategy I would follow

I would not try to build every possible product. I would build the ability to discover and ship the right products repeatedly.

1. Keep learning the underlying technology. Understand models, inference, systems, hardware, and the economics of running AI.
2. Build small products continuously. Use AI coding agents to compress the distance between an idea, a working prototype, and real user feedback.
3. Stay close to real users. Solve problems you encounter yourself, and use consulting to learn about problems businesses will pay to solve.
4. Build reusable infrastructure. Turn repeated engineering work into libraries, agents, deployment tools, and open-source projects.
5. Accumulate distribution. Grow relationships, a developer community, content, and customer trust over time.

The long-term advantage is not merely knowing how to use AI to generate code. It is combining technical depth, product judgment, an understanding of hardware and software, and the ability to find people who genuinely need what you build.

My central thesis: AI will make it possible for many more people to create products, but the most valuable founders will be those who can repeatedly turn real problems into useful products and distribute them to real customers.
