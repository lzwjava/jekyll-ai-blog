**Question:** Is it common for people to pursue two master's programs in the USA?

**Answer:**

It's not *mainstream*, but it's increasingly pursued and well-recognized in the US. Here's the breakdown:

**How common is it?**
Only roughly 2–4% of adults in the United States hold two master's degrees, making it a relatively uncommon milestone in the broader population. So it's a niche path — not the norm, but certainly not unheard of.

**Two main ways people do it:**

1. **Sequential** — Finishing one master's degree, working for a few years, then going back for a second in a different or complementary field. This is more common and flexible.

2. **Dual/Concurrent degree programs** — These are designed to reduce completion time by one to two years compared to taking two master's degrees separately. Students apply once and graduate with two degrees. You must meet admission requirements for both programs and get approval from each department, and universities set rules on how many credits can overlap.

**Who typically does this?**

- Career-changers bridging two industries (e.g., engineering + business)
- Academics preparing for research or PhD programs
- Professionals seeking credentials in interdisciplinary fields

**Most popular combinations:**
The most common type confers two master's degrees in two related fields, with popular examples including a dual MBA + JD (law). Business + public policy, data science + another technical field, and healthcare administration combinations are also frequently seen.

**Is it worth it?**
More and more schools are offering dual degrees — the opportunity to pursue two degrees simultaneously, usually cutting down the time and cost it would take to earn them sequentially. Whether it's worth it depends heavily on your career goals; it signals strong commitment but also adds time and financial burden.
