**Question:** Did Paul Graham work hard to make Y Combinator succeed?

**Answer:**

Absolutely — and what makes his story compelling is that YC began not as a grand plan, but as an improvised experiment that he then poured himself into.

**Background: Experience Before YC**

In 1995, Graham and Robert Morris started Viaweb, the first software-as-a-service company, which was acquired by Yahoo in 1998, becoming Yahoo Store. That grueling firsthand experience building and selling a startup gave Graham deep insight into what founders actually needed — insight he later poured directly into YC.

**The Founding Effort**

In 2005, Graham, along with Jessica Livingston, Robert Morris, and Trevor Blackwell, founded Y Combinator with the vision of creating a new kind of venture firm that addressed the unique needs of early-stage startups.

Y Combinator was meant to be an experiment — an alternative to a summer programming job for students — but within a few weeks Graham realized he was on to something bigger.

**Hands-On, Intensive Work**

With an application deadline only ten days off, it seemed like a shot in the dark, but over the next week 227 applications flooded in. The partners spent an intensive weekend pouring over the entries, from which they chose 21 viable candidates, then flew the teams to Cambridge the following weekend for personal interviews.

In addition to offering office hours with Graham and other partners, Y Combinator held weekly group dinners with speakers.

**A Distinctive Philosophy He Actively Championed**

What made Y Combinator different was its focus on founders rather than ideas. Graham believed that the most important factor for startup success was the strength of the founding team, not necessarily the initial business concept.

Beyond running the program, in 2001 Graham started publishing essays on paulgraham.com, which now gets around 15 million page views per year. These essays became a massive part of how he attracted talent and shaped startup culture worldwide.

**Iterating When Things Went Wrong**

Graham didn't just set YC on autopilot. When in 2012 it began to seem that Y Combinator was accepting too many startups for its own good (its summer 2012 class welcomed 80 teams), Graham openly analyzed what went wrong and corrected course.

**Stepping Back Only After Building a Foundation**

In 2014, Paul Graham stepped down from his day-to-day role at YC, passing the torch to Sam Altman, who would continue to lead the accelerator to even greater heights. By that point, Y Combinator had funded over 2,000 startups, including Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe, and Reddit.

In short, Graham's hard work at YC was multi-dimensional: personal mentorship, intensive selection processes, continuous writing, philosophical leadership, and willingness to self-correct — all sustained over nearly a decade of direct involvement.
