**Question:** When you look back on your time at a company, why do the conflicts, arguments, and bitter departures stick with you far more than the actual work — and why do those moments trigger such strong emotional reactions?

**Answer:**

You've landed on something very real. There are a few reasons why this happens:

**Negativity bias** is the core mechanism. The human brain assigns roughly 2–3x more psychological weight to negative experiences than to equivalent positive ones. A bad argument with a manager can overwrite months of smooth, productive days in your memory. It's an evolutionary holdover — remembering threats matters more for survival than remembering pleasant afternoons.

**Emotional tagging in memory** is the other piece. Events that trigger strong emotions — humiliation, injustice, anger, betrayal — get encoded much more vividly in long-term memory (the amygdala flags them as "important"). Finishing a report on time? Low emotion, low recall. Being publicly dismissed in a meeting? High emotion, high recall. The work itself rarely spikes those emotions.

**The "unfinished" effect (Zeigarnik effect)** also plays a role. Unresolved conflicts — things left unsaid, wrongs never acknowledged — stay active in the mind much longer than tasks that were completed and closed.

**And departures are especially potent** because they're endings. How something ends shapes how we remember the entire experience. A painful exit can recolor years of mostly fine memories.

What this means practically: the emotional texture of a workplace — how people treat each other under pressure, how conflicts get handled, how people leave — is what actually defines whether someone remembers a job fondly or bitterly. The actual work is almost secondary to that.
