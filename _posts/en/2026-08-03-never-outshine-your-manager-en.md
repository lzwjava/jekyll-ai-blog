Question: How do you avoid outshining your manager/mentor at a big company — what specific behaviors read as "credit-stealing" vs. safe, and how do you handle attribution for talks/work?

Answer:

This is Robert Greene's "Law 1: Never Outshine the Master" (48 Laws of Power) — not abstract theory, it maps to concrete signals people read unconsciously in status hierarchies. Here's the mechanism and the fix:

**Why it backfires (the actual mechanism, not vibes)**
Your manager's value to *their* boss is a function of "is my report competent under me." If you visibly outperform them in front of their boss, you don't just look good — you make their management look unnecessary. That's a threat to their position, not a compliment to yours. People don't consciously think "I'll punish this person," they just start quietly excluding you from info flow, promo nominations, interesting projects. It's a defensive reflex, not malice.

**Concrete behaviors that get read as "stealing credit" vs. safe**

| Behavior | Reads as | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Presenting "my" design in a review the manager hasn't seen | Blindsiding them | Send deck 24h early, ask "anything you want me to adjust before the room sees it" |
| Answering a question directed at your manager, in the room | Undermining their authority live | Answer only if they visibly can't/defer to you, then hand the thread back: "as [manager] was saying..." |
| Solo byline on a blog post / conference talk about team work | Erasing the team | Co-author, or open with "this is [team]'s work, I'm presenting it" |
| Going directly to skip-level with a win | Bypassing chain of command | CC or loop manager first, let them decide whether to escalate it themselves |
| Fixing manager's mistake publicly in Slack/meeting | Public correction = status hit | DM privately: "hey I think there might be an edge case here, want to double check offline?" |
| Taking sole demo slot for a team feature | Solo spotlight | Say "let me pull in [name] who built X part" even if you did 90% |

**Structural fix, not just etiquette**
- In 1:1s, explicitly ask your manager what they want credit-flow to look like ("how do you want me to handle attribution when I present this?"). Most friction disappears once it's said out loud instead of inferred.
- Give credit *before* you're asked, publicly, specific ("the caching strategy was [name]'s idea, I just implemented it") — cheap for you, expensive-feeling generous to them.
- Never contradict your manager in front of their boss even if you're right — take it offline, let them decide how/whether to change position.
- If you're clearly better technically (sounds like your case, given your background), the move isn't to dim your work — it's to make your manager look like the person who *enabled* it: "couldn't have shipped this without [manager] clearing the roadblocks with [other team]."

**When this doesn't apply**
If you're running your own company (which you are), this calculus mostly evaporates — you *want* to be visibly the best, that's the product. The skill above is specifically for navigating inside someone else's hierarchy, e.g. consulting engagements where you're technically stronger than the client's internal lead but need them to keep sponsoring you.
