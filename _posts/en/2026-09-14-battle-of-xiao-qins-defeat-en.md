**Question:** What does the account of the Battle of崤 (Xiáo) between Qin and Jin (秦晋崤之战) say?

**Answer:**

The **Battle of崤 (Xiáo/Xiao Mountains)** between Qin and Jin is one of the most significant military engagements of the Spring and Autumn period. Here is what the historical account tells us:

**Background & Source**

The battle is first recorded in the *Zuozhuan* (Spring and Autumn Chronicles), and took place in 627 BCE between the states of Qin and Jin. The two states had originally been allies, forming the celebrated "Qin-Jin Alliance" (秦晋之好). However, since Duke Mu of Qin, Qin had grown increasingly powerful and harbored ambitions to dominate the Central Plains — ambitions that put it on a collision course with Jin.

**The Seeds of Conflict**

In 630 BCE, Qin and Jin jointly besieged the state of Zheng. However, a brilliant Zheng diplomat named Zhú Zhī Wǔ (烛之武) successfully persuaded Duke Mu of Qin to withdraw his forces, breaking the alliance. Duke Mu pulled back but left three generals — Qǐ Zǐ, Féng Sūn, and Yáng Sūn — behind to garrison Zheng on Qin's behalf.

**Qin's Rash Campaign**

Later, Duke Mu of Qin learned that both the rulers of Zheng and Jin had died, and his garrisoned generals in Zheng sent word that an inside strike was possible. Despite warnings from his advisors, Duke Mu rashly sent out a large army. The operation could not be kept secret over such a vast distance, and Zheng was fully prepared — so the Qin army withdrew without any gain.

**The Ambush at Xiao Mountains**

As the Qin army retreated through the narrow passes of the Xiao Mountains — a rugged stretch of terrain between the Yellow River and Luo River — Jin and its ally, the Jiang Rong tribe (familiar with the local terrain), sealed off both ends of the mountain valley and launched a fierce assault.

The Qin army had traveled a thousand *li*, failed in its mission, and returned exhausted and demoralized. The Jin army, waiting in ambush, was fresh and eager. Caught completely off guard, the Qin troops were thrown into panic. Jin won decisively — the three Qin commanders (Mèng Míng Shì, Xī Qǐ Shù, and Bái Yǐ Bǐng) were captured alive, and the rest of the army was annihilated.

**Historical Significance**

This battle marked the definitive end of the friendly Qin-Jin alliance and turned the two states into bitter enemies, profoundly shaping the strategic landscape of the Spring and Autumn period. After the battle, Qin's eastward expansion was checked. Duke Mu pivoted westward, campaigning against the western Rong tribes, eventually expanding Qin's territory by twelve kingdoms and a thousand *li*, becoming the hegemon of the western regions.

The Battle of Xiao is also historically significant as one of China's earliest recorded ambush battles, and its lessons about exploiting terrain influenced later military thought, including discussions in *Sun Tzu's Art of War*.
