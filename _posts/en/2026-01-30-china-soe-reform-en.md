Question: Why did state-owned enterprises in China lay off so many people in the 1990s? Was it because they offered secure jobs with no competition, employees were lazy, and there were no incentives to work?

Answer:

No, the massive layoffs in China's **state-owned enterprises (SOEs)** during the 1990s were not primarily because employees were "lazy" or lacked incentives in a simplistic way. The root cause was deep structural inefficiency in the entire SOE system under the planned economy, combined with the urgent need to transition toward a market-oriented economy.

Here are the main reasons for the large-scale layoffs (known as **xiagang**, affecting roughly 30–40 million workers between the mid-1990s and early 2000s):

- **Severe overstaffing and low efficiency** — Under the pre-reform planned economy, SOEs functioned more like social welfare organizations than profit-driven businesses. They were deliberately overstaffed to provide "full employment" and the famous **"iron rice bowl"** (lifetime job security plus housing, healthcare, education, and pensions). This led to massive redundancy: many workers had little to do, and productivity was very low.

- **Heavy financial losses and burden on the state** — By the mid-1990s, over 60% of large SOEs were losing money. Their profits (which were once 15% of GDP in 1978) had fallen to below 2% by 1997. SOEs accumulated huge debts, including non-performing loans that threatened the banking system (some major banks were technically insolvent). The government could no longer subsidize these losses indefinitely.

- **Introduction of market competition** — From the 1980s onward (accelerated in the 1990s), China allowed private enterprises, township-village enterprises, and foreign investment to grow rapidly. These new players were more efficient and competitive, exposing SOEs' weaknesses. Rural-to-urban migration (over 100 million young workers entering cities annually) and foreign direct investment further intensified pressure on inefficient state firms.

- **Policy-driven restructuring** — The central government launched aggressive SOE reforms, especially the **"Grasp the large, let go of the small"** policy (announced at the 15th Party Congress in 1997). Small and loss-making SOEs were privatized, merged, or closed, while larger strategic ones were consolidated and modernized. This was partly to prepare for **WTO accession** (achieved in 2001) and to avoid an economic crisis similar to the **1997 Asian Financial Crisis**.

- **End of the planned economy model** — The old system had no real market discipline, hard budget constraints, or performance-based incentives. While some workers may have had low motivation due to guaranteed pay regardless of output, this was a symptom of the flawed system design rather than individual laziness being the core driver.

In short, the layoffs were a painful but deliberate part of China's shift from a centrally planned economy to a socialist market economy. The goal was to eliminate inefficiency, reduce fiscal burden, boost overall productivity, and allow resources (including labor) to move to more dynamic private sectors. Many laid-off workers eventually found jobs in the booming private economy, though the transition caused significant hardship, especially in industrial regions like Northeast China.

The "secure jobs + no competition → laziness" view oversimplifies the issue. The lack of incentives was built into the old socialist model itself, not mainly a moral failing of the workers.
