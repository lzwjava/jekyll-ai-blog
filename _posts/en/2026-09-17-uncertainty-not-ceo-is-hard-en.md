Question: Why do some people find starting and running a startup less difficult than people generally imagine, even though the early years can involve heavy mental burden and lots of work?

Answer:

Yes. I think your intuition is basically right, but there is an important distinction:

**Being a CEO is not inherently hard. Being responsible for an uncertain outcome is hard.**

People often imagine entrepreneurship as:

> CEO → must personally know everything → personally do everything → constantly stressed.

In practice, a good founder eventually operates more like:

```text
                    Founder
                       │
          ┌────────────┼────────────┐
          ↓            ↓            ↓
       Product       People       Capital
          │            │            │
       delegate     delegate     manage
          │            │            │
          └────────────┼────────────┘
                       ↓
                  Company system
```

The first phase is different.

### 1. Early startup: the founder is the system

At 0 → 1, there often aren't enough people to delegate to.

You might personally do:

```text
idea
  ↓
customer interviews
  ↓
product
  ↓
sales
  ↓
hiring
  ↓
fundraising
  ↓
support
  ↓
marketing
  ↓
back to product
```

So the difficulty isn't really **"being CEO."**

It's that **one person temporarily has to cover many functions while the company has almost no resources.**

That's why the first few years can feel brutal.

### 2. As the company grows, the job changes

Suppose you have 5 people:

```text
Founder
 ├── product
 ├── engineering
 ├── sales
 ├── operations
 └── everything else
```

At 50 people:

```text
Founder
 ├── Product lead
 ├── Engineering lead
 ├── Sales lead
 ├── Operations lead
 └── Finance
```

At 500:

```text
                 Founder
                    │
          ┌─────────┴─────────┐
          ↓                   ↓
       Leadership          Capital/
         team              strategy
          │
      organization
          │
      thousands
```

The founder's work becomes increasingly about **choosing what the organization should do**, rather than personally doing every task.

That's essentially leverage.

### 3. Delegation doesn't remove mental burden

This is the subtle part.

You can delegate:

```text
implementation
customer support
accounting
recruiting
operations
management
```

But you can't completely delegate:

```text
"What should we do?"
"Is this the right market?"
"Should we spend $10M on this?"
"Should we hire this executive?"
"Should we shut this product down?"
"How much risk are we willing to take?"
```

The founder ultimately owns the consequences.

So the burden changes from:

> **execution burden**

to:

> **decision + uncertainty burden**

And uncertainty is psychologically unusual because there isn't necessarily a correct answer.

### 4. Mental burden is also partly a function of interpretation

This is where your point about mindset matters.

Two founders can have exactly the same situation:

```text
Revenue:     $2M
Cash:        $4M
Growth:      uncertain
Competition: increasing
```

Founder A thinks:

> "We're screwed if this doesn't work."

Founder B thinks:

> "Interesting. We have $4M of runway to find the next iteration."

Same facts, radically different subjective experience.

This doesn't mean mindset magically eliminates difficulty. Some problems are objectively difficult. But **the meaning you assign to uncertainty changes the psychological cost of carrying it.**

### 5. And some people are simply unusually suited to this game

This is probably underappreciated.

Someone might genuinely enjoy:

```text
uncertainty
   +
autonomy
   +
building
   +
competition
   +
large upside
```

For that person, a startup can actually feel *less* painful than a conventional job.

Not because the startup has less work.

Because the work itself is intrinsically rewarding.

It's similar to a programmer spending 14 hours debugging something because they are obsessed with solving it. An outsider sees:

> "14 hours? That's insane."

The programmer sees:

> "I finally figured out why the allocator was corrupting memory."

The objective workload and subjective experience aren't the same thing.

### 6. The real founder skill is creating leverage

A useful way to think about entrepreneurship is:

```text
Founder effort
      ↓
   leverage
      ↓
┌─────┼─────┐
code  people capital
      ↓
 company
      ↓
 output
```

At the beginning:

```text
1 founder → 1 unit of output
```

Later:

```text
1 founder
   ↓
10 leaders
   ↓
100 teams
   ↓
1000 people
   ↓
millions of units of output
```

That's one reason entrepreneurship can become *less* operationally difficult as the company gets larger, despite the stakes becoming much higher.

The founder's job is increasingly to **build a machine that doesn't require the founder to manually operate every component.**

So I'd phrase your original idea like this:

> **People don't start startups because being a CEO is easy. They start because they see an opportunity where the expected value of taking responsibility is worth the uncertainty and burden. For someone whose strengths and temperament fit entrepreneurship, the burden may feel much smaller than it appears from the outside. Early on, they personally carry a lot of execution; over time, they can replace personal effort with people, processes, capital, and technology. What remains is primarily responsibility for direction and high-level decisions.**

And there's an even more interesting question underneath this: **why does the exact same uncertainty feel like "freedom" to one founder and "stress" to another?** I can break that down using expected value, risk tolerance, agency, and founder psychology.
