**Question:** Is doing things purely to impress others — like starting a startup or hiring people just to show off — a bad idea?

**Answer:**

Yes, largely. Here's why it tends to backfire:

**The core problem is misaligned motivation.** When the *goal* is the impression rather than the outcome, you optimize for appearance over substance. A startup built to look impressive will make decisions that look good on LinkedIn instead of decisions that build a real business. That's a recipe for failure.

**Specific ways it goes wrong:**

- **Startups for status** — Founding a company is brutal: long hours, low pay early on, high failure rate. If your "why" is social clout, you'll quit the moment it stops looking glamorous (which is fast). The people who survive are driven by the problem they're solving.
- **Hiring to look important** — Employees cost money, time, and responsibility. Hiring to inflate your headcount without real work for them to do burns your runway, creates a toxic culture, and makes *everyone* less productive.
- **It attracts the wrong things** — Performative success attracts people who also just want the association. You end up surrounded by hollow relationships and shallow networks.

**The nuance worth keeping:**

Not all external signaling is bad. Reputation *matters* — investors, partners, and customers form impressions. The difference is:

- **Healthy:** Build something real → reputation follows naturally
- **Unhealthy:** Engineer the impression → hope reality catches up (it usually doesn't)

The philosopher Epictetus put it well: most suffering comes from caring about things outside your control — like other people's opinions. Founders and leaders who last tend to be weirdly indifferent to looking impressive; they're too busy actually doing the work.

So yes — building around external validation is not just inefficient, it's structurally fragile. The impression fades; the consequences of bad decisions don't.
