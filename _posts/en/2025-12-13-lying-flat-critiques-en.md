Question: What are unpopular ideas about the "lying flat" movement?

Answer:

The "lying flat" (tang ping) movement, originating in China around 2021, involves young people rejecting intense societal pressures for overwork, consumerism, marriage, and high achievement in favor of a minimalist, low-effort lifestyle prioritizing personal well-being. While popular among some disillusioned youth as a form of passive resistance, several unpopular or critical views challenge its appeal and implications.

### Unpopular Ideas and Criticisms:

- **It's shameful and irresponsible**: Official Chinese media and commentators have labeled "lying flat" as morally wrong, unjust, and a betrayal of societal duties. It's seen as disappointing parents, taxpayers, and the nation, conflicting with values of hard work and contribution.

- **A privilege of the middle class**: True "lying flat" requires some financial cushion (e.g., parental support or savings), making it inaccessible to lower-class individuals who must work relentlessly to survive. Critics argue it's not a universal protest but a luxury for those with safety nets.

- **Harms economic growth and innovation**: The movement is viewed as threatening national productivity, consumption, and ambition. Slogans like refusing to buy homes, cars, marry, or have children are criticized for undermining China's development goals and demographic stability.

- **Passive defeatism rather than empowerment**: Some see it as hopeless resignation or "helpless activism" born from disillusionment, not a wise or rebellious choice. It's accused of spreading negativity without offering constructive alternatives.

- **Not genuine resistance**: In a highly competitive society, "lying flat" is interpreted as giving up rather than effective protest, potentially becoming a self-fulfilling prophecy of stagnation.

These views, often from official sources, older generations, or economic analysts, contrast with supporters who praise it as rational self-care amid unfair pressures like the 996 work culture.
