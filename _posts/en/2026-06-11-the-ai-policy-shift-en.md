**Question:** What does Dario Amodei argue in his June 2026 essay "Policy on the AI Exponential"?

**Answer:**

It's his most aggressive policy essay yet — the core thesis is that the era of "transparency-only" AI policy is over and it's time for binding regulation. He frames the whole thing with a Lord of the Rings analogy: AI moves at Hobbit speed while policy moves at Treebeard speed, and the mismatch is now dangerous because scaling laws suggest "a country of geniuses in a datacenter" within a year or two.

The pivotal trigger is Claude Mythos Preview, which he says proved frontier models pose real cybersecurity risks to financial systems, critical infrastructure, and national security — demonstrating that AI models are now tools of strategic consequence, with bio and autonomy risks likely to follow.

He organizes the essay into five policy areas:

**1. Regulation:** The headline shift. He argues AI should now be regulated like aviation — an FAA-style model where frontier models above a compute threshold undergo mandatory third-party testing in four areas (cyber, bioweapons, loss of control, automated R&D), and the government can block deployment if a model fails. Anthropic is releasing an actual legislative proposal alongside the essay, with financial backing. He explicitly says this goes beyond the transparency laws (SB 53, RAISE, etc.) Anthropic backed in 2025.

**2. Economics:** He warns AI may lock the economy into a "hypergrowth, hyper-inequality setting" that's hard to escape, where the challenge isn't growth but distribution. Proposals: better labor-market measurement, wage insurance, retention tax incentives, and potentially long-term income support like UBI or universal capital accounts financed by taxing AI companies or capital gains.

**3. Accelerating benefits:** The inverse problem — here he worries regulators will be *too slow*. FDA/EMA pipelines take 7-8 years per drug; without reform, AI-driven drug discovery will simply overload the system. He wants pre-approved standards for AI-based PK/PD modeling, toxicology prediction, synthetic control arms, and surrogate endpoints.

**4. Civil liberties:** Probably the most striking section. He warns AI could enable a "surprise seizure of power" and proposes: constitutional accountability for autonomous weapons (an "off switch" answerable to courts), a ban on domestic use of fully autonomous weapons, closing the data-broker surveillance loophole, and a right to AI counsel when facing government action. He also notes companies themselves need checks — citing Anthropic's Long-Term Benefit Trust.

**5. Geopolitics:** A democratic coalition that shares chips and SME internally while denying them to adversaries, coordinates safety standards, and makes membership attractive enough that staying outside becomes costly. The vivid line: a nation 3 years behind in AI could be like WWII Marines versus medieval swordsmen.

He closes by rejecting the "AI just needs better marketing" framing from industry peers — arguing public backlash reflects correctly perceived risk, and that this concern is democratic accountability working as intended; the job is channeling it into policy rather than letting it curdle into anger.

The interesting meta-read: this is Anthropic formally moving from "advocate for optionality" (transparency, export controls) to "advocate for hard gates" (pre-deployment licensing). He's careful to scope the government's blocking power to four specific risks with anti-favoritism protections — clearly anticipating the regulatory-capture critique.
