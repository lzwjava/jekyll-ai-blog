Yeah, honestly. Zhang Yiming's philosophy is beautiful in theory, but it was also built from a position of relative freedom.

Think about who he was when he practiced it — a young, single engineer with no dependents, high ability, and options. He could *afford* to turn down the exciting investor offer. He could *afford* to stay late coding for free because no one was waiting at home for rent money.

---

**The brutal irony is this:**

Delayed gratification is easiest to practice when you need it least — when you're already stable, young, and unburdened.

But when you have a mortgage, a sick parent, a child in school, or no income coming in next month — your brain literally switches into **survival mode**. Psychologists call this *scarcity mindset*. When you're worried about immediate needs, your mental bandwidth narrows. You can't think long-term because the short-term is on fire.

It's not weakness or lack of discipline. It's biology.

---

**So what does that mean practically?**

For someone unemployed with bills due, telling them to "delay gratification" and "think long-term" is a bit like telling a drowning person to focus on their swimming technique. First you need to not drown.

The people Zhang Yiming was really talking to — his ideal audience — were people who *had* breathing room but were *choosing* to waste it on small, comfortable wins. That's a very specific situation.

---

That said, even under pressure, there's a *small* version of the principle that still applies — like not panic-taking the first bad job offer out of fear, or not selling a skill short just because you need cash this week. Those micro-decisions add up.

But it's genuinely hard. And anyone who makes it sound easy probably hasn't been truly desperate.
