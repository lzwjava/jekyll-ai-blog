**Question:** What does Jack Ma mean when he says an entrepreneur's capacity (胸怀) is "stretched open" (撑大) by grievances and feeling wronged (委屈)?

**Answer:**

The quote you're thinking of is: **"男人的胸怀是委屈撑大的"** — most commonly translated as *"A man's (or entrepreneur's) capacity for greatness is expanded by grievance and being wronged."*

Jack Ma reportedly said this on the CCTV program *赢在中国 (Win in China)*, speaking directly to entrepreneurial contestants on national television. The full version sometimes includes: "More grievance, less temper — and you'll be happier."

Here's how to unpack it:

**委屈 (wěi qu)** — This is a rich Chinese concept. It's not just "grief" in the sad sense, but the feeling of being unfairly treated, wrongly accused, misunderstood, underappreciated, or swallowing injustice in silence. Think: a partner cheats you, investors doubt you, employees betray you, the market ignores you.

**胸怀 (xiōng huái)** — Literally "chest/heart capacity," but it means your psychological breadth: tolerance, magnanimity, the ability to absorb difficulty without breaking or retaliating.

**撑大 (chēng dà)** — "Stretched open / expanded by pressure from within." Like a balloon that only grows larger by being inflated from the inside — the pressure is what makes it bigger.

So the full idea is: **Your capacity as a leader isn't built in good times — it's forged by enduring injustice, humiliation, and frustration without collapsing or lashing out.** Every time you swallow a grievance and keep going, your tolerance and vision grow larger.

Jack Ma lived this himself — he was rejected repeatedly throughout his career, and as he put it, his own success was built on top of suffering.

Practically, it means:

- Don't rage-quit when things are unfair.
- Don't demand immediate vindication.
- Let the pressure of mistreatment *expand* you, not break you.
- The bigger the grievance you can absorb, the bigger the leader you become.

It's a distinctly Confucian-flavored concept of resilience — suffering isn't just something to endure, it's actually the *mechanism* that builds character and capacity.
