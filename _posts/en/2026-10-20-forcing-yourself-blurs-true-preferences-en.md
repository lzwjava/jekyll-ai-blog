**Question:** When people force themselves to do things, does that make it harder to tell what they genuinely like or dislike?

**Answer:**

Yes — in many cases, forcing yourself to act can blur your sense of authentic preference, but the picture is nuanced.

**Why it impairs self-knowledge:**

- **Overjustification effect:** When you're externally pushed into something, your brain can rationalize it ("I must like this, since I keep doing it"), which confuses compelled behavior with genuine preference.
- **Suppression of internal signals:** Chronic self-coercion trains you to override gut feelings — over time, you become less sensitive to them. This is related to the psychological concept of *alexithymia* (difficulty identifying your own emotions).
- **Ego depletion blurs enjoyment:** Forcing willpower is cognitively draining. When exhausted, people struggle to accurately assess whether an experience felt good or bad.
- **Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan):** Activities driven by external pressure (extrinsic motivation) feel less satisfying and authentic than those driven internally, making it harder to use them as a reliable signal of preference.

**The counter-nuance — sometimes forcing helps:**

- The **mere exposure effect** means repeated forced exposure can sometimes *create* genuine liking where none existed before.
- Forcing yourself through discomfort (e.g., exercise, learning) can eventually lead to authentic enjoyment, once competence grows.

**The core problem** is that forcing collapses the space between *"I chose this"* and *"I had to do this"* — and authentic self-knowledge depends on that distinction being clear.
