Question: Is being “nice” actually disadvantageous in interviews, salary negotiations, and fundraising?

Answer: **If by “nice” you mean overly agreeable, eager to please, conflict-avoidant, and unwilling to say no — yes, that can be disadvantageous.**

But **kindness itself is not the problem**.

Think of two dimensions:

|          | Low assertiveness                          | High assertiveness            |
| -------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------- |
| **Warm** | People-pleaser — weak negotiating position | **Best: respected + likable** |
| **Cold** | Passive / insecure                         | Aggressive / asshole          |

You want the **top-right**, not the bottom-right.

For example:

> “I really want this job. I'll take whatever salary you can offer.”

versus:

> “I'm excited about the role. Based on the scope and my experience, I'm targeting 45–50k RMB/month.”

The second person can still be perfectly pleasant. They just **don't surrender leverage to demonstrate that they're pleasant**.

A useful rule:

> **Be nice to people. Be hard on terms.**

You can smile, listen, thank people, and genuinely like them while still saying:

> “No.”

> “That's below what I'm looking for.”

> “I wouldn't be comfortable with that.”

> “I'll pass if that's the final offer.”

> “I need time to think about it.”

That combination is generally much stronger than deliberately acting like an asshole.

And there is one particularly important thing for you: **don't compensate for insecurity by becoming aggressive.** If you lack leverage, acting arrogant usually makes the situation worse. Build real alternatives, skills, customers, offers, investors, or cash runway. Then your confidence becomes natural because **you actually can walk away**.

**The strongest person in a negotiation often doesn't need to act dominant. They simply don't need the deal badly enough to accept bad terms.**
