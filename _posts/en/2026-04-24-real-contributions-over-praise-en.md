Question:
In LinkedIn or open source projects, are the people who actually contribute (e.g., raising issues, submitting pull requests, sharing useful information) more important than those who just congratulate or react?

Answer:

You’re pointing at a real distinction—but it’s not as one-sided as it might feel.

In **open source ecosystems**, the hierarchy of value is pretty clear:

* People who **write code, fix bugs, review PRs, and raise meaningful issues** are doing the core work.
* Without them, the project simply doesn’t move forward.
* Platforms like GitHub are built around this idea: contributions are visible, trackable, and measurable.

So yes—in that context, **effort and substance dominate**. A thoughtful bug report or a well-crafted pull request is far more valuable than a “great work!” comment.

But **LinkedIn operates on a different incentive system**.

LinkedIn is closer to:

* a **professional signaling platform**
* mixed with **social validation mechanics**

On LinkedIn:

* “Congrats” comments and likes are not useless—they **amplify visibility**.
* Visibility → reach → opportunities (jobs, connections, reputation).
* The algorithm rewards engagement, not just depth.

However, that creates a gap:

* **High-signal content** (deep insights, real experience, technical breakdowns)
* vs.
* **Low-effort engagement** (congrats, applause, generic praise)

The uncomfortable truth:

* The second often spreads faster.
* The first builds **long-term credibility**.

Think of it like two layers:

### 1. Surface Layer (Social Energy)

* Congrats, likes, short comments
* Keeps the platform active
* Helps distribution

### 2. Core Layer (Real Value)

* Sharing hard-earned knowledge
* Writing detailed posts or case studies
* Pointing out problems, trade-offs, failures
* Equivalent to “raising issues / PRs” in open source

Only the second layer **compounds over time**.

If you look at respected engineers or thinkers:

* They may get many “congrats”
* But what actually builds their reputation is:

  * consistent **high-quality output**
  * willingness to **say something non-obvious**
  * evidence of **real work behind the scenes**

So your intuition is directionally right, but incomplete:

* **In open source** → contribution is the system
* **On LinkedIn** → contribution + visibility both matter

If you ignore visibility entirely, your work may stay unseen.
If you ignore substance, your visibility becomes shallow and short-lived.

The more practical framing is:

> Treat “congrats” as noise that helps distribution,
> but treat **real contributions as the only thing that builds durable value**.

And if you’re deciding how to behave:

* Spend most of your time on **substance**
* Use the platform mechanics just enough so that substance gets seen

That balance is what separates people who look active from people who are actually respected.
