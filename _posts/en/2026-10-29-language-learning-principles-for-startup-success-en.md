**Question:** How can the principles from Zhiwei Li's English learning approach be applied to building a company and product?

---

**Answer:**

The blog post reveals powerful meta-principles that transfer remarkably well to entrepreneurship. Here's the mapping:

---

**1. Immersion over forced study → Saturate yourself in the problem domain**

The author spent 8–16 hours daily immersed in English — listening while driving, eating, falling asleep, even mid-conversation. Apply this to company building: live and breathe your industry. Read customer reviews, competitor docs, industry reports, and user forums constantly — not just when it feels like "work."

---

**2. Enjoyable input → Build what you're genuinely passionate about**

The core insight is: "Find the content and methods you enjoy the most, and stick with them." The author explicitly avoided painful exam prep in favor of TikTok, Netflix, and podcasts. For products: don't chase trends you don't care about. Build in a domain where you'd naturally spend 8 hours a day even without a salary.

---

**3. 100 examples → Deeply study 100 competitors / talk to 100 customers**

The approach was quantity-based: watch 100 shows without subtitles, learn 100 songs, like 10,000 TikTok videos. After that volume, the brain restructures itself. Do the same: talk to 100 potential users before writing a single line of code. Your intuition about the market "restructures" after enough input.

---

**4. Real-world use before mastery → Ship early, find your first real customer**

The real breakthrough came when a Dutch client on Upwork reached out. Despite painfully poor listening skills, the author completed the project, earned money, and locked in the commitment: "I must get good at English." The equivalent: get a paying customer before your product is "ready." Real stakes teach faster than any planning.

---

**5. Start easy, build up → MVP-first thinking**

"If you find certain materials difficult, look for easier ones to learn from. Once you've mastered the easy ones, move on to slightly more challenging content." Don't try to build the full vision on day one. Ship the smallest thing that delivers value. Complexity comes after you've internalized the basics.

---

**6. Background absorption → Use every context for compound learning**

The trick was playing lectures in the background so the brain could absorb even during "unproductive" moments — building circuits, driving, resting. For founders: always have a customer problem, a metric, or an insight "playing in the background." Great product intuition is built on accumulated context, not focused sprints alone.

---

**7. Brain transformation through volume → Founder intuition emerges through reps**

"After half a year, I find my brain changed miraculously... When I want to say something, my brain quickly knows how to say it. Just like using Chinese." The same happens with product sense — after enough iterations, customer conversations, and shipping cycles, your judgment about what users need becomes instinctive.

---

**8. Affordable practice → Bootstrap and find cheap feedback loops**

Rather than paying 150 CNY/session at VIPKID, the author found 18 CNY/session courses on Pinduoduo — same outcome, much lower cost. Treat early customer acquisition the same way: cold emails, communities, Reddit threads, and content marketing often outperform expensive ads, especially before product-market fit.

---

**9. Process over outcome → Fall in love with building, not just success**

The author's favorite quote sums it up: "The difference between a good student and a great one is that a good student is concerned more about the outcome while a great one is fascinated by the process of learning." The founders who last are those obsessed with the problem and the craft — not just the exit.
