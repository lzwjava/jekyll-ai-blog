Question: In most companies/startups, do people in higher positions generally get more compensation and more freedom/leave—especially in big corporations?

Answer: **Yes, generally—but “more compensation” is much more universal than “more leave.”**

In a typical large company, the hierarchy often looks roughly like:

| Level            | Compensation | Autonomy  | Working hours / leave                                          |
| ---------------- | ------------ | --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Junior engineer  | Lower        | Low       | Usually defined                                                |
| Senior engineer  | Higher       | Higher    | More flexibility                                               |
| Staff/Principal  | Much higher  | High      | Often flexible                                                 |
| Manager/Director | Higher       | High      | Flexible, but more responsibility                              |
| VP/C-level       | Very high    | Very high | Very flexible formally, but potentially **very high pressure** |

### 1. Higher position → disproportionately higher compensation

Especially in big corporations, compensation isn't simply paying for hours worked.

At senior levels, you're being paid for:

* **decision-making**
* **responsibility**
* **organizational leverage**
* **ability to allocate people/resources**
* **business impact**
* **risk-bearing**
* **scarce expertise**
* sometimes **political/organizational coordination**

For example, a senior executive making $500k/year isn't necessarily producing 5× the code of an engineer making $100k. Their leverage is different.

A CEO can make one decision that changes a company's revenue by hundreds of millions, so the economic value of the role is fundamentally different.

### 2. Higher position → usually more *freedom*

This part is very real.

A junior employee may need:

> "Can I take Friday afternoon off?"

A senior person may simply put:

> "OOO Friday afternoon."

And nobody cares.

At sufficiently high levels, **schedule control becomes part of compensation**.

You may be able to:

* work remotely
* choose when to work
* take a few days off without asking
* travel while working
* skip meetings
* delegate work
* choose priorities
* negotiate your own working conditions

So in that sense, **status buys autonomy**.

### 3. But higher position does NOT necessarily mean less work

This is the important distinction.

There are roughly two different things:

**Operational constraint ↓**

but

**Responsibility ↑**

A junior engineer might have:

> 10 tasks + fixed hours + low responsibility.

A VP might have:

> 3 meetings + 2 decisions + one crisis that can affect 10,000 employees.

The VP has much more freedom about **when/how** they work, but potentially much greater psychological responsibility.

That's why executives can look like they're "working less" from the outside while actually carrying enormous mental load.

### 4. Big corporations make this particularly visible

Large corporations have enough organizational layers that senior people can obtain **organizational leverage**.

For example:

```text
Engineer
   ↓
Senior
   ↓
Staff
   ↓
Manager
   ↓
Director
   ↓
VP
   ↓
C-level
```

As you move upward:

```text
personal execution
       ↓
technical decisions
       ↓
team decisions
       ↓
organizational decisions
       ↓
capital/resource allocation
       ↓
company strategy
```

The number of things you personally execute can decrease while the **blast radius of your decisions increases**.

### 5. Startups are somewhat different

In an early startup, the founder may have:

* enormous autonomy
* potentially enormous upside
* almost no formal leave
* enormous uncertainty
* enormous responsibility

So:

> **Founder ≠ employee with lots of vacation.**

The founder has maximum freedom but can also be psychologically "on call" 24/7.

The interesting economic tradeoff is:

**Employee**

```text
less upside
less responsibility
less autonomy
more predictable life
```

**Senior employee**

```text
higher compensation
higher autonomy
higher responsibility
still relatively predictable
```

**Founder**

```text
maximum autonomy
maximum upside
maximum uncertainty
maximum responsibility
```

So your intuition is basically correct: **as organizational power increases, compensation and autonomy usually increase together, particularly in large corporations.** But the *pressure and responsibility* can increase at the same time.

A useful way to think about seniority is not simply **“more money for more work”**, but **“more money + more control in exchange for more responsibility and leverage.”**
