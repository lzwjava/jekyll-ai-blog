**Time flies, and another year has passed.** A year ago, I never could have imagined that this is how I'd spend my 22nd year.

This year, my thinking has undeniably undergone a massive transformation. A year ago at this time, I was running QuZhibo (Fun Live) all by myself — opening my contacts list, looking for tech experts I hadn't yet invited to share on the platform, messaging them, discussing topics, publishing schedules, promoting everywhere, then waiting for the streams to go live and maintaining the platform. I kept at it until April, when Ye Fei, former VP of Jumei, invested in me. Then in April, investor Li Xiaolai put in money. By July and August, I had eight colleagues working with me on QuZhibo. Then operating costs grew unsustainable, and we were down to four colleagues plus me, and I lost my sense of direction. In September, I hit a personal relationship crisis and, in a moment of youthful impulsiveness, flew to the United States to try to win back my ex-girlfriend. I ended up traveling there for four weeks. Then at the end of October, I came back and joined Ye Fei's company part-time, doing some freelance work on the side. Then in early January, I stopped the part-time job, returned to entrepreneurship, and started taking on contract projects full-time — working on-site at a partner company Monday through Friday, sometimes running to the tax bureau to issue invoices, meeting with clients to discuss projects, and so on.

**The money in my account tells the story of the year's ups and downs more clearly than anything else.** I started the year scraping by with only a few thousand yuan. After funding, I had 550,000 yuan in the account. Then we hired too fast, spent too much, lost direction — and I was down to 100,000 yuan. Then starting in October, I took on multiple jobs, coding through the night, day after day, for four months straight, churning out project after project. I slowly clawed some money back. Freelance income totaled 230,000 yuan.

In October, I wrote an article reflecting on the gains and losses of QuZhibo:

A story very much like all the other startup failure stories we've heard.*

How similar it is to all those startup failure stories everyone's read. I had previously read about Wang Kaixin, the "miracle girl" born in 1998, and thought I would never end up like that — I was more reliable. I'd read too many stories of founders who, after raising a lot of money, became arrogant and floating, moved to bigger offices, hired more staff, and quickly collapsed. I thought I'd never make such stupid mistakes. But having lived through it, I now know: I was just as stupid.

Around that time, two new team members joined QuZhibo: Yu, a loyal user who had attended the most streams, and Wang, an alumnus from Beijing Forestry University. Thinking that attracting users through live streams was a bit slow, I tried community viral growth tactics — bringing in a few tech experts to anchor a group, designing a poster, and having people share it to join. We built a few 500-person groups, meaning the poster was forwarded over a thousand times. After the August 6th event, we started compiling event summaries and speaker transcripts. August payroll was over 70,000 yuan, plus 13,000 in rent — we had moved from Wangjing SOHO to a nearby Hesheng Qilin neighborhood and were now paying rent. After payroll, the company account had around 200,000 yuan left. At this rate, we'd be bankrupt in two months. Suddenly the pressure was overwhelming, and I reached out to friends for help.

I told the team about the situation and decided to cut salaries by one-third. I spoke with each person individually — explaining that we currently lacked a clear strategic direction and faced financial pressure, and that some team members would need to leave. The designer and four in the tech department departed. The three remaining operations team members accepted significant pay cuts and stayed.

I also made another decision: in the first half of QuZhibo, my parents had subsidized me 66,000 yuan. I had to pay them back. My entrepreneurship was my own affair — I shouldn't drag my parents into it. If I ever truly went broke, at least my parents might still be able to give me a few thousand yuan each month to start over. Then on September 16th, something happened: my girlfriend, who had been in the US for two weeks, told me she was breaking up with me. And so that's how I, the unreliable 1995-born CEO, burned through 550,000 yuan of investor money down to just 100,000.

At the end of October, after returning from America, my investor Ye Fei asked me to come help him rewrite a system. After everything I had been through, I couldn't keep drifting. I needed to focus, settle down, and return to a state of high productivity. I agreed, joining his company on a part-time basis.

Because I've run a company, because I once burned through 550,000 yuan down to 100,000, I know this. The boss carries the greatest risk. **These experiences have given me new perspectives:**

**Focus is everything when running a company.** Focus is so damn important. You can pivot, but when you do, be decisive. Don't just keep thinking about it, keep hesitating — take action, be resolute. If you're going to do media, do it. If you're going to do offline events, do it. Set the long-term strategic direction clearly, then execute swiftly and confidently. Don't just sit there endlessly trying this and that, three days of work followed by two days of rest. Overthinking and under-acting is the cardinal sin.

**Learn to say no.** Life is full of people and things that scatter your attention. Learn to say no, be clear about what your most important goal is, what the most important task is. If the most important things aren't getting done, it doesn't matter how much else you accomplish — it's all wasted effort. Everyone knows this. But sometimes we don't hold ourselves strictly to it, and don't put all our time toward the most important things and the most important people. Right now I'm making some money because I'm spending all my time making money and on the people I'm making it with — colleagues and partners. I haven't wasted time on anything else.

**If you're not making money, you're spending money.** Life costs money everywhere. If you want to save without spending, you have to be making money. When you're busy making money, you have no time to spend it. Every expense becomes a forced expense — a life necessity like rent and transportation. The best way to save is to spend all your time earning.

**I've been reflecting on why I've made more in these past few months than I made all year running my startup.** QuZhibo's total revenue for the year was 150,000 yuan; actual income was about 50,000. The first half's revenue was 120,000; the second half was a disaster. These are two fundamentally different business models. One is B2C — serving developers and regular users. Though we had about 27,000 developer users, we couldn't monetize them well. The per-order value was too low. Also, with limited human power, both the technology and promotion were weak, so the product never delivered its potential value. The other model is B2B — high per-order value, tens of thousands of yuan per deal, serving business clients.

But no experience is wasted. The reason I was able to get these freelance projects later is precisely because of the reputation I built while running QuZhibo — friends thought I was solid, growing a developer community to 27,000 from nothing and even securing funding. When they later heard I was doing freelance work, they brought projects to me. And the QuZhibo experience also toughened me up — it taught me to handle pressure, to understand how hard life is, and gave me a small amount of patience. So once I shifted my business model and direction, I started being able to sustain myself, no longer bleeding money, making a little.

**Live with a more authentic sense of self. The more successful a person, the more self-directed they tend to be.** Worshipping anyone, taking photos with celebrities, riding someone else's coattails — will that make people genuinely respect you? No. You earn your reputation by doing difficult things exceptionally well. What's worth pride is what you've created. Not worth pride: what you've enjoyed, what concerts you attended, who you met, what books you read, whose likes you got. These trivial things leave no lasting mark. Life is short — think about what we could write on our tombstone. Those are the things that matter. Focus on the things that matter.

**Dream big, but keep your feet on the ground.** Talking about how to become a billionaire is pointless. First hit 100,000 or 1,000,000 yuan per month, then talk. Discussing industry mega-trends is worthless. First build an excellent product. Always be clear about where you actually stand and what you've actually achieved. Your next goal must be concrete and achievable. Talking about things too far away is meaningless. You say you want to be a billionaire — first solve the technical problem in front of you. You say you want to build a 100-million-revenue outsourcing company — first ask yourself how many business owners you've actually visited.

**We all want to win, but we can't rush it.** You have to go step by step. If you don't manage risk and take too big a leap, you're going to fail. In QuZhibo's situation, I had no business paying out that much salary each month. With the company not profitable, and given my background, it was also very hard to raise the large sums needed for a burn-heavy business.

**Control risk, go step by step. Life is long.** I'm an impatient person. Reality has taught me to be patient.

**I have a new view on leadership now.** I always thought I was a lone wolf, someone with no leadership ability. Now I realize I do have some. Having leadership isn't simply about having high emotional intelligence or being great at motivating people. It's about being willing to do the dirtiest work, absorb the greatest pressure, and do the things nobody else wants to do. Organizing a team, finding money, finding people, finding projects — of course it's exhausting and hard. Be willing to do it, and you naturally become the core, the person everyone depends on.

Gaining users, organizing events, becoming the admin of many groups, negotiating projects and organizing freelance teams — after doing all of these things, I should have confidence in my leadership ability. Because I'm not afraid of failure, not afraid that people won't come to my events, not afraid that streamers won't broadcast on my platform, not afraid of people criticizing me. I want my skin to be as thick as a multilevel marketer's — completely indifferent to what others think, only caring whether the work is getting done.

Why do people like Ma Yun, Ma Huateng, and Cheng Wei compete in every domain? Because the more successful and self-directed a person is, the less they want to cede even an inch of territory to a competitor. Only by being stronger than your rivals can you claim the right to say you're contributing to the world, to give back to the world.
