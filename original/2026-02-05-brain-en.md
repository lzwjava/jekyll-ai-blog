Our brain is magic. My IELTS listening score is like 6. After 3 years in a global company in an English environment, my listening score is still 6. That's because I always open Zoom captions in meetings.

I worry I am unable to hear clearly, especially since half the team are Indians.

When I open Zoom captions, after some weeks, though sometimes I still can't hear clearly and have to tell colleagues to repeat, it's just in this case that I improve my listening.

When I open Zoom captions, we think we have captions, and we have AI companion in Zoom, so I just focus on listening when it's highly related to me. Even at that time, I mostly read captions.

I mean our brain is magic—if you give it some time, after some months, its listening will improve a lot.

This is a perfect case study.

With GPS constantly available, our brain never bothers to build a mental map. Why would it? But when you’re forced to navigate, that spatial awareness develops.

Like word vocabulary—if we skip new words in daily life, I'll surely keep my vocabulary at around 8,000.

I remember in high school, when my vocabulary was at 3,000, how I used a stupid way to search a printed dictionary to know the meanings of words in Paul Graham essays I'd printed. That's how I gained more vocabulary. And in the latest 10 years, I've searched thousands of times to know meanings.

But still, I skip a lot of new words in my reading.

**On the Real Gap Between 70% and 90% Scores: It's Not About Genius**

After passing 13 courses in my Computer Application associate degree program (with around 10 years of software engineering background), I've been reflecting on what actually separates high achievers from average performers.

**The Observable Pattern**

Whether it's:
- Exam scores: 70% vs 90%
- Chinese university entrance exam (gaokao): 400-500 vs 600-650
- Codeforces ratings: 1400 vs 2100

The gap isn't mysterious. It's not about innate genius.

**What High Achievers Actually Do Differently**

People who consistently score 90%+ or achieve 650 on the gaokao share these concrete behaviors:

1. **Tolerance for confusion** - They sit with difficult material longer, working through it until they truly understand
2. **Self-correction** - They systematically identify their own mistakes and weak points
3. **Targeted practice** - They focus deliberately on their weakest areas, not just what they're comfortable with
4. **Volume** - They simply do more exercises, more consistently
5. **Deep familiarity** - They thoroughly internalize knowledge points rather than superficially covering them

**No Magic, Just Principles**

This applies everywhere - academics, entrepreneurship, competitive programming. High achievement comes from:
- Being detail-oriented
- Following proven principles consistently
- Doing the unglamorous work of repetition and practice
- Not moving forward until fundamentals are solid

With my software engineering background, I recognize this pattern. The developer who becomes senior isn't just "talented" - they debugged more code, read more documentation, built more projects, learned from more failures.

**The takeaway**: The gap between good and great is built from hundreds of small, deliberate actions repeated consistently. It's demystified once you see it clearly.
